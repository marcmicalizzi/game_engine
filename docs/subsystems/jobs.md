# jobs (core)

**Purpose.** The topology-aware job system (docs/plan/11-performance-principles.md §11.5, ADR-0011). Pinned worker threads grouped into pools by efficiency class, per-worker work-stealing deques, cache-domain-local stealing, and counters for completion.

**Owned data.** Worker threads, their deques, the per-pool inboxes, and execution statistics.

**Model.**
- `Pool::Performance`: one worker per performance-class logical CPU minus one reserved for the main thread. Simulation and rendering work.
- `Pool::Efficiency`: one worker per efficiency-class logical CPU. Streaming, decompression, audio mixing, background inference. On machines without efficiency cores a small pool (a quarter of the performance CPUs, at least one) runs on performance CPUs at below-normal OS priority, so callers never special-case the topology.
- A `Job` is `{fn, data, counter}`, 24 bytes, trivially copyable; no allocation per job. `Counter` is one cache line.
- A worker pops LIFO from its own deque, then its pool's inbox, then steals FIFO from workers in its cache domain (random start), then from the rest of its pool. Pools never steal from each other.
- Submitting from a worker of the target pool pushes to that worker's deque; from anywhere else, to the pool's inbox. When both are full the submitter runs the job inline (backpressure).
- Idle workers spin briefly, then sleep on an eventcount (`epoch` + `sleepers`); submitters notify only when a sleeper exists.
- The first outside thread to submit to a pool (normally the main thread) is given that pool's **guest deque**, a worker's deque with no worker: what it submits goes there instead of the inbox, it pops its own newest work from it while it waits, and workers steal from it after their own victims. Every other outside thread, and a worker submitting to the other pool, uses the inbox.
- `wait(Counter)` from a worker executes other jobs of its pool; from an outside thread it helps the Performance pool for a short spin and then blocks. A Performance job may therefore run on the main thread with `current_worker() == nullptr`; Efficiency jobs run only on Efficiency workers. Helping is one level deep (below).

**Invariants.**
- Every scheduled job executes exactly once and signals its counter once.
- `jobs_executed == steals + inbox pops + own pops + helper runs`; tests check totals.
- A thread's stack holds at most one **lent** job — one a wait ran that was not of the group it waited for — on top of its own work; the only way past it is `help_escapes`, a parked job that found every queue full (see "Helping is one level deep").
- Workers of a pool are pinned to distinct CPUs of that pool's class when enough exist.

**Public API.** `core/jobs/job_system.h`: `Job`, `JobFn`, `Pool`, `Counter`, `JobSystemConfig`, `WorkerInfo`, `JobSystemStats`, `JobSystem` (`schedule`, `wait`, `parallel_for`, `worker_count`, `worker_info`, `current_worker`, `current`, `stats`). `core/jobs/detail/` (Chase-Lev `WorkDeque`, Vyukov `MpmcQueue`) is not public.

**Depends on.** `base`, `memory`, `containers`, `platform`.

**Testing.** `tools/dev.ps1 test -Filter jobs`. Queue tests hammer the deque with four thieves and the MPMC queue with four producers and four consumers, checking every item moves exactly once, and a ring of eight slots under three thieves checks that a slot rewritten thousands of times hands each two-word item over once and whole (the read-before-CAS race, which a ring that never wraps cannot reach). System tests cover start/stop, exact-once execution across both pools, `parallel_for` coverage and reduction, nested scheduling with waiting inside jobs, cross-pool scheduling, pinning to the right CPU class, observable stealing under a flood, that idle workers sleep, and that `stats()` read from another thread while the workers run only grows and ends exact. `help_depth_tests.cpp` saturates the pool with 64 "texture" jobs scheduled from the main thread, each a `parallel_for` of rows whose every row is a `parallel_for` of its own, at 8 workers (the content build's `--jobs 8`) and at 1, and checks with a stack probe that no thread ever has more than two of them on its stack (its own and one lent), that no parked job escaped, and that every cell ran; without the bound the test overflows its stack (306 jobs deep, `SIGSEGV - Stack overflow`). `engine_jobs_bench` (`tools/dev.ps1 bench -Filter "jobs*"`) times a small `parallel_for`'s submit and join from the main thread, the saturated nested case, and the deque's own paths on one thread (`jobs.deque.push_pop`, `jobs.deque.push_steal`: 256 jobs a round). Under ThreadSanitizer: `tools/linux-build.ps1 -Preset linux-clang-tsan -Test -Filter 'jobs'` ([what to run](../ci/what-to-run.md#sanitizers)).

## Helping is one level deep

**What it fixed (F26, 2026-10-29).** A cold `engine-content build-all` of the forest v22 manifest (260 entries, 1,787 textures, `--jobs 8`) died with `0xc00000fd`, a stack overflow, in the main thread. The texture step schedules every texture build and waits; the wait helped by running a queued job, a texture build; its mip chain's `parallel_for` waited and helped by running another texture build; and so on, one texture build — with stb's PNG decoder, which keeps large buffers on the stack — per level of helping, as deep as the pool had textures queued. The reproduction counted 105 jobs on one stack at the crash, 110 s in. Workers were exposed the same way (their stacks are the same 1 MB), and a warm derived-data cache hid it because nothing was built. A bigger stack would only have moved the depth at which it fails; the depth was set by the length of the queue, which no stack bounds.

**The rule.** A job a wait runs that is not of the group it is waiting for (the counter's jobs) is **lent** to that thread's stack. A wait may lend its thread to any job of its pool only while no lent job is on its stack; a job of the waited group is the waiter's own work, as a call would be, and does not count. A wait nested inside a lent job runs only its own group's jobs: it looks at the newest end of its own deque (where a `parallel_for`'s chunks are), at the inbox (cycled 64 entries a look) and, when the inbox is empty, at the oldest end of the other deques, and **parks** every other job it takes back in a queue — the inbox, or its own deque when the inbox is full. `Counter`'s `queued_`, the group's jobs that are queued rather than running, says whether there is anything to look for: a bounded waiter whose jobs are all running looks nowhere and backs off (a pause doubling to 1,024), and an outside thread blocks once nothing of its group is queued or after 256 fruitless looks (a group in the other pool). So a stack holds the thread's own work plus at most one lent job and the nesting of its own `parallel_for`s; the content build's deepest stack is now 3 jobs.

*Why it cannot deadlock.* A parked job goes back to a queue that every thread of the pool reads, so refusing it strands nothing; and a bounded waiter can reach every queued job of its own group (its own deque by popping and parking what lies above, the inbox by cycling, the others by stealing), so it never waits for a job that nobody may run. The one way past the bound is a park that finds every queue full: the job runs in place, lent, the way `schedule` runs a job on its submitter when every queue is full, and `help_escapes` counts it.

*Why a wait at the top still lends.* The stricter rule — a wait never runs anything but its own group — would leave a worker whose chunks are all running elsewhere idle until they finish. One lent job costs one job's stack, which is bounded. The fully group-scoped `parallel_for`, whose waiter claims its own chunks from an atomic index and never runs a queued job at all, was not built: the queued jobs that find nothing left to claim still point at the call's chunk descriptors after it returns, so the descriptors would have to live in a slab the system owns and recycle when the last stale job has run.

**The guest deque, and why the bound needed it.** With the bound alone the cook finished, but the main thread's own chunks were at the tail of an inbox holding ~1,800 queued texture builds: a bounded wait cycled the whole inbox to reach them (12 million parks over the cook), and the texture the main thread was building as its own work waited 114 s for its mips while its rows sat behind every texture queued before them. The main thread is a worker in all but name, so it gets a worker's deque: its rows go to the end it pops, workers steal them from the other end, and the parks went to zero.

**Measured** on the forest v22 manifest, `msvc-release`, `--jobs 8` (seven performance workers and the main thread), a fresh derived-data cache each time, on the shared machine (the merge gate's tests running beside it; the CPU was 24 % busy before the first run, 4–9 % before the others and 53 % at the end of the last, so the times are upper bounds):

| helping rule | exit | wall time | `helper_runs` | `foreign_helps` | `parked` | most jobs on one stack |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| unbounded (9f4caed4) | `0xc00000fd` at 110 s | — | — | — | — | 105 at the crash |
| one level deep, outside threads on the inbox | 0 | 219.9 s | 181,876 | 278 | 12,011,516 | 3 |
| one level deep, guest deque (this) | 0 | 214.1 s | 183,854 | 3,536 | 0 | 3 |

With the guest deque the slowest texture's mips took 545 ms (114 s without it) and the texture builds' summed times fell from 1,062,830 ms to 791,631 ms. `engine_jobs_bench`, same build, before and after (the "before" carries the new bookkeeping — `queued_` and the nesting count — under the old rule; the harness recorded 11–12 % of the CPU used by others, upper bounds): `jobs.nested.saturated/1` 17.92 → 11.96 ms, `/8` 2.835 → 2.846 ms; `jobs.parallel_for.small/1` 14.21 → 11.44 µs, `/8` 14.11 → 13.10 µs.

`JobSystemStats` says how it went: `foreign_helps` (lent jobs), `parked`, `help_escapes`, and `max_job_nesting`, the most jobs one thread has had on its stack.

## What a job may assume

**About the memory it was handed.** Everything the scheduling thread wrote before `schedule` happens before the job runs, on whichever thread runs it: a worker's or the guest's deque publishes a push with a release store of its bottom index that every thief's acquire load reads, the inbox publishes with its cells' sequence numbers (release on push, acquire on pop), and a job run inline runs on the thread that wrote. A job's `data` is never read or written by the job system, only handed on, so it must outlive the run: `parallel_for`'s chunks live on the waiter's stack until its wait returns.

**About a slot another worker steals.** A thief copies the slot before its compare-exchange on the top index decides whether the job is its own (Chase–Lev's read before the CAS: once the CAS succeeds the owner may write the slot again). A copy whose CAS loses is thrown away and never run, so a job runs exactly once whichever thief, or the owner's pop of the last item, wins; a copy that might be torn by the owner's rewrite is read as relaxed atomic words, so it is not a data race, and it is kept only when its CAS wins, by which time nothing can have rewritten that slot.

**About a counter another worker writes.** `Counter::signal` is an acquire-release decrement and `done()`, `remaining()` and `wait` read with acquire, so everything a job of the group wrote before it returned happens before a wait on the counter returns, or before `done()` reads true. Nothing orders two jobs of one group against each other: a job that reads what another job of its group writes (a sum several workers add to, a table hash summed from systems at the end of a tick) shares it through atomics of its own, or keeps one slot per worker and sums them after the wait. `remaining()` above zero says nothing about which jobs have run; `queued_` is a hint the helping rule reads, not a promise.

**A counter outlives its last signal** (T193, 2026-11-18). A `Counter` usually lives on the waiter's stack, and a waiter spinning on `done()` may return and destroy it the instant it reads zero. Until T193 the last `signal()` decremented to zero and *then* called `notify_all()` on the same atomic, so a spinning waiter could free the counter between the two: a call on an object whose lifetime has ended, undefined behaviour in C++ although neither MSVC's `WakeByAddressAll` nor libstdc++'s futex reads the memory. This is the case Lewis Baker's P2616R4, *Making std::atomic notification/wait operations usable in more situations* (WG21, 2023-02-15; read through the isocpp.org copy, open-std.org refused the connection), opens with — "The final call to `x.notify_one()` member function on a destroyed object … has undefined behaviour" — and its fix, a `notify_token` taken before the store, is not in C++20 or in any library this tree builds with (a 2026 std-proposals thread says it did not reach C++26; not checked against the working draft). The mend is the paper's workaround for code written today: the waiter blocks on one word and does not return until a mark the notifier takes off **last** is gone. Here both are one word, so it costs no second atomic: the low 28 bits count outstanding jobs, and the last `signal()` replaces its decrement to zero with a compare-exchange that writes zero jobs **plus a "notify in flight" unit** in one store, calls `notify_all()`, and then subtracts the unit, its last touch of the counter. `done()` is the whole word at zero, so no waiter returns while the notify runs; `remaining()` masks the unit off; `wait_blocking()` blocks while jobs are outstanding and spins through the few instructions between the notify and the unit coming off, which no notify follows (the paper names that cost: a waiter spins if the notifier is descheduled there). A counter added to again as it reaches zero (the audio stores' and physics adapter's reuse) keeps its new count, since the add and the unit's removal are both read-modify-writes of the one word; the unit is a count, not a bit, so two final signals in flight do not collide. Every signal is now a compare-exchange loop where it was a `fetch_sub` — one locked instruction either way when it does not retry. A counter counts fewer than 2^28 jobs (asserted in `add` and the constructor). The case `Counter: done only after the last signal has finished with it` runs 4,000 stack counters each waited by spinning and by blocking, and the reuse; the ThreadSanitizer claim is the stack's `linux-clang-tsan` container's to make.

**About `stats()`.** Each worker counts in its own atomics, written only by its own thread with a relaxed load and store (the same instructions as a plain increment on x86; no locked add on the job path) and read by `stats()` once each, relaxed; the counters outside threads touch (`helper_runs`, `inline_runs`, the helping rule's) are shared relaxed atomics no worker's pop or push touches. So a `JobSystemStats` is **not a snapshot taken at one instant**: its fields, and the workers' parts of one field, are read at different moments while the workers run. What holds: every count only grows between two calls; a job is counted before it signals its counter, so after `wait(c)` returns the totals include every job of `c`; and with no job running, the totals are exact.

**What the orders are, and where they come from** (T186, 2026-11-18). The deque is Chase and Lev's (SPAA 2005) with the C11 memory orders of Lê, Pop, Cohen and Zappa Nardelli (PPoPP 2013), which proved them on ARM and POWER. Neither paper's text could be read through this run's fetch tool (both PDFs came back compressed): **both are marked not read**, and the orders below were checked against what could be read — the patent of the same algorithm (US 7,346,753, Chase and Lev: the element is read before the CAS because the location may be refilled by a concurrent push once it completes), and two implementations that cite the 2013 paper and give its orders line by line, crossbeam-deque's `deque.rs` and a Nim translation of the paper (its `push`, `pop`/`take` and `steal`). A 2022 note on the paper (wingolog.org) reports an underflow in its `take` from an unsigned bottom; the indices here are `i64`, so an empty deque's pop reads −1 and finds it empty.

| operation | before T186 | now | the paper's order | why, on x86 and on a weaker machine |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `push`: load of top | acquire | acquire | acquire | orders a thief's read of a slot (before its winning CAS) before the owner's rewrite of it a lap later; on x86 every load is an acquire, on ARM an `ldar` |
| `push`: write of the slot | plain copy | relaxed atomic words (the compilers' intrinsics) | relaxed (an atomic buffer) | a thief's speculative read may meet it: plain accesses are a data race, undefined behaviour in C++ whatever is done with the value; relaxed costs nothing on either |
| `push`: publication | release fence, relaxed store of bottom | release store of bottom | release fence, relaxed store | the same order for the one store a thief reads; on x86 both are a plain `mov`, on ARM an `stlr` for a `dmb` and a `str`; TSan models a release store and ignores fences, so it saw no stolen job as published (T179's 138 reports) |
| `pop`: store of bottom (decrement, and the restore after an empty or lost pop) | relaxed | release | relaxed | C++20 (P0982) dropped a thread's later stores from a release sequence: a thief whose acquire reads one of these values synchronizes with nothing under C++20, where the paper's C11 proof had them in the push's release sequence. A `mov` on x86; an `stlr` on ARM |
| `pop`: fence between the store of bottom and the load of top | seq_cst | seq_cst | seq_cst | the owner's store and a thief's load of the same two indices must not pass each other, or both take the last item; a store-load order, an `mfence` or locked instruction even on x86 |
| `pop`: read of the slot | plain copy | relaxed atomic words | relaxed | thieves read the same words; atomic for consistency with them |
| `pop`, `steal`: CAS on top | seq_cst, relaxed on failure | unchanged | seq_cst, relaxed on failure | decides the last item between the owner and thieves, and among thieves |
| `steal`: load of top, fence, load of bottom | acquire, seq_cst fence, acquire | unchanged | acquire, seq_cst fence, acquire | the acquire on bottom is what receives a push's publication; the fence pairs with `pop`'s |
| `steal`: read of the slot, before the CAS | plain copy | relaxed atomic words | relaxed | the read-before-CAS race: a thief that loses may read a slot the owner is rewriting |
| worker counters (`jobs_executed`, `steals_*`, `inbox_pops`, `sleeps`) | plain `u64`, read by `stats()` on any thread | atomics, relaxed, one writer | (not the paper's) | a plain read beside a write is a data race; on x86 relaxed is the same `mov`, and a single writer needs no locked add |

**What it cost, measured** (`engine_jobs_bench`, `msvc-release`, 15 repeats; "before" built from the bench commit with the base's deque, "after" from the change, both from one worktree, run before–after–before–after; the machine was never quiet — `--wait-quiet` gave up after 20 minutes with 95–100 % of the CPU used by other agents' builds and tests and the GPU lock held by their tests — so the spread, not the medians, is what the pairs show). The slot words were first spelled `std::atomic_ref<u64>`: `msvc-release` is RelWithDebInfo, which MSVC builds with `/Ob1`, inlining only what is marked `inline`, so every word of a slot became a call into the library (`_Atomic_address_as`, through an incremental-link thunk) and `jobs.deque.push_pop` went from 4.6 and 5.5 µs a round of 256 to 8.6 and 8.1 µs, `push_steal` from 6.4 and 7.3 to 10.8 and 9.0 µs. Spelled with the intrinsics `atomic_ref` is built on (`__iso_volatile_load64`/`store64` on MSVC, `__atomic_load_n`/`store_n` relaxed elsewhere, which ThreadSanitizer instruments as atomics) and unrolled over the three words, the last pair, taken with this row's own TSan container compiling beside it: `push_pop` 9.27 and 9.29 µs before, 10.18 and 9.91 after (each run's spread 4–7 %), so about 3 ns a push and pop, the three word stores that may no longer merge into one 16-byte store; `push_steal` 12.31 and 12.34 before, 12.72 and 12.46 after, inside the spread. The system's own paths showed nothing past their spread in the first pairs: `jobs.parallel_for.small/8` 16.1 and 15.4 µs before, 13.5 and 14.4 after; `jobs.nested.saturated/8` 4.9 and 6.2 ms before, 3.8 and 4.1 after (spreads 10–27 %). Under ThreadSanitizer (`tools/linux-build.ps1 -Preset linux-clang-tsan -Test -Filter 'jobs|scene_collision|scene_gen|store|events'`, 2026-11-18): T179's run had 138 reports in `jobs`, 5 in `scene_collision` and more in `audio`, every one the job system's; after the change, 11 of 11 tests passed with **no report** (`scene_collision` 840 s under TSan), on the `atomic_ref` spelling, and `jobs|events|store|scene_gen` again on the intrinsics, which are what libstdc++'s `atomic_ref` compiles to. The workers' counters cost a relaxed load and store where a plain increment was, the same instructions in an inlining build and a library call each in `/Ob1`, once per job, under the noise of those two.

**What stays resident.** Per worker: its `Worker` (a few hundred bytes: its fields, a victim list with room for 64 inline, and its counters on a line of their own) and its deque, whose ring is `queue_capacity` × 24 bytes (96 KiB at the default 4,096) committed at start and **resident only as far as the deque has ever been filled** (the ring is not written at construction; the change to atomic words kept it so). Per pool: the inbox, `queue_capacity` × 32 bytes (128 KiB), resident from the start because its constructor writes every cell's sequence number, and the guest deque, another 96 KiB committed. On this machine's 36 logical CPUs (35 performance workers and 9 efficiency workers by default) that is 44 deques and two guests, 4.3 MiB committed, and 256 KiB of inboxes resident; the threads' stacks (1 MiB reserved each on Windows, committed as used) are the platform's.

## Long-running jobs and tuning

**A job holds its worker until it returns**, which matters because the pool is sized for short ones: there is no preemption and a blocked job is a worker the pool cannot use. One consumer does that deliberately — `domain/ecs` hosts each flecs worker as a job that runs for the world's lifetime ([ADR-0030](../adr/0030-flecs-workers-are-long-running-pool-jobs.md)) — and it is allowed only because it bounds itself: the adapter keeps a budget of how many performance workers may be held at once and refuses a world that would exceed it. Anything else long-running belongs on `Pool::Efficiency`. The counterexample is on record: hosting those same workers as *per-tick* jobs, so the pool re-dispatched a long-running worker every tick, cost 1.6–2.2 ms a tick on a 100,000-entity world while the pool's own submit-and-join path stayed at 1.2–5.7 µs ([E6](../experiments/e6-ecs-store.md#why-per-tick-hosting-cost-what-it-did-2026-09-18)). Nothing here changed to fix it, and that is the point: the spin-then-sleep policy below is right for short jobs, and it was the usage that was wrong.

**Performance notes.** Deque and inbox capacities are configurable powers of two (default 4096). Spin-before-sleep iterations are configurable (default 2000). Candidates for the tunables harness: spin length, steal order policy, whether SMT siblings each get a worker, and whether Efficiency workers may help the Performance pool when it is saturated (currently never, to keep slow cores off the critical path).
