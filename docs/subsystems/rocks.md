# rocks (domain, capability)

**Purpose.** The rock solver (roadmap G62): a capability that turns a shape's parameters and a seed
into a closed rock mesh — a **boulder**, an **outcrop** of boulders, **scree**, or a **cliff** face
along the ground ([Cliffs](#cliffs), roadmap G71) — so the engine's rock fields, rocky shores,
outcrops, escarpments and talus are solved rather than modelled or generated by an
image-to-3D service. Desert Survival's dunes want rock fields and the oasis a rocky shore; the
fantasy RPG wants outcrops, boulders and scree; and the Tripo-generated props failed E10 mostly on
topology ([e10](../experiments/e10-generated-props.md)), which a solver never has. A `.rock.json`
recipe is a mesh like a glTF: a scene's mesh table, a scatter layer's kind or `engine-view --mesh`
names one, and it is built through the derived-data cache by the mesh-generator registry
([assets](assets.md#generated-meshes)). It does not place boulders (a scatter layer or a scene's
instances do) — but it does place cliffs, through its scene generator `cliffs`, which finds a
terrain's steep ground and stands faces along it ([The cliffs generator](#the-cliffs-generator)).
It supports tiled PBR sets and static geological weathering. It does not simulate erosion over
time or give rocks an interior to break.

**Why this shape.** It is the flora capability's shape ([flora](flora.md)) because the problem is
the same one: a deterministic solver at content-build time, a parameter file whose canonical bytes
are the cache key, one function that builds the whole container so engine-content and the renderer
write the same bytes, and a registration into the mesh-generator registry so nothing below a host
names the capability. **Every body is star-shaped about its centre**, a cube-sphere grid of
directions each pushed out to the body's radius in that direction, because that one choice makes
the topology free: a radial map of a sphere is closed, two-manifold and cannot fold, whatever the
radius function does, so the cuts, the weathering and the bedding are all just a function of
direction. The alternatives were a convex hull of points (closed, but no rounding and no surface
detail, and its triangles are as uneven as its points), CSG of planes against a mesh (the exact
facets, at the price of a remesh and the slivers E10 failed), and marching cubes over a signed
distance (any topology, but a mesh whose triangle count is the volume's resolution rather than a
budget, and a cube-sphere's budget ladder is exactly `12 n²`). The cost of the radial form is that
a body cannot overhang its own centre — no arches, no undercut ledges deeper than the bedding
grooves — which is why an outcrop is several bodies rather than one, and why a cliff is not a body
at all but a box bent along a line ([Cliffs](#cliffs)).

## The model by type

**Lithology, G94 (2026-10-28).** `RockType` selects a mixture of processes rather than a single
noise style. `rock_family(type)` derives the origin; a recipe cannot contradict its type with an
independent family. [Rock lithology](../experiments/rock-lithology.md) records the grounding and
the limits of this representation. These presets are authored examples, not calibrated geology:
limestone and sandstone can both be resistant, and weathering is exposure in 0–1, not years.

| Family | Types | Dominant representation |
|---|---|---|
| Igneous | `Granite`, `Basalt`, `Obsidian` | joint blocks and granular/exfoliation loss; columns; smooth concave fracture scars with sharp lips |
| Sedimentary | `Sandstone`, `Limestone`, `Dolostone`, `Shale` | unequal bed retreat and granular loss; dissolution pockets; thin fissile plates |
| Metamorphic | `Slate`, `Schist`, `Gneiss`, `Marble`, `Quartzite` | planar cleavage; scaly folded sheets; folded massive bands; massive carbonate solution |

`geology` is a `RockGeology` on every shape. `weathering` (0–1, default 0.5) increases old joint
edge rounding and process exposure; newly cut facets retain only 2–18% of that rounding, so worn
edges and fresh angular breaks coexist. `irregularity` (0–1, 0.65) controls broad asymmetric removal
chips and unequal retreat. G95 replaces generic bowl-shaped damage with sixteen seeded, bounded
four-plane chips. Their central faces and side walls form angular recesses; the chip's width fades
over two to four source cells. Six broad curved scars remain weighted by the type's conchoidal
fracture tendency (strong in obsidian, weak in basalt, absent otherwise). These are surface-removal
approximations, not fracture mechanics or detached pieces.

`bed_m` (0 off, otherwise 0.01–20 m; absent the type's), `dip_deg` (−85–85, 0),
`azimuth_deg` (−180–180, 0) and `fold` (0–1, absent the type's) define the geological fabric.
Unequal seeded sheet retreat forms broad surfaces separated by short risers. Solver v8 also
varies each station by up to 0.28 authored spacings times `irregularity` (0.2 per-bed offset,
0.08 smooth lateral wander). Ordered stations yield local thicknesses of 0.44–1.56 spacings
at maximum irregularity. The continuous coordinate mapping is shared by geometry and baked
PBR; zero irregularity keeps evenly spaced beds. The transition is at least two source cells
wide in the local bed; bedding fades between two and four cells across the minimum possible
thickness, including the baker's texel-footprint filter. `flaking`,
`dissolution` and `granular` (each 0–1, absent the type's, explicit zero off) mix sheet removal,
asymmetric solution pockets and three octaves of granular loss. Pocket and grain detail also fade
when unresolved. Small grains belong in normal/roughness maps.

An outcrop shares the fabric seed and resting-frame field. Scree retains each fragment's fabric
before yaw. Cliffs evaluate it in (distance along face, height minus datum, 0); their macro beds
follow the folded coordinate too. This is face-local, not a continuous world-space folded stratum
across separately traced cliff objects. `Cliff.bed_m` overrides the macro ledge spacing;
`geology.bed_m` supplies it when the former is absent, and independently controls the finer fabric.

### Geology on the world formation (W85)

A cliff can take its bedding from the scene's formation (`engine.scene.Formation`, scene.schema;
[renderer](renderer.md#formation-bands) draws the same field on the ground;
[grounding](../experiments/formation-field.md)). The recipe carries the field itself,
`Cliff.formation` (version 5), and where its origin stands in the world, `Cliff.world_origin`, so
the build stays a function of the recipe: a face cut from a world with a field is keyed by the
field. With both, `detail::make_fabric` takes the field's normal and dip direction (as integers
over 2^24, the GPU's definition; through `gfx::pack_formation_frame`, T94, which packs the frame
and no table, where a whole 98,624-byte block stood on the stack of every rock built),
switches the type's own fold off, and `sample_bed` counts the beds
by `gfx::formation_coordinate` (`domain/gfx/formation.h`) — the f64 twin the terrain's shader is
held to — at `world_origin` plus the point **on the face's mean plane in the recipe's frame**, not
the face-local (along, height − datum) it reads without one. So the bed coordinate at a point of
the foot line is the one the terrain's band at that point is drawn from, and a dipping or folded
bed runs from the ground into the face and out of its far end. The stations, the unequal retreat
and the ledges (`Cliff.bed_m`, the type's spacing) still space the face's own beds along that
coordinate: the orientation, the fold and the phase are the field's, the ledge spacing is not yet
the field's sequence (the exposed-bedrock solver's, W85's "Next").

**The override rule.** `RockGeology.from_formation` (version 2) defaults to **true**: a face in a
world with a field joins it unless its recipe opts out, because the point of a field is that every
face agrees and a face that silently kept its own dip would draw beds crossing the ground's. With
`false` the recipe's own `dip_deg`, `azimuth_deg` and `fold` stand. The canonical parameters leave
`from_formation` out while it is true and `formation` and `world_origin` out while the field is
absent, so no recipe written before keeps anything but the key it had (`canonical_rock_json`). A
field out of the scene's ranges is refused with the scene's sentence, prefixed
`engine.rocks.Cliff.`. Boulders, outcrops and scree recipes do not read the field yet (their recipes
have no world origin); the `deposits` generator's talus and shed blocks do, through
`DepositLayer.formation` ([What a face sheds](#what-a-face-sheds), G143). The `cliffs` generator
copies its layer's `formation` into every face it writes, with the face's own world origin
([Faces in the slope](#faces-in-the-slope), G142); a hand-written recipe fills both fields itself.

**What a solver asks the formation** (W94, 2026-11-10; `domain/gfx/formation.h`; the grounding:
[formation sequence](../experiments/formation-sequence.md)). The formation is now a section drawn
from a seed and its **members** — up to 512 beds, each in a member, a member a cliff-former or a
slope-former by its `resistant` share — whose tops move along strike and may be thrown by a fault,
so a solver that wants the bed the ground draws at a point asks with the point:

- `gfx::formation_sample(params, frame, x, y, z)` gives the coordinate (the fault's throw in it),
  the two lateral fields there, the **bed** and the **member**. Each is a `FormationBedSample`:
  `index`, `fraction`, `resistance`, `kind` (resistant, recessive, a lens), `member`, and `base`
  and `top`, where the bed's two contacts stand **at that point** along the coordinate, so
  `top - base` is the bed's thickness there (zero where a lens has pinched out) and a ledge can sit
  on the contact the terrain's band draws.
- `gfx::formation_member(params, coordinate, lateral)` is the member alone: its bounds are where a
  cliff band starts and stops, and its resistance at or over `k_formation_cliff_resistance` (a
  half, the line `exposure` and the `cliffs` generator already draw) says it forms a cliff. A
  formation with no macro sequence answers as one member, the whole period.
- `gfx::formation_lateral(frame, x, y, z)` and `formation_bed(sequence, coordinate, lateral)` are
  the two halves, for a solver that walks many coordinates at one place.
- `gfx::formation_section_m(params)` is the thickness of section inside which nothing repeats.

`formation_coordinate` applies the fault for every caller. **The queries W85 gave keep their
meaning**: `formation_bed(sequence, coordinate)` without a point is the **reference section**, every
top where the table's `start_m` puts it, which is what the ledges, the exposure rule, the deposits
and the landform read tonight; where a top moves they are off by its travel (under 0.45 of the
thinner bed, a lens's whole thickness), and they should move to the forms above and the plain one
go. Recipes, ledge patches and deposit patches that carry a formation keep their keys while it is an
authored one: the canonical parameters leave the seed, the members and the lateral length out of a
formation with no members, a fault with no throw, and a bed's `variation` and `lens` at their
defaults (the walk `detail::drop_absent_sampling` makes, the one all three canonical forms share).

`quality` (`Low`, `Medium`, `High`, default `High`) spends respectively 10%, 35%, or 100% of the
authored triangle budget, with a floor of 192 triangles per body or 2,000 per cliff and never above
the authored budget. These are **offline source variants**. The existing cluster DAG and renderer
LOD error remain the runtime cost controls; this does not add a live graphics-setting switch.
No runtime pass or per-instance state is added.

**Structural detail, G89 (2026-10-25).** The original cuts, warp and bedding below now carry
`RockSurface` on boulders, outcrops and scree. Its joint recesses follow the body's joint
directions, with irregular plane spacing and variable penetration along the plane. An outcrop
uses one seeded field in its intended resting frame across all bodies; scree samples each
fragment's joint frame and layout offset before applying its yaw. This is a surface representation of weathered fractures,
not a volume-fracture or rockfall simulation. The final burial adjustment can shift a body's
pattern vertically by the change in its extrema.

Five octaves of physical-scale surface relief add smaller features. Granite, sandstone and
basalt have signed relief; limestone has pits. Each octave fades independently from absent at
two cells across its wavelength to full at four. A joint fades by its **opening width**, not
its spacing, so a wide spacing cannot enable an unresolved narrow trench. Recession is clamped
to 35% of the local radius and outward relief to 5% to protect extreme authoring inputs. The
shell remains radial. Solver version 7 filters each corner's area-weighted normal to incident
faces within about 37° on fresh/unweathered surfaces, widening to 60° on fully weathered old
surfaces. Incompatible corners receive separate attributes at identical positions and UVs;
smooth regions retain shared normals. Triangle counts stay unchanged, but vertices and cluster
storage can increase. This replaces the earlier whole-triangle flat-normal fallback.

**Fracture rims and sampling, G98.** Each radial chart begins with equal-angle cube sampling and
seeded offsets of at most 0.1 cell; chart boundary coordinates stay fixed and seam copies share
one welded sample. Nearby samples move towards hull-plane intersections, by at most 0.45 angular
cell, with fitting faded out across resolved rounded blends. A move is rejected if any incident
directional triangle falls below 20% of its original area. Each cell then chooses the safe diagonal
whose midpoint better follows the radial generating surface; if neither meets the extra 5%
cell-area margin, it keeps the already-validated original diagonal. The surface baker uses that
same connectivity, including for fresh-fracture masks. These are offline bounded engineering
choices, not geological constants; [grounding and studies](../experiments/rock-lithology.md#g98-fracture-rims-and-sampling).
Temporary adjacency, direction and normal arrays scale with the body's surface, not its volume.
Solver v8 extends fitting to fully resolved finite chips: their outer minimum with the shell
and their internal maximum of four planes. A projected boundary must lie on the visible
envelope; hidden cut planes are rejected. A chip-boundary move must also improve the incident area-weighted
normal agreement with that envelope before weathering/warp, avoiding harmful positional fits.
Cliff macro coordinates filter station variation by the minimum bed width to prevent sub-cell
offsets from perturbing block faces. Fading chips, curved conchoidal transition bands and
later bedding/displacement still use sampling and normal filtering. This remains a bounded
approximation, not complete feature-conforming remeshing or a replacement of radial shells.

The schema's physical controls are `strength` (0–1, default 1), `joint_spacing_m` (0.05–20,
default 0.65), `joint_width_m` (0.005 to half the spacing, default 0.12), `joint_depth_m`
(0–2, default 0.12), `wavelength_m` (0.02–10, default 0.4), and `relief_m` (0 to the smaller
of 0.5 and a quarter wavelength, default 0.018). These are authoring values, not inferred
measurements of the owner's photographs. [Rock structure](../experiments/rock-structure.md)
records the source evidence, engineering bounds and budget trades. `strength: 0` disables this
surface field; the independent geology field still applies. The engine's normal runtime cluster
LOD still controls draw cost; these source budgets are not a new runtime graphics-preset switch.

A body's radius along a unit direction `u` is the **smooth minimum** (the polynomial one, a band
`rounding × (0.04 + 0.3 weathering) × cut wear` wide) of the enclosing hull and every planar cut's radius: a cut is a plane of unit
normal `n` at distance `d` from the centre, `d / (u · n)` away along `u`. Before weathering that is
the convex intersection of half-spaces with its edges rounded. Six oblique support planes enclose
the body even without joint sets; no spherical patches remain between the cuts. The cuts are **joint
sets** — a pair of parallel planes on opposite sides per set, at seeded distances — and the type's
**facets**, cuts in seeded directions. A bounded chip uses the maximum of four plane distances to
enter its removed volume and the minimum with the hull to remove only that patch. Then the **warp** (one low octave of value noise scaling the
radius: the body's shape, at every rung) and the **grain** (up to three octaves of finer noise, each
present only where its wavelength spans four cells of the rung, so a coarse rung is the same rock
with the detail it cannot carry left out). The cut body is then **stretched** per axis so its box
is exactly the half-extents asked (an affine map, so a cut stays a plane), **banded** along its
bedding direction when it has layers, and given the structural surface above. Those last two
steps change the nominal extents. It is then **lowered** so the share `buried` of its final height
is under y = 0.

**Additional count-based bedding** (`layers`, absent off) is a groove at every bedding plane: `t = (p · bedding) / spacing + phase` plus a
little noise so the beds are wavy, and the radius drawn in by `depth · (2 frac(t) − 1)⁸` — sharp at
the plane, nothing mid-bed — with each bed's depth its own (35–100% of the type's), because beds
weather at their own rates. The groove fades out where a bed is narrower than four cells of the
rung, so it never aliases. Limestone's grain is pitted (`1 − amp |2v − 1|`, solution hollows)
rather than bumped.

### The boulder

`engine.rocks.Boulder`: one body of half-extents `size_x/y/z`, its joints from the type, cut at
0.5–0.75 in unit-body coordinates and its facets at 0.58–0.8, the bedding tilted up to 8° from vertical.
`RockReport::cuts` includes the six enclosing support planes per body, but not the bounded chips'
planes. The existing triangle-budget ladder is unchanged.

### The outcrop

`engine.rocks.Outcrop`: `count` boulders grown from one bedding plane (y = 0) inside the
half-extents, the largest in the middle and each later one smaller (down to 45%), placed round it at
45–95% of the footprint. **They share one joint set and one bedding direction**, drawn once from
the outcrop's seed — one rock mass, broken — with their own cut distances, and the bedding phase
carries on through them (a bed is at the same height in every boulder). Each is sunk `buried` of
its height, deeper than a boulder by default (0.3). The bodies may interpenetrate: each is closed,
and the renderer draws the union.

### Scree

`engine.rocks.Scree`: `count` freshly broken fragments lying on the ground inside the footprint:
the largest `fragment_m`, the rest 35–95% of it, flattened (height 50–85% of length times the
type's plate factor: 0.16 for slate through 0.8 for granite/basalt), each yawed at random, with
its own three joint sets and the type's facets plus two, cut at 0.42–0.75, sharper than the
type's boulders (40% of its rounding) with inherited geological fabric. Fragments are spread with a few tries apart
from those already placed.

## Cliffs

**The question it answers** (roadmap G71, 2026-10-19): does the solver reach landscape scale —
cliffs, the rocky parts of mountains? The radial bodies above do not: a body is star-shaped about
one centre, a boulder to 20 m and an outcrop to 50 m, and nothing can overhang its centre. A cliff
is another object: a **face** that follows the ground, hundreds of metres long and tens high, with
ledges, joint-bounded blocks, overhangs and talus at its foot. `engine.rocks.Cliff` is that face,
a fourth shape of the same rock file (`"shape": "Cliff"`), built through the same registry,
container, key, material block and pack roles as the other three.

**The model.** A cliff is a **line on the ground** (`points`: the foot of the face, metres from the
recipe's origin, each vertex's `y` the ground there, a `height` and a `batter` per vertex or the
face's own) and a **box of quads bent along it**: lattice (i, j, k) is station i along the line,
row j from `buried` under the foot to the top, and layer k from the back to the front. Walking along
`points` the face looks to the right (+z for a line along +x). The six faces of the box share their
border points by lattice key, as the cube-sphere's charts share theirs, so **the box is closed and
two-manifold whatever the carving does**; the lattice's axes are (along, up, out), a right-handed
frame, so a face wound outwards in the lattice is outwards in the world. The front leans back by
the batter and is **carved** by a function of (s, y) — the distance along the line and the height —
moving it only along the line's horizontal normal, so the sheet cannot fold over itself, and a
ledge or an undercut is one row of quads stretched outwards rather than a remesh. The normal is the
chord's a window of half the depth either side, so the frames turn smoothly round a vertex of the
line. What carves it:

- **Joint sets** (`joint_sets`, in order): the horizontal set — a granite's sheeting joints, a
  basalt's flow tops, a bedded rock's bedding planes — makes **ledges**; the vertical set across
  the face makes **columns** (a basalt's prisms, each with a ridge mid-column); the dipping set cuts
  diagonals. Each block of the three sets stands back its own seeded share of `relief`, and across
  a joint the two blocks blend over a band `rounding` sets, so the edges weather round.
- **Beds** (`bed_m`): a groove at every bedding plane, as the boulders', each bed with its own
  hardness; the beds are wavy along the face.
- **The undercut** (`undercut`): a weak bed is notched deepest just under the hard bed above it,
  which returns to the face within the bed's top eighth — the **overhang**'s underside.
- **The low warp** and **the gullies**: one octave over the face, and gullies between buttresses,
  cut back up to 6% of the height (at most 6 m) where a slow noise along the face says the joints let
  water in.
- **The grain**: up to three octaves from 3 m (limestone's pitted), and **the crest** rounded back.
- **The skyline**: the top broken back by up to 15% of the height, slowly along the face.

**The front is held up.** Each station's column of rows is carved and then walked from the foot up,
no row standing out over the one below by more than 0.4 of the height between them — rock does
not hang off a face unsupported, so a block whose footing has fallen back leans back over it — and
only then are the undercut's notches taken off, whose hard beds are what overhang. Without an
undercut nothing on the face faces down more steeply than that lean, and with one the hard beds'
undersides do: the test counts 2,716 triangles facing down by more than 60° above the foot of a
120 m sandstone face at `undercut` 1 and none at 0, and a vertical line through one meets the face
twice, into the bed from above and out of its underside.

**Closed against the ground.** The back stands `depth` behind the face's mean plane (which leans
back by the batter, so the back leans with it), **buried**: absent, the carving's deepest reach —
relief, the undercut's deepest notch, the grooves, the warp, the gullies, the grain, the columns and
the crest's rounding — plus 2 m, and never under 4 m (10.2 m for the default sandstone face 40 m
high, 18.9 m for a granite one 200 m high). The front is held half a metre in front of the back, so
the ends — a ladder between the back and the front at each row — are wound one way: a rung's two
triangles have twice-areas `(y₁ − y₀)(x₁ + D)` and `(y₁ − y₀)(x₀ + D)`, positive while the front is
in front. The top is a flat cap from the crest to the back's top, the bottom one at the buried foot.
**A cliff on flat ground is a wall** whose back is seen from behind; it is meant to stand against a
slope, as the generator stands it, with the back inside the hill.

**Patchy talus (solver v11).** A second closed box beneath the face represents deposited debris.
`talus` is its height fraction (default 0.08), `talus_angle_deg` its interior slope (default 35°).
`talus_patch_m` is the mean along-face deposition interval (2–100 m, default 14), and
`talus_coverage` the occupied share of an interval (0.1–1, default 0.6). Unequal station intervals
and smooth deposit envelopes vary the height and toe width. In gaps the entire surface sinks
below the reference ground; the shared closed substrate remains buried. Coverage 1 restores a
continuous apron. End tapers and two resolved noise octaves soften the deposits' boundaries.
These are authored deposition envelopes, not a transport or angle-of-repose simulation.

The fallen blocks (`talus_blocks`, `talus_block_m`) remain closed fragments from the radial solver,
sharpened with fresh type-default fabric. Their size varies with distance from the face. The
terrain patch corrects both their placement and the deposit; bare gaps take the full correction.
The internal apron is one connected mesh even when its visible deposits are disconnected.

**Terrain contact (solver v9).** Generated cliffs carry a `ground` patch: local x/z origin,
spacing, rectangular dimensions and row-major heights relative to the recipe origin. The
generator measures the debris footprint with a preliminary solve, then samples its `Ground`
at half the tracing spacing, capped at 1 m (coarsened only to fit the documented sample limit).
`fit_cliff_ground` exposes the same bake for standalone recipes. It leaves the recipe unchanged
on failure. Sampling is deterministic and the samples participate in the canonical recipe/cache
key, so changed supporting terrain invalidates the derived mesh.

With a patch, the skirt's underside follows terrain at least 1 m below it; the toe is also
buried relative to terrain, not relative to the cliff foot. The top progressively takes the
ground-height correction between its face attachment and toe, and fallen blocks use that same
correction (or the terrain directly with no skirt). Coverage and finite samples are checked;
an undersized patch is refused. No patch preserves the standalone reference-plane behavior.
This is a sampled terrain-contact correction, not sediment settling or exact terrain stitching;
see [G117 evidence and limits](../experiments/rock-lithology.md#g117-terrain-contact-under-cliff-talus).

**The budget ladder** is a cell edge, coarsest first: 32, 24, 16, 12, 8, 6, 4, 3, 2, 1.5, 1, 0.75,
0.5, 0.375, 0.25, 0.1875, 0.125, 0.09375 and 0.0625 m (`k_cliff_cells_m`). The blocks are priced first, each on the finest rung of
the cube-sphere ladder that 8% of the budget shared among them holds (and no finer than n = 20); a
budget too small for every block at n = 4 within its 8% keeps the first ones it can. The face then
takes the finest cell whose box — `4 (ns·nh + ns·nd + nh·nd)` triangles — and the skirt's at twice
the cell fit what is left. Every feature finer than four cells of the rung fades out (the blocks,
the beds and the columns by their spacing, the grain octave by octave), as the boulders' grain does,
so the coarsest rung is the same face with its detail left out. A budget that cannot hold the face
at 32 m and its blocks is refused with the number it needs. The rung is the authoring LOD; the
cluster DAG over it is the runtime LOD, so **a far face is a few clusters** whatever rung it was
built at.

The ladder's steps are a third to a half apart in cell, so a budget is used to between half and all
of it (the kilometre face at a million takes 526,928, because the 1 m cell would need about 1.07
million). Timings are a debug build on a machine shared with other agents' builds, an upper bound;
the solve is single-threaded and linear in the triangles.

**Scale and the origin** ([ADR-0053](../adr/0053-world-positions-are-f64-and-the-gpu-sees-none.md)):
a face to 1,000 m long and 200 m high is one mesh in its recipe's frame — metres from its origin,
the first vertex of its line where the generator writes it — and one instance at that origin; the
16-bit position grid over a kilometre is 1.5 cm, and the cluster DAG carries it to a few clusters
far away.

**UVs** are a box projection by the box's orientation, in metres over the set's repeat: the front
and the back in (along, up), the top and the bottom in (along, back), the ends in (back, up) — and
cut into **charts 16 repeats square**, each moved by a whole number of charts to sit round zero, so
a tiled set samples every chart from the same phase (a chart's seam is invisible), a kilometre face
stays inside ±16 (`geometry.uv_range`) while a cell is under four repeats, and E10's atlas sees
separate islands. What the box projection cannot do is follow a ledge's top: the front's charts are
(along, up), so a ledge stretched outwards by one row samples one row of the set. The blocks keep
the scree's own six charts.

### The cliffs generator

**A mountain gets its cliffs without an author drawing them.** `cliffs` is a placement generator
in the scene-generator registry ([scene_gen](scene_gen.md#adding-a-generator)), registered from
this capability (`src/cliffs_generator.cpp`, `include/domain/rocks/cliffs.h`) rather than from the
scatter: nothing of the scatter's placement machinery — a Poisson disc of a few kinds — fits a face
that is a line hundreds of metres long and a mesh of its own, and the face's recipe and solver are
here. A scene names it as `{"generator": "cliffs", "params": {...}}`, an `engine.rocks.CliffLayer`:

- **Where the steep ground is.** The region is cut into **blocks** of a world-anchored grid
  (`block_m`, 256 m by default, a whole number of `spacing_m`). In each, the ground's heights
  (`Context::ground`, whole millimetres, as every generator asks) on a lattice of `spacing_m` (4 m),
  the slope by central differences, and **marching squares** on "steeper than `min_slope_deg`"
  (50°), its segments oriented with the steep side on their left and the saddles settled by the
  cell's centre. A segment whose steep side is uphill is a **foot**; the brow and the ends of a band
  are not. The feet are joined by their shared lattice edges into lines, in the order they were
  found.
- **A line into faces.** Each line is resampled at the spacing and smoothed; at each vertex the
  slope's profile is walked uphill while it stays steeper than four fifths of the threshold (a ledge
  part way up does not end the face), at most 200 m up. The face **stands off** downhill
  (`standoff_m`; absent, three quarters of the type's relief plus 3% of the height) so its carving
  stays proud of the ground; its **height** reaches half a metre under the brow, so the ground's own
  edge stands over its crest; its **batter** is the lean that keeps its mean plane the standoff in
  front of every point of the profile — not the chord from foot to brow, which a slope that
  steepens and then rounds over rises above and buried the first faces in (the first captures). The
  heights and batters are smoothed along the line, which is cut into faces of equal length no longer
  than `max_length_m` (300 m), each kept when it is at least `min_length_m` long and
  `min_height_m` high on average. **A line the block's edge cut runs on 12 m past it**, so the face
  the next block traced overlaps its end instead of leaving the ground showing between two capped
  ends and two tapering skirts.
- **The written line is merged to the recipe's vertex gap** (2026-11-05, G127). The stood-off foot
  line can fold where the ground bends sharper than the standoff (the inside of a hairpin, the
  round end of a pit: the offset curve of a bend of radius ρ moves at a rate 1 − standoff/ρ and
  stops at ρ = standoff), so consecutive vertices come within centimetres of each other, which
  `Cliff`'s validation refuses ("consecutive vertices at least 5 cm apart") — and a refused recipe
  refuses the whole scene (the v31 hand-off's `spacing_m` 2 site did exactly that). A face's
  vertices are therefore kept first-to-last, each next one dropped while it is within 5.05 cm of the
  last one kept (`k_min_gap`: the validator's bound and a hair for the float32 rounding of the
  face-local coordinates), before the identity is hashed and the recipe written, so a line the
  generator writes always passes its own recipe's validation and the validator is untouched. A
  piece left under two vertices or a metre of line is **skipped and counted** (`faces_skipped` of
  `trace_cliff_block`, a `skipped` field of the "cliffs traced" log line, and one log line naming
  the block); the existing `total < min_length_m` test drops a wholly collapsed line first, so this
  is a net for a line that folds back on itself, not a case the tests can build.
- **The rock** is the first of `bands` whose `below_m` the face's foot is under, else `face.type`;
  everything else is the layer's `face` block (a `Cliff` without its line), its seed mixed with the
  face's identity, its `datum_y` the world's zero so faces cut from one mass share their beds.
- **What it writes.** A face's recipe is a `.rock.json` named by its canonical parameters' hash
  (`cliff-<hash>.rock.json`) under `recipes`, else the host's derived-data root's `cliffs/`
  (`Context::ddc`), else the scene's own `ddc/` (T119) — the scatter's rule for
  its procedural rock — and a file already there is the right one. The scene reader builds it through
  the mesh-generator registry like any recipe; the placement is the face's **origin**, the first
  vertex of its line in whole millimetres (ADR-0053), unturned.
- **Fallen boulders** at the feet, beside the skirt's own blocks, are **scatter-compatible
  placements** of any recipe or glTF (`foot_mesh`, `foot_per_100m`, `foot_scale_min`/`_max`): along
  the line and out over the skirt, on the ground, turned and scaled from the face's identity, tag 1.

**Tile-stable.** A block's faces are a function of the layer, the block and the ground's heights
over it and a two-point apron — nothing of another block — and **a face's identity is a hash of its
line in whole millimetres and its heights and feet in centimetres**, so a streamed world and a whole read make
the same faces, the same recipes and the same bytes whichever tile asks first. A face belongs to the
region and the tile its **anchor** is in — the middle vertex of its line, which the run-on past a
block's edge never moves out of the block. `open` traces every block of the region once and writes
the recipes; `meshes` is every face's recipe and the foot's; `tile` places the faces anchored in the
tile and their boulders; `occupies` says whether any is. **An earlier placement's footprint** within
`clear_m` of a face's line clears the whole face, in `expand` and in `tile` alike — a streamed tile
cannot name a mesh `meshes` did not list, so a face is not split round a building (yet).

The tests (`tests/cliffs_generator_tests.cpp`): on an escarpment of 30 m over 20 m along z, a block
finds one face the block long, standing off downhill of the foot, 26.5 m high, leaning back about
the band's run over its rise, looking downhill, whose recipe solves; flat ground, a 30° slope and a
block beside the band find none; a block traced alone is the same face as in a region of four, and
`tile` over every tile in reverse order is `expand`, recipe for recipe; a footprint on a face's line
clears that face and one far away nothing; a layer out of range is refused by field; and the
escarpment scene's own layer over its ridge dresses both flanks; and on a stadium-shaped pit (a
5 m floor radius round each end, 60° walls), a sweep of 141 standoffs from 3.5 to 7 m through the
radius of the ends writes faces that all validate with no gap under 5 cm, with fewer vertices at
the standoffs that fold the line (93 to 96) — without the merge the same sweep fails the trace
itself on the validator's refusal.

### Faces in the slope

G132 found the generated faces reading as retaining walls ([diagnosis](../experiments/v29-cliff-diagnosis.md)).
What the generator does about it (`engine.rocks.CliffLayer` version 2, `engine.rocks.Cliff`
version 6). Nothing is kept as it was for an older scene's sake: a generated face is open and its
ends go into the ground, whatever layer wrote it.

- **No back and no bottom.** Every face the generator writes is `Cliff.open`: the box's front,
  top and two ends, sharing their borders as the six faces did, priced `2ab + 2ac + 4bc` for a
  grid of a × b × c cells instead of `4(ab + ac + bc)`. A face in a slope shows neither its back
  nor its bottom, and what they cost now goes to a finer cell. A hand-written recipe is a closed
  box unless it says `open`: a face on open ground is seen from behind.
- **`formation`**: the scene's formation, repeated in the layer because a placement generator is
  handed its entry and the ground and not the scene. Every face's recipe then carries the field
  and `world_origin`, the face's first vertex and the ground there, so its beds are the terrain's
  bands and the ledges' ([Geology](#geology-on-the-world-formation-w85)). The field changes the
  rock and, through the tiers' breaks ([below](#a-face-in-tiers)), where a band is cut.
- **`taper_m`** (16 m; 0 leaves the ends standing): where the steep ground itself ends (not where a block's edge cut the line, which
  the next block's face runs on from), the face comes down to its least height, a metre, and its
  section is lowered until its top is 0.3 m under the ground at its foot, over this length of its
  line. A face's height cannot go under a metre, so an end is sunk rather than shortened to
  nothing: the cap is under the ground.

**The triangles.** On the test's 30 m escarpment at a budget of 20,000, the front's share of a
face's triangles:

| Face | Back | Cell | Front |
|---|---|---|---|
| a closed box, as the generator wrote before | 9.1 m (the carving's reach and 2 m) | 2 m | 36.5 % |
| the generator's face, one tier | 9.1 m | 1.5 m | 70.2 % |

G132 counted 13.9 % on v29's closed box with its 24 m back. A closed box cannot pass a half,
since its back mirrors its front. What is left of an open face is its top, which grows with the
back's distance.


### A face in tiers

**One box a line cannot both sit in a convex slope and cover it.** A face's top is flat from its
crest to its back, and a top that stands over the ground behind it is a wall's: from behind, an
open face is then a hollow shell. Holding one box's top in the ground left the escarpment's faces
on the lower two fifths of its flanks (the first agent's capture; 19 % of the steep ground of the
test's rounded crest). So a steep band is cut into **tiers** (`CliffLayer.tier_m`, 12 m), each a
face of its own that follows its own part of the slope's profile, and **the seated top is the only
behaviour**: there is no option to stand a top proud.

- **The breaks are the world's.** Break k is the level `k tier_m`, or, with `formation` (beds
  dipping 20° or less), the top of a resistant bed (resistance 0.5 or more, a weaker bed over it)
  within a third of a tier of that, in the formation's plumb coordinate: a face steps back where a
  cliff-forming bed ends. They are a function of the layer alone, so every block and every line
  cuts at the same ones. Past 20 degrees a bed does not run along a face, the breaks are levels,
  and the members carve the face instead ([Beds at any attitude](#beds-at-any-attitude)). A break within a third of a tier of the foot, the brow or the break below
  is not cut, so no tier is a sliver.
- **A tier's plane** starts where its break meets the profile, stood off downhill by the standoff,
  and leans by the batter that keeps it the standoff in front of every point of its own part of
  the profile. The lowest tier is leaned from the traced foot the same way and its line moved out
  to the ground that lean reaches (at most a standoff more): leaned from the ground under the
  stood-off foot, as the single box was, a plane is steeper than the slope and stands metres off
  it at the top, where no top can reach the ground.
- **A tier stands on the one below.** Its foot is the break, lowered to what the tier below left
  (its top less the 15 % a skyline may be broken back, and this tier's `buried` less half a metre),
  so its bottom edge is under that top and where the tier below ends this one goes down to the
  ground. The written line is smoothed once so that step is a bend.
- **Seated.** A top that does not go into the ground within the back, or whose ground comes back
  under it further behind (a fin, a rounded crest), is lowered a quarter of a metre at a time to
  the first height that does and keeps the ground 0.3 m over it from 1.5 m behind its entry; under
  a metre of face the section is sunk instead. The search runs again after the taper, along the
  written line's own normal, and the recipe carries the back it was seated to (`depth`, the
  solver's own rule on the tier's tallest height: `cliff_back_m`).
- **Ends.** Where the steep ground ends a tier tapers over `taper_m`; where a tier's own band
  leaves the steep ground inside the line it tapers over four spacings and the tier beside takes
  over.
- **What a tier carries.** The layer's budget times its height's share of the band's (never under
  2,000). Only the lowest tier has the skirt (the band's, as a share of the tier's height), the
  fallen blocks, the ground patch under them and the foot boulders. `min_length_m` and
  `min_height_m` are asked of the whole band, once a line.

| Ground (the test's, a 256 m block, budget 20,000 a line) | Faces | Triangles | Steep ground covered | By one seated box |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| a fin, 30 m, a top 2 m wide | 2 | 12,204 | 100 % | 71 % |
| the escarpment scene's ridge, one quadrant | 10 | 17,124 | 99 % | 19 % |
| a planar 56° slope under a flat top | 2 | 12,204 | 100 % | 71 % |

On all three no top is over the ground behind it (0.43 m under at the least), and no vertex of
any face's upward triangles stands over the ground behind where its top went in
(`tests/cliffs_generator_tests.cpp`, "no generated face shows from behind its own top").

**A tier's rock is its beds'** (G142c). With a bedded formation (dip 20° or less) a tier's
colour is the type's (or the layer's tint) times the mean tint of the beds its height cuts,
sixteen samples up its middle vertex, and its roughness plus their mean offset, so tiers cut from
different members read apart as the ledges' units do: on 16 m of dark mudstone under 14 m of pale
sandstone the lowest tier's red is 0.54 of the next one's ("a tier's rock is the beds its height
cuts"). Within a tier the beds are not told apart; that is the resolve's (a rock material banded
by the formation, as the ground is).

**A tier over another keeps its bottom** (T101, `Cliff.bottom`). A tier's foot is at the plane
of the tier under it, and that tier's front is carved back from its plane by its blocks, warp,
gullies and grain (1.75 m on the mean, over 5 m at worst), so an open bottom hung in front of the
carved crest and the ground showed through the face from below: the pale wedge of G142c's `foot`
capture, at a tier seam. On the escarpment scene's layer the open edges of the faces stood up to
9.2 m over the ground with no other face's rock round them; with the bottom closed (two triangles
a cell of the line, at the top's coarser cell) none stands more than 0.23 m over it, at the
recipe's quality and at Low (`tests/cliffs_generator_tests.cpp`, "no face of the escarpment opens
over the ground"). The lowest tier's bottom is in the ground and stays open. **Its foot's carving
stays in front of the ground**: a generated lowest tier stands on the ground at its foot with the
ground rising behind it, so a recess carved in its first metres went behind the ground and the
ground showed in the face over the talus's gaps (the other pale wedge of that capture); with a
ground patch, a row whose carved point is under the ground keeps the most of its carving that is
not (eight halvings against the patch). Seen in the pictures; no case measures it yet.

**What it does not do.** The tiers of two blocks meet at the same breaks but are seated
separately. The unbroken cap along each tier's top, and a face's talus by what fell from it, are
as they were. A tier's top and ends are at a coarser cell than its front ([Benches](#benches)):
75 % of the bedded escarpment's triangles are on a front, against 59 % before.

### Benches

**A bedded tier is a staircase, not a plane** (G172; [grounding](../experiments/exposed-bedrock.md#benches-on-a-bedded-face)).
A generated face whose layer carries a formation bedded at 20° or less is written with `benches`,
and the solver draws each station's column as the beds' profile (`src/cliff_benches.*`): a
resistant member (resistance 0.5 or more) stands as a **riser** at 80°, a weak member lies back
as a **bench** at the debris's angle of repose, 35°, and the first half metre of a weak member
over a resistant one is a **tread**, the cliff-former's stripped top, a quarter of the weak
member's plumb thickness (0.3 to 2 m). A member thinner than a metre steps nothing. The profile's
run over the tier is the tier's batter times its height, so its chord is the plane's and tiers
meet as they did: on ground gentler than the staircase the treads widen; on steeper ground the
treads go first, then the benches steepen (never past 60°), then the risers go plumb. A tier
breaks at a cliff-former's top under a weaker bed ([A face in tiers](#a-face-in-tiers)), so a
tier is a bench at its foot and a riser over it, and the tier above stands on its tread.

**Stood off by what the steps take back.** Between its foot and its top the staircase falls
behind its plane, most at a riser's foot, and the plane was only the standoff in front of the
ground. So the generator asks the same profile of every tier at a vertex (`BenchProfile::behind`)
and stands all of them off by the worst over the two vertices either side, times 1.1 and a
quarter of a metre more: every tier moves out together, so a tier's foot is still behind the
crest of the one under it.

**The crest's shoulder** (`CliffPoint.crest_back`). The topmost tier's top is the slope's brow,
and its flat top from the crest to where the ground rises over it is a level slab seen from the
crest. Where a tier is the topmost at a vertex, the generator measures that stretch and writes it
less 0.3 m, the most for which every row of the shoulder stays in front of the ground under it,
looked for a quarter of a metre at a time; the solver lays the front's last rows back by it (over
1.25 times its length of height, 1.5 m at least, half the face at most), fades the carving out
with it, and does not break the skyline back there: the skyline is the ground's own edge.

**The top at a coarser cell.** An open face's top and its ends are flat from the crest to the
back and in the ground past the crest, so their layers back are cut at four of the front's cells
(no coarser than four of the set's repeats, which keeps a chart inside its UV range). The
stations along are the front's, so the crest is a seam of shared points and nothing needs
stitching. A closed box keeps one cell: its back is seen.

**Measured** (`tests/cliffs_generator_tests.cpp`, the escarpment scene's layer with the scratch
scene's formation of six members, 78 faces): 75.4 % of the faces' triangles on a front (58.9 %
before), 1.48 million triangles in all (1.57 before); 20.3 % of the fronts' area slopes 25 to
45° as a bench (9.4 % before, the carving's own). From a walker at the crest's edge, level tops
over the ground near the brow: 0.12 to 0.33 m² a metre of the brow's faces with the shoulder,
1.27 to 2.14 without it — what is left is the 0.3 m the shoulder stops short of the ground. The
profile on a 30 m column (`tests/cliff_tests.cpp`): a 1 m weak member on ground at 50 and at 63°
lies at 35° with treads widened to 3.7 and 1.2 m; a 20 m one at 40.5 and 55.9°, its tread given
up; the profile falls behind its plane by 0.7 to 2.7 m.

## The parameters and ranges

`domain/rocks/schemas/rocks.schema` owns `engine.rocks.*`. Boulder, Outcrop and Scree are
version 3 with `RockSurface` (version 1), `RockGeology` (version 1) and quality; Cliff is version 4,
CliffPoint remains version 1. A rock file,
`<name>.rock.json`, is an `engine.rocks.RockFile`: `shape` (`Boulder`, `Outcrop`, `Scree`, `Cliff`)
and only that shape's block (absent is its defaults, except a cliff's, whose line it must give;
another shape's block is refused). `RockFile` is version 2 since the cliff: its `cliff` block is
**left out of the canonical parameters when absent**, so a boulder's, an outcrop's or a scree's
canonical bytes did not change when cliffs were introduced. G89 adds canonical surface fields
and changes their defaults; G94's solver version 3 invalidates every rock container again. G95's
version 4 replaces the radial hull and chip geometry, including the cliff's fallen blocks. A cliff's fields
and ranges are the schema's (`engine.rocks.Cliff`, `CliffPoint`): a line of 2 to 4,096 vertices
1 to 1,000 m long, `height` 1–200 m (40), `batter` 0–1.5 (0.1), `buried` 0–20 m (2), `depth` 2–200
m (absent: the carving's reach plus 2 m), `relief` 0–20 m (the type's: granite 4, sandstone 3.5,
basalt 2, limestone 3, dolostone 3.5, quartzite 4), `joint_sets` 0–3 (3), `rounding`, `bed_m` 0 or 0.25–50 m (sandstone 2.5,
limestone 1.6, dolostone 2.2, quartzite 3; other types have their own presets), `undercut` 0–1 (0), `talus` 0–0.6 (0.08), `talus_angle_deg` 20–45
(35), `talus_blocks` 0–256 (12), `talus_block_m` 0.1–10 m (1.5), `datum_y` (the height the beds
are counted from), `triangle_budget` 2,000–4,000,000 (250,000) and the material block.

| Field | Boulder | Outcrop | Scree |
|---|---|---|---|
| `seed` | any u64 | any u64 | any u64 |
| `size_x`, `size_y`, `size_z` | 0.05–20 m half-extents (1.2, 0.8, 1.0) | 0.2–50 m (4, 2, 3) | `size_x`, `size_z` only: 0.1–20 m footprint (1, 1) |

## The budget ladder

A body is a cube-sphere of `n × n` cells a face, `12 n²` triangles; `n` is a rung of
`k_grid_rungs` = 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 16, 20, 24, 28, 32, 40, 48, 56, 64, 80, 96, 112, 128, 160, 192. **Every part
gets the coarsest rung first** (192 triangles), so a small fragment is never priced out by a large
one, and the rest of the budget is shared by surface (`xy + yz + zx` of the half-extents); each part
then takes the finest rung its share holds. The shares sum to the budget and each rung fits its
share, so the whole never exceeds it; a budget below `192 × parts` is refused with the count it
needs. The default boulder, budget by budget:

| Budget | n | Triangles | Steps down |
|---|---|---|---|
| 500,000 | 192 | 442,368 | 0 |
| 200,000 | 128 | 196,608 | 2 |
| 80,000 (default) | 80 | 76,800 | 5 |
| 60,000 | 64 | 49,152 | 6 |
| 20,000 | 40 | 19,200 | 9 |
| 8,000 | 24 | 6,912 | 12 |
| 3,000 | 12 | 1,728 | 15 |
| 1,200 | 10 | 1,200 | 16 |
| 500 | 6 | 432 | 18 |

At every rung down to 500 the boulder is still the rock: closed, its box within a tenth of the
finest's on every axis and its volume within 15% (the test). What the coarse rungs lose is the grain
and the bedding grooves, by design (the octave and groove fades above). The rung is the
*authoring* LOD — how much detail the source has; the cluster DAG built over it is the *runtime*
LOD, as for every mesh.

## UVs and materials

### Baked geological surfaces (G96, solver version 5)

`material.surface_maps` opts a Boulder, Outcrop or Scree into a unique PBR atlas. An empty
block uses 1024-square maps, variation 0.55, 0.025 m grain and no veins. `resolution` accepts
256, 512, 1024 or 2048 and is a **total per-recipe** budget, independent of `quality` and the
triangle budget. Three RGB8 maps require at most 36 MiB of uncompressed bake pixels at 2048;
encoded bytes and temporary mesh data are additional. Runtime memory depends on texture build
and upload format; this is not a promise of a 36 MiB resident GPU allocation.

`variation` and `veins` are 0–1. `grain_m` is 0.002–1 m, `vein_spacing_m` 0.02–20 m and
`vein_width_m` 0.001 m to one quarter of spacing. Optional `vein_tint` is linear RGB 0–1;
the default is pale quartz `(0.75, 0.72, 0.65)`. Body `tint` and `roughness_factor` are baked
into the maps once; `normal_scale` remains the glTF normal-map multiplier. Metallic is zero.

Solver v10 adds independently filtered normal relief at five physical wavelengths, .12 m down
to .0075 m, between mesh recession and the authored mineral grain. Lower atlas resolutions
fade each unresolved scale instead of turning the whole normal map flat or enlarging grains.
Lithology's `granular`, weathering, material `variation` and the existing rind grain multiplier
control its response; zero granular or variation disables it. Geometry and base roughness are
unchanged. These are authored relief approximations, not measured specimen spectra; see
[the v20 surface diagnosis](../experiments/rock-lithology.md#g118-the-v20-waxy-surface-diagnosis).
Base colour is encoded as sRGB, roughness in linear G (B zero), and normals as tangent-space
UNORM XYZ. There are no baked shadows, AO or metallic ore deposits.

The baker samples the same pre-recession, pre-placement fabric coordinates as the geological
geometry. Outcrop bands share a formation field; fallen scree carries its field through its yaw.
Gneiss gets stronger folded compositional banding; a separate warped plane field supplies optional
cross-cutting veins. Fine pigment and bump detail fade over two to four texels of wavelength.
The six charts per body occupy unique square cells, four texels of edge padding on each side,
with at least sixteen interior texels per chart. A resolution too small for the body count is
refused with a sentence. More fragments therefore need more atlas resolution for equivalent detail.
Padding protects the first mip levels; distant mip averaging and chart seams still need visual
review. The maps add no triangles, renderer pass, shader fields or per-instance state.

The mode takes precedence over a material pack. Combining it with authored image paths is
refused because those maps expect repeated physical UVs. Without `surface_maps`, the existing
tiled-map workflow below is unchanged. `build_rock_container` carries the three PNGs inside
the container, and `engine-content rocks --glb` embeds the same three material slots and images
for standalone export. Cliff recipes explicitly reject this mode: their face/talus atlas is
future work. Fresh fracture masks are described below; exposed interiors, detached flakes and mineral-specific
physical destruction remain future work; veins are visual surface fields, not deposit volumes.
Sources and authoring approximations: [rock lithology](../experiments/rock-lithology.md#g96-geological-surface-maps).

### Weathered exteriors and fresh breaks (G97)

Solver version 6 adds optional `material.surface_maps.rind` to radial baked surfaces. The
existing material tint, bands and veins describe the substrate. The rind is an exterior
coating with `amount` 0..1 (default 0.8), optional linear RGB `tint` (default brown
0.18/0.12/0.07), `roughness` .02..1 (default .9), and `grain_scale` 0..2 (default .6).
Colour can be lighter or darker than the substrate and roughness higher or lower. Defaults
are authored approximations, not measured rock properties.

Coverage is amount times `geology.weathering` times the old-surface mask. The mask follows the
actual support planes, fresh facet cuts, bounded chips and conchoidal scars used for geometry;
it is shared at welded chart seams. Coated areas blend toward rind colour and roughness and
scale the existing grain normals. Fresh areas retain the uninterrupted mineral field. Zero
amount or zero weathering disables the effect. Omitting the block preserves the G96 maps.
No extra runtime textures, shader work or source triangles are required; the same total atlas
resolution and runtime mip/cluster LOD controls apply. This classification is an artistic
history approximation, not an erosion simulation or destruction-volume representation.
Later surface recession is not separately classified. Cliff atlases remain unsupported.
See [the grounding and validation](../experiments/rock-lithology.md#g97-fresh-breaks-and-weathered-exteriors).

### External tiled maps

**A box projection by the cube-sphere's faces**: six charts, one per face of the cube the grid was
made on, each the face's `(i, j)` grid scaled to a quarter circumference of its two in-face
half-extents over `tile_m` — so a chart is in **repeats of a tiled PBR set**, roughly metres over
`tile_m` across the surface. The charts sit in a 3 × 2 grid centred on the origin, each in a cell
of a whole, even number of repeats, so every chart starts on a whole repeat (a tiled set samples
each from the same phase), no two overlap (the atlas statistics and any later bake see six islands),
and the coordinates stay as near zero as the layout allows: the content build warns past ±16
(`geometry.uv_range`), and the first layout — the charts side by side along u — was what failed
three of the four recipes on exactly that warning. Why not triplanar: a triplanar material needs no
UVs, but the engine's materials sample UVs today, and a box projection is triplanar's seams placed
once, at the cube's edges, where the cluster simplifier can respect them. Each chart carries its
own border; seam overhead depends on the grid rung and extra crease vertices. Normal filtering
uses welded incidence across charts, so UV seams do not themselves create shading creases.
A large rock with the default `tile_m` (1 m) passes ±16 at about 4 m
of half-extent; such a recipe sets a larger `tile_m`.

**The material is a tiled set, or the flat PBR constant by type** (the table above), one-sided,
cones on. The set is chosen as the flora's are, through [materials](materials.md): the shape's
`material` block (`base_color`, `normal`, `roughness`, `metallic`, `height` paths relative to the
recipe, `tile_m`, `normal_scale`) when it names an image; else the material pack's
**`rock/<type>`** — the lowercase name of any of the twelve types, e.g. `rock/granite`, `rock/slate`, `rock/obsidian`
(`rocks::rock_role`); else the flat colour. **The set is chosen before the solve**, because the
charts are laid out in whole repeats of its `tile_m`: a pack's set is solved at the pack's repeat
(the recipe's own `tile_m` belongs to the recipe's own images), so the UVs need no transform and the
container records none (`UvLayout::repeats`). A recipe with no images and no pack solves and draws
exactly as before. Roughness alone in the metallic-roughness slot with metallic 0, the normal map at
the set's strength, metallic and height carried and unread ([materials](materials.md#what-the-container-records)).
A pack set with a small `tile_m` raises the UVs in proportion: a sandstone boulder at 0.5 m is
twice the coordinates of one at 1 m, so the ±16 warning is reached at half the size.

`RockMaterial` version 3 adds optional `tint` (linear RGB, each channel 0–1) and
`roughness_factor` (0.02–1). They override the flat constants, or multiply the corresponding
texture when present, after pack resolution. Unset factors retain the normal material-set
behaviour. The factors are in canonical parameters and therefore the cache identity.
Veins, mineral deposits, fine grains and weathering colours can be supplied by these PBR maps;
this solver does **not** generate vein maps, align texture veins to its fabric, or assign different
materials to fresh fracture faces. Those remain needed for the photorealism target. UV seams and
ledge stretching are also still limitations; a smooth normal at a seam does not guarantee that
two independently oriented texture charts match.

## The build

`rocks::build_rock_container` solves the file's rock and builds one cluster LOD DAG with normal
cones on (every body is closed and one-sided: [geometry](geometry.md#normal-cones-fit-to-what-is-drawn)),
laid out in streaming pages, with the material, the identity and section 34, the generator section:
`rock`, the shape, the canonical parameters. The key is `rock_build_key`: `cluster_cache_key` over
`rock_source_hash` — `hash_bytes` of the **canonical parameters** (the schema's JSON with the
shape's block present and defaults filled in, compact and in field order) mixed with
`k_rocks_version` — under the LOD options, no weld, and the page target.

`engine-content rocks <params.rock.json> <out.clusters> | --cache [--ddc <dir>] [--force]` is the
command line over it, and `--glb <out.glb>` writes the solved mesh as a GLB instead (positions,
normals, UVs, the colour), which is what E10's harness takes ([apps](apps.md)). **`build` and
`build-all` take a `.rock.json` too**: `content_build` finds any generated mesh's generator in the
registry and builds it under the same key the scene reader uses (`source_hash_of`,
`mesh_build_key`; [content_build](content_build.md)), so a manifest lists rock recipes beside glTF
files and the cache entry is the one the renderer reads.

The registry's name is **`rock`**, the word in the file's name (`granite-boulder.rock.json`), as
the flora's is `flora`. A build without the capability refuses the path with the registry's
sentence — which, deriving the switch from the generator's name, says `ENGINE_WITH_ROCK` where the
switch is `ENGINE_WITH_ROCKS` (a wart of the registry's sentence, not fixed here: the registry
belongs to `assets`).

## Not yet

- **The rock sets themselves** (G60's other half): the engine reads a recipe's block and the pack's
  `rock/<type>`; the lab has made none of the four yet, only a generic stand-in
  ([content/materials/README.md](../../content/materials/README.md) says what they need).
- **Lichen and moss**, by exposure and facing: a second material or a mask over the first.
- **Erosion by exposure**: weathering that depends on what faces the sky and the wind (upper faces
  rounder, a desert varnish on what the sun sees), rather than one rounding for the whole body.
- **The interior for destruction**: a rock is a closed shell; a fracture needs a volume.
- **Overhangs on a boulder**: a star-shaped body cannot undercut its own centre (above, "Why this
  shape"); a cliff's face can ([Cliffs](#cliffs)).
- **Caves and arches**: a cliff's front moves only along its normal, so it cannot close over a
  hollow; a cave is a second surface the face would have to be cut by.
- **Erosion by exposure on a cliff**: its weathering is one rounding for the face, not what faces
  the sky and the wind; its talus is a heap by noise, not what fell from the blocks above it.
- **Rock material by slope**: the terrain's own steep ground drawn as rock rather than sand or soil
  is the per-tile maps' job (roadmap W25, [world](world.md#an-authored-world)); a cliff is the
  geometry above it.
- **A ledge's top sampled along the ledge**: the box projection gives a face's front (along, up)
  charts, so a ledge stretched outwards samples one row of its set (above, "UVs").

## Determinism

`rocks::k_determinism` is **"derived"**: a rock is content, rebuilt from its parameters wherever it
is asked for, never part of the simulation's state. The same parameters give the same bytes on
every toolchain and at either CPU baseline: f64 with IEEE `+ - * /` and `sqrt` only, contraction off
(ADR-0035), `det::sin`/`det::cos`, a splitmix64 over the seed with one stream per (part, purpose) —
layout, joints, cuts, noise, bedding — so a draw added to one never moves another, and one thread.
The cube-sphere's direction for a lattice point is a function of that point alone, so the two charts
that share an edge compute the same bits for it and the seams weld by position exactly. The default
boulder's mesh and container and each committed recipe's mesh are pinned by hash in
`tests/rocks_tests.cpp`; a change that moves them bumps `rocks::k_rocks_version` and the numbers in
the same commit — and **must**, because the version is in the cache key: while the solver was being
tuned, an unbumped version served the previous solver's containers from the derived-data cache as
the answer, which is the rule working as written. A change to **what the file says** needs no bump:
`RockMaterial.normal_scale` (2026-10-19) is spelled out in the canonical parameters, which moved the
source hash, the key and the container's generator section — so the container's pin — and left the
solver and every mesh pin where they were.

**Owned data.** The shapes' parameter types and the solver. Nothing persistent: a rock is derived
data, rebuilt from its parameters.

**Invariants.** Each checked by `tests/rocks_tests.cpp`:

- every shape and type, the sharpest and roundest settings and no joints at all, is closed and
  two-manifold once the seams are welded by position (every directed edge once, its reverse once),
  has no degenerate triangle, has exactly `parts` connected components, a positive signed volume,
  and no face wound against its corners' normals;
- the triangle count never exceeds the budget, falls as the budget does, and at every rung down to
  500 the tested default boulder keeps its box to a tenth and its volume to 15%; an outcrop's parts all fit, and a
  budget under `192 × parts` is refused;
- `buried` is the share of the box under y = 0, to 1e-4; scree lies on the ground;
- one seed is one rock, another seed another, and the type changes the shape;
- unresolved surface features vanish at low budgets, zero strength reproduces the old simple
  mesh, doubling all physical lengths doubles positions exactly, and extreme valid surface
  settings retain closed shells for all four lithologies;
- the file canonicalizes (spacing, order, spelled-out defaults, an absent block) and refuses an out
  of range or unknown field (a material's `tile_m` and `normal_scale` among them) and another
  shape's block, each with its name;
- a sandstone boulder with no set draws flat and names no image; with a pack's `rock/sandstone` it
  names the pack's images at the pack's strength, with a texture record each, no transform, the
  pack in the key and not in the source hash, and the charts solved at the pack's repeat; a granite
  with no role in that pack draws flat; a recipe's own image wins over the pack's;
- every cliff type at budgets from 2,000 to 120,000, with and without an undercut, is closed,
  two-manifold, outward-wound and holds its budget; a 300 m × 40 m face at 250,000 and at 2,000 and a
  kilometre face 200 m high at a million are closed (`tests/cliff_tests.cpp`, which prints the
  ladder's numbers);
- the undercut makes overhangs — downward-facing triangles above the foot, a vertical line through
  one meeting the face twice — and without it there are none;
- a cliff's file is read, canonicalized and refused field by field (its line, its height, its
  undercut, a bed, a vertex's height, its budget, a cliff block beside another shape and another
  beside a cliff), a boulder's canonical bytes name no cliff, and a cliff's container is built
  through `build_rock_container` with its generator section, cones and the pack's `rock/<type>`;
- one seed is one face and the same parameters solve to the same bytes; the committed
  `sandstone-escarpment.rock.json` is closed, holds its budget, keeps its UVs within ±16 and is its
  pinned bytes;
- the generator `rock` in the registry builds the same container `build_rock_container` does, with
  the identity and the generator section, clusters carrying cones;
- the default boulder's mesh and container, and each committed recipe's mesh, are their pinned
  bytes, and each committed recipe's UVs are within ±16.

`apps/engine_content/tests/rocks_tests.cpp` runs `engine-content rocks` end to end (named output,
cache, cache hits, `info`, `build` of the recipe under the same key, the GLB imported unwarned, and
`build-all` building then skipping a manifest of recipes), and
`systems/scatter/tests/scatter_scene_tests.cpp` reads the rock field through the renderer's scene
reader (the four recipes as meshes, over 300 placed, none inside the outcrops' circles).

### Lithology, irregular masses and rounding (solver v11)

[G127 grounding](../experiments/rock-lithology.md#g127-cliff-masses-and-weathered-boulders-2026-11-04)
records the geological and environment-art sources. Limestone and dolostone are separate carbonate
presets: solution weathering accompanies blocky ledges and relatively close joints. Quartzite is a
metamorphic preset with resistant, less rounded masses, wider ledges and much weaker dissolution.
All three work in Boulder, Outcrop, Scree and Cliff, with roles `rock/limestone`, `rock/dolostone`
and `rock/quartzite`. Colours and roughnesses are starting points, not measured mineral constants.

Cliff relief reserves 35% of its envelope for broad buttresses, sampled consistently across source quality tiers. Close-joint relief uses the remaining 65% and filters independently, so Low can drop fine bedding without losing all broad relief. These are authored scale weights, not measured rock constants.

Cliff joint stations have unequal intervals, block faces have seeded independent tilts, and the
oblique fracture set fades through intact bridges. Some adjacent blocks share a face across a minor joint, producing larger unequal masses. Bedding and joints still share the formation's
datum and fabric. The sheet remains a single-valued outward displacement: localized bed undercuts
are supported; arbitrary caves and disconnected overhangs are not. The existing support constraint
and closed back remain. This is greater shape variety, not yet a photorealistic cliff asset system.

A narrow ridge with two exposed steep flanks and one connected broken crest is outside this
cliff contract. Exposing its buried back or overlapping two cliffs does not supply that shape.
[G131's ridge proposal](../experiments/rock-ridges.md) describes a separate closed ridge solver,
shared formation coordinates, budgets and the precision limit on long climbing-distance meshes.
It is a design only: no `Ridge` recipe is accepted yet, and the existing cliff path is unchanged.

For radial rocks, `rounding` above 0.45 progressively retreats the old hull's corners toward an
inscribed ellipsoid. At 1 it retains 10% of the unequal hull; the affine shape, macro warp, physical
surface detail and fresh finite chips remain. The old planar rim fitting is disabled for this
retreated envelope because those planes no longer describe its edges. Lower values retain the
previous edge-wear behavior. Rounded does not mean polished: PBR roughness is independent.

Cliff and debris UVs receive a bounded continuous variation at a 7 m wavelength (up to 1 m per
axis at full strength). All PBR channels use the same coordinates and actual UV-derived tangents;
whole-repeat chart offsets still preserve chart continuity. This breaks aligned rows of repeated
features, but cannot hide a distinctive repeated motif. There are no additional texture fetches
or runtime shader changes. The radial unique surface bake remains unavailable to cliffs.

Solver v11 changes cache keys, cliff defaults and geometry, and high-rounding radial geometry.
Soil v5 tracks this dependency; the default granite stones use rounding 0.3 and keep their geometry.

## Terrain-aware deposits (G128)

The opt-in `deposits` placement generator adds connected cliff-foot talus, scree tongues,
mixed fragments, gravel pockets and sediment in drainage channels. It belongs to this rocks
capability and uses the existing scree fragment kernel. Existing `.rock.json` output and version
10 keys are unchanged. [Research and bounds](../experiments/rockfall-deposits.md) distinguish
observed talus geometry from authored controls.

**Exclusion (G135).** A patch whose four corners and middle are all over a terrain hole is dropped from both the whole read and its tile ([scene_gen](scene_gen.md#exclusion)); gravel and rubble in a stream's bed or under water are what a bed is, so channels and water are not asked.

`engine.rocks.DepositLayer` supplies a finite `region_min` / `region_max` in world metres,
`spacing_m`, an explicit `max_samples`, and the source, repose, runout and channel controls.
`plan_deposits` samples static `scene_gen::Ground` in whole millimetres, routes D8 contributing
area in height/index order, and stands what each face sheds at its foot by the supply rule of
[What a face sheds](#what-a-face-sheds) (G143), which replaced G128's descending source envelope
and its depth cap. Channel sediment occupies low-gradient cells above the
contributing-area threshold. Pits retain sediment and terminate drainage. No water simulation,
erosion rate or rockfall hazard prediction is implied; the talus conserves the supply it is
given, not a mass balance through time.

The region is the complete catchment for this bake. Its boundary admits no unknown inflow.
Changing it can change drainage and deposits. The entire region is planned once on `open` (no patch's
mesh is solved there: [A tile solves](#a-tile-solves)), then
split into patches aligned to world tiles (`patch_m`, default 8 m), each at most 32 m and
64 cells across. The patch edge divides the tile and is a whole number of sample spacings.
`expand` and
`tile` use the same immutable plan and mesh list. IDs are candidate-patch indices plus one, kept
in order even when empty patches or earlier footprints omit an instance. Earlier footprints
clear entire overlapping patches, with `clear_m` added; `avoid_earlier: false` explicitly allows
composition with earlier source cliffs whose bounding footprints would otherwise clear their
own talus; the existing same-tile footprint limit
of streamed scenes still applies. `Context::stand` is refused: changing terrain requires a
rebake. The height source's own material masks are not rewritten or assumed to be geology.

Each patch is a schema recipe `deposit-<hash>.deposit.json`, keyed by sampled
terrain, thickness, coarse share, seed, material, budgets, `k_deposit_version` and
`k_rocks_version`, handed with its tile and written nowhere since G191 ([A tile solves](#a-tile-solves)):
its `path` names where its file would be, under `<ddc>/deposits/`, and `<ddc>` is the host's root
and nothing else (T119; [scene_gen](scene_gen.md#the-derived-data-root)): an entry opened with no
root is refused by name and makes no directory, and the world's streamed consumer opens it with the
whole read's root, so every recipe a streamed load hands is named under `--ddc` and nothing of the
entry lands beside the scene or the working directory (`deposits_tests.cpp`'s last case and the
world's `placement_roots_tests.cpp`). The mesh-generator registry builds it through `engine-content
build`, `build-all` and scene loading. No new app flag, renderer pass, scheduler work or dependency on
another capability is added. With no deposits entry there is no work or persistent storage.

The near mesh is a continuous sampled bed with a buried underside and boundary walls, plus
representative angular fragments in three overlapping size groups. Small visible gravel is
8-64 mm across; finer sediment is the bed/material. Coarse fragments become larger towards
the toe. The fragment kernel retains lithology and fresh angular facets. A local support shear
fits each fragment to the same triangular surface the bed draws; this is contact fitting, not
rigid-body settling. Normals follow the support slope. Cells with less than 1 mm depth disappear. At diagonal-only contacts a buried zero-depth cell joins the underside, avoiding a four-way boundary edge; this adds no visible thickness and is charged to the triangle budget.

The patch's `triangle_budget` covers bed and every requested fragment: surface triangles first,
then each fragment receives the finest existing cube-grid rung up to 20 that fits its equal
share. A budget below all fragments at rung 4 is refused, with no silent count reduction.
Default 24 fragments (96 before G143's third pass drew the bed's clasts as its relief) / 32000 triangles per patch; `max_samples` defaults to 262144 vertices.
CPU temporary storage and mesh builds scale with that finite region and its explicit budgets;
this first version is an offline bake and does not promise constant-time world opening.
At distance the normal cluster DAG reduces geometry; the streamed host's ordinary distance-form
cuts apply. Opaque geometry casts standard shadows and receives the existing 5 cm collision cut. The example scene and its before/after budget and LOD image comparisons are recorded on the grounding page.
There is no additional impostor chain or cover mask.

Materials use `rock/<type>` from the pack, overridden by the patch's `RockMaterial`, else the
existing flat lithology colour; since G143's third pass the bed asks `rock/scree` first, and since
T108 the loose stones are a material of their own that wears the rock's set ([A stone wears its
rock](#a-stone-wears-its-rock)). The bed projects x/z in metres per repeat; fragment charts use
the existing box projection. Baked `surface_maps` are refused for patches. Fine grain appearance
requires a suitable tiled material; this feature does not generate sediment PBR maps.
A textured deposit initializes its material-sampling record from the solved material before
applying either explicit maps or the pack's set. UVs already contain physical repeats, so all
slot transforms remain identity. An untextured deposit still omits the optional sampling
section, preserving its existing container bytes and pins. Material setup does not rewrite
geometry, vertex attributes or cover masks. The regression covers explicit and pack-supplied
base-colour, normal and roughness maps: the missing sampling record previously caused an
out-of-bounds write in materials::apply_set during scene startup.

the deposit mesh pin remains 6863964757150498499. This identity change is present before the

### What a face sheds

G143 (`DepositLayer` @version(2), `DepositPatch` @version(2)) replaced G128's capped apron with one
supply rule, and added the blocks shed below the faces and the formation's lithology. The grounding
is [rockfall-deposits](../experiments/rockfall-deposits.md#g143-what-a-face-sheds-2026-11-10):
Mohadjer et al.'s rock-wall retreat from talus volumes for the supply, Francou and Mante's 34-36
degree talus, the 31-34 degree concave toe, Kirkby and Statham's exponential travel and fall sorting,
Evans and Hungr's 27.5 degree minimum shadow angle, and block size from the source's joints.

**Why the apron went.** G128's envelope descended at the repose angle from every steep sample and
was capped at `talus_depth_m` (3 m): it knew where rock can fall from, not how much does. Below a
face with three gullies its depth at the foot was 2.69 m under each gully and between them, and its
reach 4.5 m in both: a flat-topped slab of even depth, the same under a 5 m face as under a 50 m one.
G143's first agent stood cones in the gully mouths beside it, switched on by a field; the owner's
rule is to replace a wrong shape, not build beside it, so both are gone (`talus_depth_m`,
`cone_supply_m`, `cone_max_m` and the four `gully_*` controls; a migration drops `talus_depth_m`
from a version-1 layer, and one that set it to zero gets `supply_m` 0).

**The rule.** What lies at a face's foot is what the face shed. Face area is routed down steepest
descent (D8) through the steep samples (`source_deg`, 50), each adding its own surface area (its
plan area times `sqrt(1 + slope²)`), until it reaches a gentle sample: its **foot**. A foot's
**supply** is the part of that area its pile has not buried times `supply_m`, the wall's retreat since
the ice left (default 1.2 m by [How much a face sheds](#how-much-a-face-sheds); range 0-50 m, the
published rates' 0.015-2 mm a year over the Holocene). Each foot spreads its own supply along the
feet round it over its own pile's reach `r = h / tan(repose)` through a kernel (since G183 the
cone's, `1.5 (1 - d / r)² / r`: [Cones that climb their gullies](#cones-that-climb-their-gullies)),
which integrates to one along a line, so volume is conserved; a foot's pile is the
wedge that holds what it receives, `h² a = q`, with `a = (1 / tan(repose) - 1 / tan(face)) / 2` the
wedge's section against the face (the face's slope at the foot; at least `0.1 / tan(repose)`).
Under a planar face `q` is the face's own supply per metre and the piles of neighbouring feet are
one **sheet**, `a h² + q h = q H` with `q` the supply a metre of the face's height `H` (it went as
the root of the face's height before G143's third pass let the talus bury its face). A foot fed by a gully
receives `1.5 V / r`, so `h³ = 1.5 V tan(repose) / a`, a **cone**, its neighbours on the kernel's
flanks (G143 had `h³ = V tan(repose) / a` from a triangle kernel). Sixteen steps, each halfway
to the law's height, settle every height together; no pile competes with another, so no step can
run away (a first version that scaled each pile by what it won of the union let a few piles bury
the rest). A pile never stands above the wall it fell from, nor more than 20 m (the patch's limit).

**The surface.** Each pile's **apex** stands against the face above its foot, over the last sample
of the face (walked up its largest inflow) below the pile's top, so a pile leans on its wall and is
never a point in front of it. From the apex its surface falls at `repose_deg` for three quarters of
its nominal length `h / tan(repose)`, then eases linearly to `toe_deg` (31) over one more nominal
length, then holds it: straight over concave, never steeper than the repose. The deposit is the
**union** of the piles' surfaces, found by one head-priority flood from every apex (a sample takes
the highest surface that reaches it; ties by distance, then pile), within `runout_m`, where the
surface stands over the ground, and nowhere deeper than the pile's own height over its foot (so a
pile on a bench at a face's brink does not drape the face below it). A union of surfaces each no
steeper than the repose is no steeper than the repose, so **where a cone meets a sheet there is no
step** (`deposits: the union holds the angle of repose`). The coarse share rises from 0.2 at a
pile's apex to 1 at its farthest sample (Kirkby and Statham's fall sorting). Channel sediment is
G128's, and a sample's deposit is the larger of the two.

**What it does on the tests' faces** (msvc-debug, the cases' MESSAGEs). A planar face whose height
rises from 10 m to 16 m along its foot stands a sheet whose pile rises from 10.0 to 11.3 m over the
foot and whose depth half a metre out rises from 6.5 to 7.2 m, the height over the root of the
face's constant to 1%; the deposit holds 2,767 m³ for the 2,632 m³ the face shed (5% over: the
wedge on a meadow that falls away from it). A planar 24 m face stands 14.0 m piles along its whole
foot, the same to 0.2%. Its three funnel gullies, 12 m apart, each route 43.6 m³ (at 1 m of retreat)
to their largest foot against 13.4 m³ a planar foot, and their piles stand 8.7 m over the foot
against 7.6 m between them (G143's numbers; with the buried face's feedback and G183's kernel,
8.0 m against 6.3 m): **on a face this short with gullies this close, the cones merge into an
apron that bulges at each gully** (each cone reaches farther than the gullies are apart). A cone
stands alone where its gully drains more face than the faces between it shed, as in reels r04 and
r19, where a gully drains a basin; the review pictures' funnel face (G143's report) is built so.

**Shed blocks.** The top of a talus is a deposit's gentle sample under a sample whose top is steep
and higher, with no steep drop below it (a notch's floor at a face's brink is a gully's head, not a
talus' top). From those tops a shadow envelope runs down the deposit's top, never uphill, losing
`tan(shadow_deg)` (27.5) a metre: Evans and Hungr's line. Candidates stand on the world's lattice
near a metre (a whole number of spacings), so a block is where it is whatever window or tile asks;
each carries `blocks_per_100m2 / 100 * exp(-d / block_decay_m)` blocks, d its run-out along the
envelope. The default is 20 a 100 m² at the talus' top, authored against the reels (r04: "granite
boulders 1-3 m scattered on the meadow"): no published count of blocks a unit area below a face was
found (the USGS counts boulders over 1 m on a 20 m grid for rock avalanches, not talus feet). A block
is kept only where the straight line from its talus' top falls at no less than `shadow_deg`, where
the top under it is no steeper than the repose (3% of slack for the sampled facets, under talus'
39-40 degree friction), off a face and out of a sediment channel. Its edge is `block_m` times
`0.5 + d / (d + block_decay_m)` (the largest farthest) times 0.6-1.4; its thickness the type's plate
of its width, never more than half the bed's thickness where the layer has a formation; its burial
`buried_min`-`buried_max` (0.15-0.5) of its height by a drawn age. The patch its centre stands in
builds it with the scree's fragment kernel at the finest rung under `block_triangles` (432: rung 6)
and shears it onto the plane of the top under it, so it rests on every side. A patch of meadow with
blocks and no bed is a patch.

**The formation.** With `formation` (the scene's `engine.scene.Formation`, carried like a cliff's,
W85), every deposit sample reads the bed at the face it came from: its pile's apex, or a block's
talus' face. The bed is `gfx::formation_bed` at `gfx::formation_coordinate` of that face sample,
through `geology.h`'s includes as they are. Its rock is the `lithology` row naming the bed, else the
type its name spells in any case (`Limestone`), else `patch.type`. A patch is one material, so it
takes the type most of its deposit samples and blocks voted for (the lowest type on a tie).

**A bed of scree is a surface whose largest blocks alone are geometry.** The deposit is one merged
mesh a patch (8 m by default, at most four a 16 m tile and sixteen a 32 m one), with the
representative fragments and the blocks in the same mesh, never an instance a stone. The cluster
DAG is its LOD policy and its coarsest level its far form. A bed's bare rim on steep ground (a
sample under 1 mm deep with more than 10 cm of relief round it) sinks by half that relief, top and
underside together, because a bed triangle between a deep sample and a face's sample stood out of a
face the terrain triangulates the other way (`k_deposit_version` 2).

**What the ground is under it.** Nothing can raise the terrain yet (W93 is reshaping the ground's
edit), so the deposit is a bed lying on the ground it was asked for: the terrain under it stays the
gully's floor and the foot, and a walker's collision meets the bed's own mesh where the scene's
collision takes deposits (G128's 5 cm cut) and the ground otherwise. A raising edit would put the
deposit's surface into the tile heights at every level, so the far terrain, the cover and the
placements that read the ground (G135's exclusion, the sward) would see it, and the bed could thin
to its fragments and blocks; until then it does not clear the sward or the litter it covers.

**Determinism.** Integer millimetres for every place, `det::exp`/`det::log` for the cube root and
the decay, one correctly rounded square root for distances and areas, a fixed count of kernel steps
updated together in pile order, splitmix64 per world candidate; the plan and a patch with a block
are pinned (`deposits: G143's plan and a patch with blocks are their pinned bytes`).

### How much a face sheds

G143's third pass, 2026-11-11 ([grounding](../experiments/rockfall-deposits.md#g143s-third-pass-the-shading-fault-the-supply-by-setting-and-the-rubble-2026-11-11)).
**A buried face sheds nothing.** Fisher (1866) and Lehmann (1933) model a talus rising against its
wall: the part it buries stops shedding, so the exposed face shrinks as the talus grows and the
talus can never bury the wall that feeds it (Sanders 2012: "perhaps no other depositional system
with such a rigid negative feedback"). Each pile now knows the face samples routed to its foot and
their heights over it, and sheds the supply of those above its own top only; the heights settle
together as before. On a planar face of height `H` at slope `s` the sheet then stands at the root
of `a h² + q h - q H = 0`, `q = R sqrt(1 + s²) / s`: the 10 m and 100 m faces of `deposits: a
buried face sheds nothing, so the talus never buries its wall` stand 4.17 m and 15.4 m against the
law's 4.12 and 15.6 (without the feedback the law gives 5.38 and 17.0, and the first pass's 3.5 m
gave 9.1 m against a 10 m wall).

**The pile's top meets the face between samples.** The apex was the last face sample under the
pile's top and the surface was measured from it, so every pile stood up to half a spacing out from
its face and held more than it shed: 12% more on the rising test face once the feedback made its
piles lower. The surface is now measured from where the face reaches the top (`lead`, up the face
from the apex sample by the face's slope), and the face holds 3.5% more than it shed.

**The default is a temperate wall's Holocene, not an alpine wall's.** `supply_m` is 1.2 m: Hinchliffe
and Ballantyne (1999) measured 4.3-7.8 m of retreat on a basalt escarpment since 17.5 ka, four
fifths of it before 11.5 ka, and about 0.1 mm a year now, so the Holocene share is about 1.1-1.2 m.
By setting: an alpine wall's Holocene, 3-4.2 m (Mohadjer et al. 2020's 0.27-0.38 mm a year over
11 ka); a temperate wall since deglaciation, 4.3-7.8 m (Hinchliffe and Ballantyne); a wall in
recent rockfall, Sanders' 7-18 mm a year for decades after the event. At 1.2 m a 10 m crag is
buried 42% and a 100 m wall 15%; at the alpine 3.5 m, 59% and 25% (the talus' 20 m cap holds the
latter at 20%). What sets it from the world (a layer's elevation, aspect and climate from
`scene_gen::Site`, G144, and the age of the deglaciation) is not read yet; the field is the knob.

### Cones that climb their gullies

G183, 2026-11-11 (`k_deposit_version` 7; [grounding](../experiments/rockfall-deposits.md#g183-and-g182-cones-that-climb-and-the-bed-at-one-metre-2026-11-11)).
From across, G143's apron under the gullied review face was a thin even band with a ruled upper
edge and a small pile at each gully's foot: a gravel berm against the wall, not cones that climb
their gullies and merge into an apron between. Sanders et al. (2009) describe the two forms: cones
below cliffs whose chutes "focus" the supply into point sources, aprons below planar cliffs, and
cones that "laterally merge" where the point sources are close. Talus slopes of 29-36 degrees
(Wegner et al. 2021) put the default repose's 35 inside the measured range.

**Why the edge was ruled.** Every foot spreads its supply along the wall to the feet within its
pile's reach, and a foot's pile is the wedge holding what it receives, `h = sqrt(q / a)`. A triangle
kernel `(1 - d / r) / r` gives a point source's neighbours `h0 sqrt(1 - d / r)`: a ridge along the
wall standing above the cone's own surface, about 0.7 of the apex's height half a reach away where
a cone stands at half. The gully's volume was spent raising the apron beside it, and the margin
between two gullies 12 m apart on the 24 m test face stood at 0.90 of the cones'. **The kernel is
now the cone's**, `1.5 (1 - d / r)² / r`, which also integrates to one along a line, so a planar
face's sheet is the same sheet (the sheet and buried-face cases hold to 1%), but a point source's
neighbours stand `h0 (1 - d / r)`: the pile's top falls along the wall at the repose angle, the
cone's own surface. A gully's pile is then `h³ = 1.5 V tan(repose) / a` (15% taller for its volume
than the triangle's), its apex walked further up the gully's floor ([What a face sheds](#what-a-face-sheds)),
and the margin falls from each apex to the apron the spurs shed. On the discrete feet the kernel's
sum is `1 + sp² / (2 r²)` where `r` is a whole number of spacings, so it is divided by that: a short
pile's volume is conserved on the lattice too.

**What it does** (`deposits: cones climb their gullies and the apron between them is lower, 10 to
100 m`, the default 1.2 m supply, gullies a fifth of the wall deep): the margin along the wall
stands at the gullies and between them as below, the apron at most four fifths of the cones where
the cones are cones (a design value: the triangle kernel fails it on every face but the crag).

| Wall, gullies apart | Cones' margin | Apron's | Ratio (triangle kernel) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 10 m crag, 12 m | 4.45 m | 4.13 m | 0.93 (0.93) |
| 24 m face, 12 m | 8.42 m | 6.30 m | 0.75 (0.90) |
| 24 m face, 18 m | 8.39 m | 6.57 m | 0.78 (0.84) |
| 100 m wall, 40 m | 19.9 m | 15.0 m | 0.75 (0.81) |

The crag's cones reach 6.3 m along the wall from gullies 6 m from their midpoint, so they merge:
an apron that bulges at each gully, as on the reels where a short wall is closely gullied. The
buried-face feedback holds at both ends (the 10 m crag's cones bury 45% of it, the 100 m wall's
20%). What the kernel cannot do: make a gully drain more than its face. On the review face the
gullies are 5 m notches in a 24 m wall and drain a modest share, so its cones stand 2 m over the
apron and not twice its height; a gully that heads into a basin (r04) makes the larger cone by the
same rule. Rockfall concentrated in gullies beyond the area they drain (a jointed gully retreating
faster than its spurs) would raise the contrast further; no inventory with numbers was found.

### Where a pile comes to rest

G190, 2026-11-12 (`k_deposit_version` 9, and 10 for the edited faces below; [grounding](../experiments/rockfall-deposits.md#g190-where-a-pile-comes-to-rest-2026-11-12)).
The forest proof of concept withheld the deposits from its world for two reasons (its asks audit,
gaps 41 and 42): below its massifs the ground falls at 35 to 50 degrees for about 100 m before it
can hold anything, and **the foot was the first sample under `source_deg`**, so every pile stood on
ground steeper than its own surface, which never met the ground; and **the flood cut such a surface
at `runout_m` or at the pile's height**, so the bed ended in a vertical wall, up to 19 m measured,
half its outline stepping more than a metre.

**Where it rests.** Rock that leaves a face falls, bounces and rolls through a transit zone and
comes to rest in a deposition zone where the slope no longer keeps it moving (Dorren 2003's three
zones; the talus' own friction angle, Francou and Mante's 34-36 degrees, and its concave base at
31-34). A pile here is a surface that falls at the repose and eases to `toe_deg`, so it meets the
ground only where the ground is gentler than its toe. **A sample no gentler than the toe carries
what reaches it**: a face's area is routed down steepest descent through the face and every carrying
sample below it, and its foot is the first sample that does not carry, so on the forest's ground the
pile stands on the meadow at the bottom of the transit slope and its wedge leans up that slope
(its section against the slope it leans on, as G143's against a face). A sample on the region's border
whose lower neighbours are all outside it carries too: what reaches it leaves the region.

**It never ends in a cut.** After each flood, every bare sample beside a pile's sample is asked
how far that pile's own surface would stand over its ground. A surface that meets the ground stands
under it; one cut by the run-out or by the pile's height stands the cut's height over it, and a pile
whose cut exceeds `k_cut_m` is **moved on**: down its fall line, over the next brink (a carrying
sample) to the first ground below it that holds, its face's area with it, and the round runs again.
So is a pile that **drapes** a drop below it, which meets the ground at last and so is no cut but a
wedge filling the drop (found by what it covers: carrying ground lower than its own foot by more than
its height; on a 2 m bench at a 45-degree drop it held five times what it shed). A pile with no brink
below it, or still cut after `k_rest_moves` (8) moves, is **refused**, and the plan records each
refusal (`DepositPlan::refused`: the foot in world millimetres, the volume asked, the cut; a fall that
left the region has cut 0) with the moves and the rounds (at most `k_rest_rounds`, 32); the
generator logs the counts and the refused volume once an entry. **The bound, `k_cut_m`, is a clast,
not a metre**: the lowest the bed's coarsest drawn clast stands, `k_clast_stand_lo` (0.2) of the
smallest clast of the 2 m lattice (0.55 of its cell), 0.22 m, a step the toe's own clasts hide.

**What it does** (`deposits_rest_tests.cpp`; the table is on the
[grounding page](../experiments/rockfall-deposits.md#what-g190-does-on-the-tests-faces)): below a
face whose foot falls 12 m at 35 to 60 degrees, the outline meets the ground everywhere (the base's
stood 7.43 m over it at 42 degrees), no foot is on ground steeper than the repose, and a pile holds
what it shed to 4% from 42 degrees up (the base's six times it); piles on a bench at a brink are all
moved over it; a fall with nowhere to rest is all refused.

**A face an edit cut sheds nothing.** A source is a rock face of the land. The forest's second world
read its channels' cut banks (45-56 degrees) as faces and shed 649 piles into a stream's bed. The
generator hands `plan_deposits` the ground before its edits (`Context::uncut()`, the terrain less
every cut, fill and bench, R144), when the host's differs from the ground, and **a sample sheds only
where both are steeper than `source_deg`**; a cut bank or a bench's riser still carries what falls
onto it from a real face above. Held by `deposits: a channel cut through a slope sheds nothing`: a
3 m channel with 56-degree banks down a 20-degree hillside sheds 312 piles over 2,103 talus samples
read on the cut ground alone, and none read against the ground before it, directly and through the
generator's `Context` hook. The plan's key (T120, [A tile solves](#a-tile-solves)) covers both
grounds.

**What it does not do yet.** A pile leaning on ground at the repose angle itself lies parallel to it
and holds only its toe: 37% of what it was asked at 35 degrees, and a moved pile against a 45-degree
drop 40-57%; the volume not held is not drawn (the wedge law `h² a = q` has no term for the toe's
concave apron, the part that holds it). The plan's region still ends where `region_max` says, so a
pile whose run-out crosses it is cut by the region (G192). The 35-to-31-degree profile is one
setting a layer; a rock-avalanche runout past the shadow line is not modelled.

### The edge and the seams

G143's second stage (`k_deposit_version` 3). **The outline.** G128 and G143's first agent put a
bare sample's top 1 mm under the ground, so between a deposit sample and a bare one the drawn top
met the ground within a hair of the bare sample: the outline was the sample lattice's staircase,
and a thick toe stood as a step. The plan now carries a signed **edge field** per sample: inside,
the deposit; outside, the piles' own surfaces continued under the ground where one reached
(`DepositPlan::sink`, the depth of the drawn top below the ground, 0-2 m), else the toe's slope
(`k_edge_slope`, tan 31 degrees) down from the outline by a two-pass chamfer distance in whole
millimetres. The first ring of bare samples past the outline is moved by world-anchored value noise
of amplitude `k_edge_amp` (0.1 m) on a `k_edge_lattice_mm` (2 m) lattice, out or in. The drawn top

### The coarse forms keep the patch's border

G143's third pass, 2026-11-11. From across a valley the apron drew as a patchwork of 8 m square
panels of different shade with dark wedges between them, and nobody had found why. The cause is the
LOD, not the shading: a patch is a **closed** mesh (its top, a buried underside and walls round it,
so it stays two-manifold for the simplifier and the collision), and a closed mesh has no open border
for the cluster DAG's simplifier to hold. Each patch's coarse forms pulled its top off the line it
shares with its neighbour in its own way, so two neighbours' coarse tops parted along it and their
walls showed between them. Measured on the gullied test face, two neighbours' cuts at errors of 0 to
1 m: the tops parted by more than 2 mm at 156 places along their shared line, up to 12.7 cm; their
tops sank no more than a millimetre past each cut's own error anywhere else, so the coarse forms
were not wrong inside a patch, only at its edge. Every vertex on a patch's border is now locked
(`geometry::AttributeSource::locked`, as the terrain's chunks lock theirs), and the two cuts meet to
0.14 mm at every error (`deposits: neighbouring patches' coarse forms meet along their border`,
which fails without the lock). **The lesson for any generator that cuts one surface into closed
pieces:** lock the cut, or the pieces' DAGs will not meet. The container pin moved to
1421504051895875902.

### A stone wears its rock

T108, 2026-11-11 (`k_deposit_version` 6). The bed samples the pack's `rock/scree`, which is right
for a bed: its set is a picture of many clasts on a dark ground. Until T108 the loose fragments, the
toe's clasts and the shed blocks were drawn in the same mesh with the same material, so every loose
block past the toe was one stone wearing a picture of many stones (G143's third pass's one-metre
picture). **A patch's mesh is now two primitives**: the bed (material 0, the scree's set as before)
and every loose stone (material 1), which wears its rock's own set, chosen as a boulder's is: the
recipe's own images, else the pack's `rock/<type>` (`rock_role` of the patch's type), else the
type's flat constant. A stone is charted at **its own set's repeat** (`solve_deposit`'s
`stone_tile_m`; the box projection of [External tiled maps](#external-tiled-maps)), so a 0.9 m
block shows half a 2 m granite repeat, not four repeats of a 0.5 m scree. Each primitive is its own
cluster DAG with the patch's border locked, merged by `merge_cluster_lod`, so a cluster names one
material. A patch with no loose stone keeps one material; a patch of meadow with blocks and no bed
has the stones' primitive alone (`deposits: a loose stone wears its rock's own surface and the bed
the scree's`). The patch's `RockMaterial` factors (tint, roughness) go on both.

**What a fresh break would add.** A block that fell this century shows a fresh break on the faces
it broke along: paler and less rough than its weathered face, without lichen (the G97 rind is the
same idea for a baked boulder). A stone that has lain on the talus for millennia is weathered all
round. A second role a rock, `rock/<type>-fresh`, would let the blocks' broken faces (the fragment
kernel already knows which facets are cut) and the youngest blocks (the plan draws each block's
age as its burial) take it; the matgen sets have no such role yet, so this is a request to the
material sets' row, not built here.

### A patch under the cluster limit

G191's review, 2026-11-12 (`k_deposit_version` 11). A streamed host refuses a tile's mesh over
`renderer.meshes.max_clusters` (4,096) by name, at the walk, and its tile draws the rest without it
([world](world.md#a-tiles-meshes-residency-by-reference)). A patch is one mesh, so a patch over the
limit is a hole in the talus that appears as a walker comes near. The first G191 capped a patch at
160,000 triangles, blocks included, because the largest patch of one test massif built 3,934
clusters: a 4% margin on one terrain.

**Why no bound on the input holds it.** The builder is meshoptimizer's cluster LOD (`clodBuild`).
One clustering of T triangles makes at most `max(ceil(3T / (v - 2)), ceil(T / t))` clusters
(`meshopt_buildMeshletsBound`, with v = 64 vertices and t = 124 triangles a cluster): 6,194 at the
forest's 128,000, over the limit before a level of the DAG is built, and a mesh of many separate
parts (the fragments and blocks, each a closed solid of a few hundred triangles) fills its clusters
to the vertex limit, not the triangle limit, which is why the count follows the parts more than the
triangles. Above the leaves, each level simplifies a group of clusters to half (and keeps any group
that will not go under 85%) and clusters it again; the groups' sizes are the partitioner's and are
not bounded below, so the levels' sum has no bound short of the leaves' count times the levels' (19
at a 0.85 ratio). A bound that held by construction for one mesh would cap it far under the
forest's density.

**The limit is a mesh's, not a tile's, so a dense patch is handed as pieces.** Building a dense
patch again coarser would draw a sparser, coarser pile beside full ones, which a walker sees. The
generator's `tile` hands a patch whose finest form holds more than `k_deposit_piece_triangles`
(40,000) as **pieces** (`deposit_patch_pieces`), each an ordinary `DepositPatch` recipe over a
rectangle of the patch's samples, its own mesh and placement at its own corner:

- **Where it is cut.** Along a line of samples of the piece's longer side where the deposit's
  triangles balance (a cell's bed at the patch's steps and its share of the loose stones, and each
  block at the blocks' rung, in integers), the piece holding the most cut next, up to 16 pieces; a
  cut that would leave a side with no deposit, or one that does not fit, is not made. Cuts on
  sample lines keep every bed cell whole.
- **What a piece keeps.** The patch's samples, its sink and its gradient over the rectangle; the
  blocks whose centres stand in it (a centre on a cut belongs to the piece after it); the patch's
  rock and material; a share of the loose stones by its deposit's cells; and a budget that is its own
  bed at the patch's steps plus each stone the patch's share (`DepositForm::each`), so
  `solve_deposit` chooses the patch's steps and the patch's rungs for it. Its corner is the patch's
  plus its samples, so its clasts are the patch's (they are anchored to the world); its seed is the
  patch's and its corner, so its loose stones are drawn afresh at the same density. Together the
  pieces hold every stone and block of the patch, at the patch's form, plus the walls along their
  cuts (`deposits: a dense patch is handed as pieces that keep what it holds`).
- **Where pieces meet.** A piece's border is locked as a patch's is ([The coarse forms keep the
  patch's border](#the-coarse-forms-keep-the-patchs-border)), so two pieces meet at every coarse form
  as two patches do, and the walls along a cut are back to back inside the bed, as between patches.
- **Names.** The first piece keeps the patch's placement id; the k-th is the id plus k times the
  layer's patch count, so every placement of an entry has its own stable name. The pieces are a
  function of the patch's recipe alone, so a tile hands the same bytes whenever it comes in.

**And the build holds it.** `build_deposit_container` counts the merged DAG's clusters; over its
`clusters_max` (`k_deposit_clusters_max`, 4,096, the host's default) it builds the patch again at
half its `triangle_budget`, half its fragments and half its blocks' rung (192 at the least), up to
eight times, logs each (`deposit patch built again under the cluster limit`, naming the patch by its
hash and the count) and counts it (`deposit_builds_reduced()`). Each attempt is a function of the
recipe alone, so the container a recipe builds is the same bytes on every host and every run, and
the derived-data cache's key (the recipe) stays the key. A patch still over the limit after its
builds is refused by its build with a sentence naming it and its count (`engine.rocks deposits:
patch deposit-<hash> builds <n> clusters after <k> builds, over the 4096 a streamed host admits`),
so no container over the limit ever reaches a host. The pieces are why nothing reaches that path at
the forest's density; it is the bound's guarantee, not its plan. The generator's 160,000-triangle cap
at `open` is gone with it.

**Why 40,000.** The cluster count is a density, triangles a cluster, and it is the loose stones
that make it dense: a closed stone of 192 or 432 triangles fills its clusters to the vertex limit.
Measured on the two cases (msvc-debug, 2026-11-12; the counts do not depend on the build or the
machine): the forest's budget with 160 loose stones on the massif's foot built whole patches of
107,000 to 128,000 triangles at 23.6 to 26.7 triangles a cluster (4,329 to 4,784 clusters, every one
over the limit, at the commit before this rule: `deposits: no mesh a tile hands at the forest's
density is over a streamed host's cluster limit`, which fails there); 600 stones of 192 triangles on
a 16 m patch built pieces at 20.1 triangles a cluster (1,915 clusters from 38,592); and the kilometre
case (`deposits: what a kilometre of massif foot costs`) at the forest's budget hands its 466
patches as 1,476 pieces whose largest builds **2,442** clusters, the most seen, from at most 40,000
triangles: 16.4 a cluster or more. The first G191's kilometre measured 29.5 at both its budgets.
At 40,000 triangles a piece is at most 60% of the limit at the densest seen, where a cut at 48,000
would have been 72%. The same tile at the forest's budget is
handed as 16 pieces of 23,000 to 34,000 triangles, the most 1,342 clusters, 1.3% more triangles in
all than the 4 whole patches (the walls along the cuts), and nothing is built again at half its
budget (`deposit_builds_reduced()` unmoved over every case).

## Exposed bedrock (G142)

"Rock emerging from vegetation": the resistant beds of the world's formation
([Geology on the world formation](#geology-on-the-world-formation-w85)) standing out of a
soil-mantled slope. [Grounding](../experiments/exposed-bedrock.md): the sources, each number
against its source, and what is authored.

### The exposure rule

`include/domain/rocks/exposure.h`: `prepare_exposure`, `ground_shape`, `exposure_fraction`,
`resistance_factor`, `exposure_at`. A point of the ground is described by its **slope** and its
**convexity** (minus the Laplacian of the height), both central differences an arm
`ExposureRule.scale_m` (4 m) either side of it from heights in whole millimetres, and by **the bed
the formation puts at the surface there**. The exposure fraction is the share of a resistant bed's
thickness that stands out of the soil:

- exactly 0 under `soil_slope_deg` (30°), whatever the curvature: a soil mantle is whole;
- on planar ground, rising linearly to 1 at `rock_slope_deg` (45°), past which no soil is held;
- raised by `convexity / critical_curvature` (0.03 1/m) on a nose, where soil thins, and lowered by
  the same in a hollow, where it gathers; the rock slope still ends every mantle.

**The soil's patches.** On a 38° slope the fraction is a half, and a rule that stood every
resistant bed to half its height along its whole trace would draw a contour map. The measured
slopes are a sixth rock at that angle, in patches. So rock shows where the fraction is above
`soil_cover`, a seeded field of the world's millimetres `patch_m` (12 m) across, and
`standing_share` rises from nothing to the whole bed over a quarter of the fraction's range past
it: the fraction is then also the share of such ground where a resistant bed shows (18 % of points
at 32°, 47 % at 36°, 77 % at 40°, all of them from 44°; the test prints them). `patch_m` 0 is no
patches, and the share that stands is the fraction itself.

A bed stands only at or above `min_resistance` (0.5), by a factor from 0.35 at the threshold to 1
at resistance 1. `Exposure::stand` is the product, and the one number the ledges are sized by.

**Why a rule on its own.** A mesh is the wrong place to learn that rock shows on a 20° lawn. The
rule is a pure function of five heights and the formation, tested before any mesh exists
(`tests/exposure_tests.cpp`): its ranges, the shape's exactness in millimetres, nothing under the
soil slope and everything past the rock slope, the resistance threshold, the resistant bed exposed
and the weak one mantled on one slope, a nose barer than the plane and a hollow keeping its soil,
the share of a slope that is rock rising with it under the patches, and a pin over 225 points of a
dipping folded formation that MSVC, Clang and GCC reproduce.
**Determinism:** integers in, then IEEE `+ - * /` and one square root in f64; the fold's sine is
`det::sin`; no allocation and no state.

### Outcrops, not stripes

With the soil's patches alone the ledges were still a layer cake: along a transect up the test
hillside 91 % of the resistant beds cropped out, so from across a valley they were stripes of even
spacing, broken along their length and not across it. Rock on a soil-mantled slope is not like
that ([grounding](../experiments/exposed-bedrock.md#outcrops-not-stripes)): resistant beds are
"horizontally continuous but not contiguous", few continuous outcrops pass 100 m, and they are a
few percent to a third of the ground; cliff-forming members stand between slope-forming intervals.
Two more factors on what stands, both functions of the world and neither of a tile
(`member_factor`, `stretch_factor`, `exposure.h`):

- **Members.** A resistant bed stands only inside a cliff-forming member. Where the formation has
  `macro_beds` the member is the macro bed at the bed's coordinate, by its resistance factor.
  Where it has none the rule groups the sequence itself: a seeded field along the bed coordinate
  `member_m` (12 m) across, standing over `member_share` (0.5) of it. That grouping is authored
  and is a stand-in: a formation with a longer sequence would say which beds are members.
- **Stretches.** Each member (`member_id`) has a cover field of its own, `stretch_m` (32 m)
  across, open over `stretch_share` (0.4) of its trace and shut over a sixth of the field past
  that. The beds of one member crop out together, as a **crag** of stacked ledges, and stay under
  the soil together between crags; a crag's end tapers as the rule's other ends do, and two
  members' crags are not one above the other. (A field per bed top, tried first, scattered single
  beds' lines over the hill, which read as contour lines from across the valley.)

The ledge solver multiplies a unit's step by both; `exposure_at` is still the mantle's rule at a
point, without them (it names a bed, not a member). On the test hillside (two 64 m tiles, 26
transects): **24 % of the resistant beds crop out along a transect, with a deviation of 19 points
between transects** (92 % and 19 before), four transects have no rock at all, and the front falls
from 2,214 m to 583 m. `stretch_m` 0 and `member_m` 0 are the stripes again.

### Ledges

`include/domain/rocks/ledges.h`, `src/ledges.cpp`, and the scene generator `ledges`
(`src/ledges_generator.cpp`): `{"generator": "ledges", "params": {...}}`, an
`engine.rocks.LedgeLayer`.

**The geometry.** A **unit** is a run of the formation's beds at or past the rule's resistance;
it has a top bedding plane. Where the ground has cut below that plane, the ledge is the unit put
back under it: a **tread** in the plane, from the line where the plane meets the ground (its
buried back, carried 8 cm under so the line is the depth buffer's) out to where the plane stands
`h` over the ground, and a **front** from the tread's edge down to the ground and a little under.
`h` is the unit's plumb thickness (never over `max_step_m`, 4 m) times `Exposure::stand`, so a
front is tallest where the slope is steep or the ground is a nose and falls to nothing where the
exposure ends: **the ends taper because the rule does**. Scarp slope and dip slope are not cases:
beds dipping into the hill leave the ground quickly and make short treads and crisp risers, beds
dipping with it make slabs, and both are the same intersection. What a front's rows recess along,
how it leans and what a steep tread is drawn in follow the beds' attitude to the slope
([Beds at any attitude](#beds-at-any-attitude)).

**Blocks.** Joints cut a unit into blocks along the strike, its thickness over `joint_ratio`
(1.3) apart on average, their stations moved by up to three tenths, and their planes up to 20° off
the dip direction per bed top. A block keeps 55 to 100 % of its step, more at one end than the
other, so its front is not parallel to the contour; one in five has fallen out; some have lost
one or two rows off their tops, so neighbours' treads step. Every joint is an open cleft with a
flank on each side (**A block is a body**, below). Each bed parts into **rows** about 0.45 m thick, each standing
back under the one above by up to `relief_m` (0.25 m), weaker beds further: the front's faces and
the undersides between them. Every tread and every underside is in a bedding plane.

**Worn round.** A jointed block weathers at its corners before its edges and its edges before
its faces ([grounding](../experiments/exposed-bedrock.md#how-a-jointed-block-weathers): the order
is published, no ratio or radius by lithology was found). So an edge is worn back by
`0.3 m × rounding`, the rock type's own rounding that the cliff faces use (`cliff_type_rounding`:
sandstone 0.4 is 12 cm, limestone 0.45, granite 0.35, slate 0.08 is 2.4 cm), and never more than
0.45 of the row under it: the tread's **lip** is drawn back along the bedding plane and joined to
the front by a worn face in the front's own normal class, so the two shade as one round, and so
is the top edge of every row that stands out past the row above. A front is **fluted** by as
much again: two scales of the world's noise (1.1 m and 0.37 m) set each row's edges back, the
same from both segments that share a vertex and from either tile. A triangle of a front, a worn
edge or a joint face that the ground covers whole (a row set back into the slope by its recess
and its flute) is not drawn. **Corners are worn in plan**, half as far again as an edge (a
corner is attacked through three faces, an edge through two): where the front meets a joint the
block is cut back at 45 degrees, never more than a quarter of its length, so two blocks meet in
a notch and a block reads as a lump and not as a length of plank. Measured on the test hillside:
0.082 m of worn width a metre of front on sandstone, 0.089 on limestone, 0.020 on slate; worn
corners are 44 % of a sandstone front's length and 10 % of a slate's; 43, 42 and 47 triangles a
metre of front before a block was a body (below). What it is not: a corner is a chamfer and not
a curve, and a fresh break and an old surface are one material. The far form has none of the wear.

**A block is a body** (G142c; [grounding](../experiments/exposed-bedrock.md#a-block-as-a-body)).
- **Every joint is a cleft.** A block's faces stand half of each joint's open width in from the
  joint's station, so two neighbours never share a plane: half the rock's wear (6 cm on sandstone)
  plus the cube of a seeded draw times a fifth of the unit's thickness, 0.4 m and a fifth of the
  spacing at most (gulls up to 0.4 m wide in a massive bed 1.9 m thick). Each side is a **flank**
  from the tread down to the ground and under it, drawn whatever the block beside does, so a cleft
  is as deep as the step (0.25 m on the mean on the test hillside, 0.91 m the deepest).
- **Rounded all round.** A joint's edge is worn as the lip is: the tread drawn in along the strike
  by the same amount, the flank's top lowered by it, a worn rim between them in the flank's class.
  A rock that wears under 4 cm (slate) keeps sharp joints (`k_rim_m`).
- **Undercut where the bed below is weaker.** A unit's last row is an undercut: from a third of
  the step above the ground (0.5 m at most) down to the foot, standing back under the rows above by
  the contrast in resistance with the bed under the unit (over 0.6, at most 1) times 0.42 to 0.6 of
  the step (0.6 m at most), grown in with the step; its underside faces down. Where every bed of a
  sequence stands there is no weaker bed and no undercut.
- **A cleft's flank follows the undercut** (G174). Where a joint meets the front over a notched
  foot, the flank's end at the face goes down to the undercut's top and in by its depth, so it
  does not stand past the undercut as a fin (G142c's `slope-near` walker-along). Two triangles a
  notch, so only a notch 0.15 m deep or more and 0.3 m high or more is followed (`k_notch_min_m`,
  design); a shallower one leaves its fin. The ledges' budget is why: slate's front stood at 55.6
  triangles a metre against 56, and following every notch took it to 57.3. On the test hillside's
  beds no notch is that large; on a 2.4 m sandstone over shale 0.56 m² of the notches' sides are
  taken out of the flanks (29 % of their area); the rows above the undercut, standing back a few
  centimetres, are not followed. `LedgeReport::notch_m2` and `fin_m2` count both.
- **Crept.** One standing block in four has crept out over the weak bed: moved out along the
  ground's fall by up to a fifth of the unit's thickness (0.3 m at most) and down with the ground,
  and settled toward its high joint by 0.02 to 0.08 m a metre (1.1 to 4.6 degrees), a function of
  where each vertex ends up, so the block turns as one body and leaves its bed everywhere. A crept
  block's tread is the one tread not in its bedding plane. It does not lean out: a lean by its
  step's height is not linear in the place, and folded a worn rim over the vertex beside it.
- **Unequal.** Blocks keep their earlier variety: lengths from 0.4 to 1.6 of the spacing, 55 to
  100 % of the step, more at one end, rows lost off the top, one in five fallen.

**A crag has mass** (G142c). The ground between a crag's ledges is its rock too: where the share
of a bed that stands at a lattice point (the exposure's, the slope and the soil's patches) times
its member's opening (`member_factor` times `stretch_factor` of the bed at the ground) passes the
layer's `riser` (0.5; 1 for none), the lattice triangle is drawn 3 cm over the ground in the rock
of the unit whose top is next above. So a crag reads as one body of rock with ledges on it and not
as dashes on turf: 466 m² of a 64 m tile of the test hillside in 1,044 triangles (2.2 a square
metre). **Near form only**: drawn in the far form too, a skin 3 cm over the ground z-fought with
the terrain's own coarser levels at those distances and showed as a grid of stripes (G142c's
streamed pictures), so from the valley a crag's mass waits on a rock mask in the ground's own
material. It is a skin: the ground under it keeps its
shape, and the beds between the ledges are not told apart in it. What a crag's height and length
should be against its hill is not grounded beyond the outcrops' lengths (under 100 m, Marshall
and Roering 2014); the threshold is authored.

What it costs: 54.7 triangles a metre of front on sandstone, 55.6 on slate, 54.3 on limestone
(**the budget is 56**, the ledges alone; 48 before: the flanks of every cleft and their rims
about 5, the undercut row about 3), and the far form keeps its 2. The risers are on top: 64.5 a
metre of front with them on the test hillside.

**Treads, and the beds' own rock.** A tread carries soil to its lip: the tread polygons are one
material, `tread_tint` (absent: a dark turf, linear 0.07 0.09 0.04, roughness 0.95), and the
fronts, the worn edges, the shelves and the joints are the rock. The rock is one material **a
unit**, the type's colour times its top bed's `tint` and its roughness plus the bed's
`roughness` (the formation's, the numbers the terrain's bands are drawn with), so beds are told
apart on a slope as they are on the ground. A mesh is therefore a primitive a material, clustered
a part and merged (`merge_cluster_lod`), and a cluster names one material; the pack's set
(`rock/<type>`) goes on every rock material with the bed's tint over it, and the soil takes
none, because the pack has no turf. What a tread cannot do yet: take the ground's own material
and the sward's blades (it is a flat colour chosen to sit beside them), cut the ground under it,
or clear the cover off its back.

**Why a lattice of the world.** Each piece is the part of a lattice triangle (`cell_m`, 1 m,
at the ground's own millimetres) between level lines of functions that are linear on it, and a
crossing on a lattice edge is computed from the edge's two ends in the world's order. So a ledge
that crosses a tile's border meets itself there, a block is the same block from either tile (its
index is its place along the strike), and nothing is decided by a tile.

**Merged, with a far form.** One mesh a tile and form, never an instance a block. The **near
form** (rings under `near_rings`, 1) is the above. The **far form** (rings under `far_rings`, 3)
is the same rule on a `far_cell_m` (4 m) lattice with one face a front, no blocks and no rows, and
only steps of `far_min_step_m` (0.8 m) or more; it cannot see a hollow narrower than its cell and
bridges it. Further rings draw nothing: the terrain's band layer carries the beds there. Under
both, the cluster DAG is the runtime LOD.

| Form | A 64 m tile of the test hillside (31 bed tops) | Triangles a metre of front |
|---|---|---|
| near | 6,852 triangles with the risers (5,808 without), 7,487 vertices, 295 clusters, 106 m of front (5,255 and 123 m before the clefts, which take their widths out of the front; 37,492 triangles and 1,041 m as every bed's stripe, unworn) | 54.7 (the budget is 56) |
| far | 121 triangles, 148 vertices, 6 clusters, 59 m of front | 2.0 |

68 % of the near form's triangles are above the ground (the solver's count on its own lattice;
74 % against the hillside's function), against 13.9 % on the front of the box G132 measured.

**The generator.** `open` reads the layer and, for every tile whose corner is in the region,
samples the ground over the tile and the rule's apron, solves both forms, and writes a recipe
`ledge-<hash>.ledge.json` for each that has ledges, under the derived-data root's `ledges/`; the
mesh generator `ledge` builds it like any recipe. `tile` places the tile's mesh of the ring's form
at the tile's corner; `expand` places the near form; `representation` is 0 near, 1 far, 2 nothing.
The patches' seed is the layer's with the world's, never a tile's. `cliff_slope_deg` (0, none)
leaves ground at or past that slope to the `cliffs` generator's faces: set it to the cliffs
layer's `min_slope_deg` and no ledge is solved under or behind a face.

**What it does not do yet, and what each would take.**

- **Nothing exists before its tile, but its recipe does.** A streamed tile may name only a mesh
  `meshes` listed when the scene was read, so `open` solves every tile of the region (at most
  16,384) and the scene reader builds every listed mesh. A tile-time mesh in the placement
  contract would make the entry the whole plan; the solver is already a function of the tile.
- **The formation is repeated in the layer**, because a generator is handed its entry and the
  ground, not the scene. A `Context` that carried the scene's formation would end the copy.
- **It does not write to the ground or tell the cover where it is.** The host's footprint for a
  placement is a circle round its mesh's bounds: a tile-wide circle for a merged mesh, which
  would clear a tile of whatever avoids earlier placements, and nothing clears the sward off a
  tread or thins it at a front's foot. A band-shaped footprint (a polyline with a half-width, or
  the generator's own "is this point rock" query) is what the treads need; the blades that
  stand through a tread's back today do so because nothing says otherwise.
- **A block is still faceted.** It is rounded by one chamfer a worn edge and split by clefts
  (above), but there is no curve, and a crept block does not lean out over its front.
- **No old surface beside a fresh break.** The baked surfaces (G96, G97) are radial-only, and a
  ledge or a tier is one tiled material a unit. What it would take: a second rock material a
  unit, the fresh break, on the faces a block lost (a fallen neighbour's flank, the rows lost off
  its top, a cleft's flank where the block beside fell) with the old surface's darker, rougher
  set on the rest, chosen per face by the class the mesher already gives each; and from the pack,
  a weathered and a fresh role per rock (`rock/<type>` and `rock/<type>-fresh`).
- **A fallen block is handed over, and nothing lays it down yet.** `ledge_gaps` (`ledges.h`)
  lists a patch's fallen blocks in order of identity: the block (its bed top and its place along
  the strike, the same number from every tile), the centre of its gap on the ground, the area of
  tread it would have carried inside the patch, its length between its joints and the step it
  would have stood to. A gap across a tile's border is an entry in each tile with one identity;
  their area-weighted mean is its place. 306 in a 64 m tile of the test hillside with every bed
  cropping out, 5 of them across the border to the next. The deposits that would lay each block
  under its gap are G143's; the generator does not write the list anywhere.
- **No holes or water**: a tile's mesh is not split round the exclusion's holes or channels.

Tests (`tests/ledges_tests.cpp`, 9 s in msvc-debug; the outcrops, the wear, the soil and the tints,
and the gaps each have a case of their own, named by what they hold): ledges only on resistant beds past the soil
slope, none on weak beds, on 20° or in a hollow; tops within 1.5 mm of the formation's planes
(0.002 mm measured); at least 60 % of triangles above the ground; no vertex whose height the
ground behind does not reach within 8 m (the retaining-wall test); a front's height under the
unit's thickness times what stands there, with ends under 0.3 m and full steps over 0.8 m both
present; a border's standing vertices matched across tiles within a millimetre; a block a body
(a joint's worn rim in the rocks' order, clefts with depth, an undercut where the bed below is
weaker and none where every bed stands, crept blocks); a crag's risers on the ground, none in the far form; the far form
beside the near form's steps; the meshes' and recipes' pins; and the generator's tiles in reverse
against its whole read, a form a ring.

### Beds at any attitude

**A mountain range of steeply dipping beds is the common case** (G200; the
[grounding](../experiments/steep-dip-bedrock.md)). The ledges and the tiers were written for beds
dipping 20 degrees or less, and on the forest proof of concept's 57 degree gneiss they drew the
wrong mountain. What a steep dip did on a hillside of 35 to 48 degrees with a 60 degree band over
it (`tests/ledges_tests.cpp`, "steeply dipping beds, by their attitude to the slope", the forest's
formation with its folds off, four 64 m tiles of ledges and the band's faces):

| Dip, attitude | Before (G142, G172) | Now |
|---|---|---|
| 40 to 75, any | every tread turf, on planes of 40 to 75 degrees (100 % of tread area past the 30 degree soil slope) | a tread past the soil slope is its unit's rock (0 %) |
| 57, strike up the slope | of the faces steeper than 60 degrees out of the bedding plane, 65 % faced downhill and 30 % along the contour: every row recessed along the fall line, sliding sideways along its own front | 6 % downhill, 89 % along the contour: the bed edges', ribs running up the slope |
| 75, strike up the slope; any dip, with or into | the faces already followed the bed edges (95 % along the contour at 75; 68 % up the hill at 57 with the slope, the overdip slab's back), their rows recessed a little off their own normal | the same faces (96 %, 76 %), the rows recessed square to them |
| 40 and 57, with and into | plumb fronts (0.4 to 0.6 % of the rock overhanging) | slab edges overhang, toppled beds lean out: 1.4 to 4 % |
| 20, any | plumb fronts, recessed along the fall line | plumb fronts, recessed along the bed edge's normal (the same within a few degrees) |
| over 20, the band's faces | nothing: the tiers' breaks and the benches need 20 degrees or less, and the 9 faces were the same 16,778 triangles at every dip and attitude | the faces carve their members (below) |

(The "before" column is the solver with its recess on the fall line, no lean and its treads all
turf, run under this case; a first count that took every triangle past 45.6 degrees was ruled by
the risers on the steepest ground, and is not used.)

**What the solver takes from the beds' attitude.** At each point of the lattice the bed edge's own
outward normal on the ground is where the bed coordinate falls along the surface (its gradient
along the ground, horizontal, reversed): it is where a front faces, whatever the slope's aspect,
and it turns with the ground round a spur, so nothing has a seam there. A front's rows, its lip,
its worn edges and a cleft's flank at the face recess along it (G142 used the fall line, which is
the same only where the beds strike across the slope). The beds' **apparent dip along it**, `a`,
positive where they dip out of the face, sets the front's lean (`front_lean`, `ledges.cpp`), as
tangents so no angle is taken:

- `|a|` under 20 degrees: plumb, as G142 built it (a cuesta's scarp).
- beds dipping out of the face (a dip slope's slab edge): the joint square to the bedding,
  overhanging by `a` (at most 40), ramped in from 20 to 30 degrees: the slabs' stepped overlaps.
- beds dipping into the face, 20 to 30 degrees: square to the bedding, leaning back by `|a|`.
- past 30 into the face the beds topple (Goodman and Bray's condition with a near-vertical face
  and a friction angle of 30 degrees): the front leans out along its own bedding by `90 - |a|`,
  never more than 25 degrees, crossing over from 30 to 40.

The foot follows the lean over the ground it reaches (a front leaning back over falling ground
goes deeper, at most 2.5 times: the lean is held to `lean * fall <= 0.6`), and a cleft's flank at
the face leans with it. **A tread steeper than the rule's soil slope is rock**: a slab at 57
degrees holds no soil, so it is drawn in its unit's rock and the turf is left to the treads that
can carry it. Strike running up the slope needs nothing of its own: the resistant units stand as
ribs along the strike, their bedding faces to one side and their edges to the other.

**A face's members** (`Cliff.members`, `engine.rocks.Cliff` version 8). With a formation that has
a macro sequence and dips past 20 degrees the cliffs generator sets `members`, and the solver
stands each weak member back from the resistant ones round it by a quarter of the member's own
thickness (8 m at most) times their contrast in resistance over half, eased over 3 m of the bed
coordinate and ramped in from 20 to 35 degrees of dip, so a face cut through steep beds shows the
members as ribs and gullies where the strike crosses it and as banded recesses where the dip does.
Not a share of the face's height: a tier is 12 m, and the first pictures' tenth of it (1.2 m) did
not read from 100 m; the member is the mountain's, and the forest's 38 m pelitic schist stands 8 m
back from its quartzite and orthogneiss. The back is 8 m further behind (`cliff_back_m`), so the
face stays closed. Under 20 degrees nothing changes: the benches
step the members there, and `members` is left out of a recipe's key when false, so every face
written before it keeps its key.

**What a scene writes on a 57 degree dip.** Nothing new: the attitude is read from the formation
and the ground. Copy the scene's `formation` into one `ledges` entry and one `cliffs` entry per
region window (the test scene `content/test-scenes/steep-dip/` is the pattern: the forest's own
formation, macro sequence included, since the members are what stands as crags on the ledges and
as ribs and gullies on the faces). On the `cliffs` entry: `min_slope_deg` 50, `tier_m` 12, the
type `Gneiss`, `face.talus` 0 and `face.talus_blocks` 0 if the deposits shed instead; the faces
set `members` themselves because the formation dips past 20 degrees, and the tiers break at
levels, which is right for beds that do not run along a face. On the `ledges` entry:
`cliff_slope_deg` 50 (the cliffs' `min_slope_deg`), `type` `Gneiss`, the rule at its defaults.
Leave `near_rings` on the cliffs at 0 until the faces arrive with their tiles ("A massif inside a
budget"): until then the far form only adds bytes. Expect every resistant bed of the fine sequence
to stand (its resistances are 0.55 to 0.85, so no bed undercuts another) and the crags to come
from the members: the schists (0.30 and 0.35) are the gullies between them.

**Pinned**: the steep-dip patch (the forest's formation at 57 and 128 degrees, folds and all) and
the first face of its band, by mesh hash ("a steep-dip patch and face are pinned to the bit").
**Tests**: the table's case asserts each "now" (4 s in msvc-debug).

**What it does not do yet.** A toppled block is a gap in its ledge and nothing lays it at the
foot (`ledge_gaps` hands it over with the fallen ones); a flexural topple is a lean, not a bend;
there is no second joint set and so no wedges; a weak member between two ribs is the ground's own
surface (the ledges cannot cut a gully into the terrain); the crest is not broken by the members.

### A massif inside a budget

The forest's steep ground is 3.40 km² past 50 degrees in a 38 km² world (its deposits part's
measurement), "tens of millions of triangles" of faces at the escarpment's density. What each part
costs (`tests/ledges_tests.cpp`, "what a steep-dip tile costs" and "a massif's faces past the near
rings are their far form"; msvc-debug timings beside other agents' builds, an upper bound; bytes
are the source mesh's, positions, normals, UVs and indices, not the clusters the GPU keeps):

| Part | Near | Far | Where it is drawn |
|---|---|---|---|
| ledges on 35-48 degree ground, the forest's formation, a 64 m tile | 3,378 triangles, 105 a metre of front (56 on the 12 degree test hillside: the steep dip's slabs are wide treads), 115 KB, 32 ms | 77 triangles, 2.1 a metre, 3.7 KB, 2 ms | near in the first `near_rings` (1), far to `far_rings` (3), nothing past (the terrain's bands) |
| cliffs past 50 degrees, a square metre of steep plan, the default 250,000-triangle layer budget in 12 m tiers | 15.5 triangles, 501 bytes, 93 µs | 2.4 triangles, 89 bytes, 14 µs (`far_budget` 4,000 a face, no fallen blocks) | as written, or with `near_rings` the far form past them |

**A 1 km window** (244 tiles) at the forest's density, 8.9 % of the plan past 50 degrees: the ledges
are 0.82 M triangles and 28 MB near, 19 k and 0.9 MB far; the faces 1.38 M triangles and 45 MB as
written, 0.21 M and 7.9 MB as far forms. **The forest's 3.40 km²** of faces is 52.7 M triangles and
1.70 GB as written, 8.1 M and 0.30 GB as far forms.

**What arrives with a tile and what is resident.** Today neither generator hands a mesh with its
tile: `open` writes every tile's ledge recipe and every face's recipe, `meshes` lists them all, and
the scene reader builds every one at load, so every row of the table above is resident from the
start whatever the camera does, and the build is every face solved (about 8 seconds for the 1 km
window's faces in msvc-debug, five minutes for the forest's, before clustering). The cluster DAG already draws a far
face as a few clusters, so a far form saves no frame time on its own. **The far form is what makes
the massif affordable once the faces arrive with their tiles** (R142's contract, `tile_meshes`): a
face's tile in the first `near_rings` hands the face as written, every other ring its far form, and
the 1.70 GB becomes the near rings' faces and 0.30 GB. That conversion is not done here (it changes
the host's room for every scene with cliffs), so `near_rings` is 0 by default: with it set, both
forms are resident and the far forms cost 18 % more bytes, and `tile` already places the far form
past the near rings and says so through `representation`. A face's far form keeps its line, its
tiers, its members and its formation's bands (the solver's coarse rung keeps every feature wider
than four of its cells) and loses its fallen blocks and its fine joints: the silhouette and the bed
pattern, the blocks gone.
