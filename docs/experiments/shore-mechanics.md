# Shore mechanics: a wave from deep water to the top of the beach (S102)

Roadmap S102 (the owner, 2026-11-12): "waves, streams, water in general needs to conform to the terrain it's on. the waves on the seashore rather than being an animation at the shore ... becomes something more representative of the real mechanics behind it: a wave moves over the water, the wave has energy, as it moves over the shore, it loses energy and height, until it's dissipated, and then because it's above the waterline, it recedes, and may be overtaken by another wave, depending on how periodic the waves are. whitecaps are a consequence of the wave breaking ... the ground wetness recession is a result of the water filtering into the ground above the waterline."

This page is the bodies' half of that (a sea, a lake, their shores): what main drew and what it read from the ground (the audit), the published models with the numbers the engine takes, one design in which every quantity at a place follows from the ground there, and what the first pass built. Running water at its obstacles is S103's page; the two name the same quantities the same way (below). The code is [water](../subsystems/water.md) ("The shore", "The swash", "The swell"); the page before this one, with S94's, S97's and S100's models, is [the shore](shore.md).

## The quantities, named once

| Symbol | What | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| d | the still depth at a place: the body's level less the ground | m |
| H | a wave's height, crest to trough; H₀ in deep water, H_b where it breaks | m |
| T, ω | its period and angular frequency 2π/T | s, rad/s |
| k, L | its wavenumber and wavelength 2π/k at the depth it is over; k₀, L₀ = gT²/2π in deep water | 1/m, m |
| c, c_g | its phase speed ω/k (the celerity) and its group speed, the speed its energy travels at | m/s |
| E | its energy per square metre of surface, ρ g H²/8 | J/m² |
| F | its **energy flux** per metre of crest, E c_g: what is kept until something takes it | W/m |
| θ | the angle between its crest and the depth contours (its direction to the shore's normal) | rad |
| β | the slope of the shore where the level meets it (rise over run) | – |
| ξ | the surf similarity (Iribarren) number, β / √(H₀/L₀) | – |
| R | the run-up: how high above the still level the swash's front climbs | m |
| u, Fr | a flow's speed and its **Froude number** u/√(g d): under 1 a wave can travel upstream, over 1 it cannot | m/s, – |

A wave in shallow water travels at √(g d), so a bore and a river's standing wave are the same object seen from two frames, and the Froude number is what S103's channels and this page's surf share: a bore is the moving hydraulic jump between a supercritical sheet and the slower water behind it.

## The published models

Each with what the engine takes. How far each source was read is said in [Sources](#sources): several here were opened only as secondary pages, and two only as abstracts.

### Speed, direction and height before breaking

- **Dispersion.** ω² = g k tanh(k d); in the shallows c → √(g d), in deep water c = g/ω. The engine solves it per pixel (`water_wavenumber`, [the shore page](shore.md#the-models)).
- **Refraction: Snell's law.** Along a straight coast the phase's slowness along the shore is kept, sin θ / c = sin θ₀ / c₀: a crest slows where it is shallow and so turns to lie along the contours. In general the phase τ obeys the eikonal |∇τ| = 1/c(d), whose first-arrival solution is the refracted crest pattern; **a plane wave is its boundary condition**. Held: `water_tests.cpp`, "a train that comes in at an angle turns to the beach by Snell's law".
- **Shoaling.** The energy flux between two rays is kept while nothing takes it: H/H₀ = K_s K_r, K_s = √(c_g0/c_g) = (2 n tanh k d)^−½ with n = ½(1 + 2kd/sinh 2kd), and K_r = √(b₀/b) the rays' spacing, which on parallel contours is √(cos θ₀ / cos θ). In shallow water at normal incidence that is **Green's law**, H ∝ d^−¼ (valid under about d < L/20). The engine has K_s — held: with the group speed taken from the dispersion relation by a difference, H² c_g is deep water's to 2 × 10⁻⁹ from 20/k₀ deep down to the break at 1.5, 4.7 and 10 s, and falls with every step inshore of it (`water_tests.cpp`, "a wave keeps its energy flux to the break, and loses it from there") — and **K_r is the next pass's** (the design, below).
- **The gravity–capillary minimum.** With surface tension ω² = g k + (σ/ρ) k³ and no wave on water travels slower than c_min = (4 g σ/ρ)^¼ = **0.23 m/s, at a wavelength of 1.7 cm**: shorter ripples are capillary waves and run faster again. A lake's catspaws and the rings of a raindrop live there. Not built; the lake's stage takes it.

### Breaking

- **The limit.** A solitary wave on a level bed breaks at H = 0.78 d (McCowan 1894); in general the steepness limit is Miche's (1944), H/L = 0.142 tanh(k d), which is 0.142 L in deep water and 0.89 d in shallow. The breaker index used for irregular seas is γ = H_b/d_b of 0.7 to 0.8. The engine keeps **0.78**.
- **How it breaks: Battjes (1974).** By the surf similarity number with the deep-water steepness, ξ₀ = β / √(H₀/L₀): **spilling under 0.5, plunging from 0.5 to 3.3, surging or collapsing past 3.3** (with the breaker height, 0.4 and 2.0). Spilling: the crest's water slides down the face as a roller; plunging: the crest outruns the base that the bed holds back, curls and falls; surging: the base runs up the beach before the crest can break. The beach scene's sea on its 1:30 beach has ξ₀ = 0.19 and in its pocket 0.70: one spills, the other plunges. Since S102 the surging test is made with each stretch of shore's own slope. The plunging shape is a later pass's.
- **A broken wave keeps losing energy as a bore.** Battjes and Janssen (1978): the dissipation of a random sea is the fraction of waves broken Q_b times a bore's, D = (α/4) ρ g f Q_b H_max² with H_max = γ d (in shallow water) and Q_b from 1 − Q_b + (H_rms/H_max)² ln Q_b = 0; Thornton and Guza (1983) give the same with a smooth weighting in place of the truncated distribution. With the flux equation d(E c_g cos θ)/dx = −D this is what makes a wave that broke on a bar stay lower across the trough behind it and re-form, instead of regaining its height when the depth does. The engine's saturated bore (H = 0.78 d) is the limit of this on a plane beach; the equation itself is the next pass's.
- **Setup: Longuet-Higgins and Stewart (1962, 1964).** The waves' momentum flux (radiation stress) falls through the surf and the mean level rises to balance it: a small set-down to the break point (about a fifth of the setup), then a rise to at most **(5/16) γ H_b** at the shoreline. For the beach scene (H_b about 1.2 m) that is 0.29 m: a quarter of the wave, and most of what Stockdon's first term measures. Not drawn as a level; it is inside R.

### The swash

- **The front is a projectile: Shen and Meyer (1963).** After the bore collapses at the shoreline the front moves up the slope as x_s(t) = u₀ t − ½ g β t² (for a small slope): launched at u₀, slowed by gravity along the slope, **its top u₀²/2g high whatever the slope, reached after u₀/(g β) seconds**. The sheet behind it is thin, its depth falling as the square of the distance behind the front (h = (x_s − x)²/(9 g t²) in Peregrine and Williams's 2001 form of the same solution). So the height a sheet reaches is the bore's (its energy's); **how long it takes is the slope's**. Friction and what sinks into the sand make the backwash the longer half on a real beach; the engine keeps S94's 0.4 for the uprush's share.
- **How high: Hunt (1959), Stockdon et al. (2006).** Hunt: R/H = ξ for ξ under about 2.5 (regular waves on a smooth slope). Stockdon et al., from ten field experiments on sandy beaches: R₂ = 1.1 (0.35 β (H₀L₀)^½ + ½ [H₀L₀ (0.563 β² + 0.004)]^½), the first term the setup, the second the swash, of which the 0.004 is the infragravity part that does not depend on the slope. The engine takes Stockdon's, **per stretch of shore**, and above it a ceiling for steep shores, where a sandy beach's fit grows without bound: Van der Meer's (1988) 2% run-up on a rock slope is at most **1.97 H** however long the wave (Hunt's limit on a smooth slope is 2.5; EurOtop 2018 says "around 3").
- **Whether the next wave meets the backwash.** The natural swash period T_s (the sheet's whole life) against the wave's T: under 1 the swash is free, up and back before the next bore; over 1 the incoming bore overruns the backwash, the shoreline never returns to its rundown, and the swash at the wave's own frequency **saturates**: more wave gives more setup and more infragravity motion, not a longer excursion each wave (Brocchini and Baldock 2008 review this and the literature behind it). With the projectile's time and S94's uprush share, T_s/T = √(2E/g) / (β q T) for a sheet that climbs E: **4.4 on the scene's 1:30 beach under its 4.7 s sea, 1.7 in its pocket, 0.9 on a 1:5 shingle bank under a 1 m, 10 s swell**. That ratio is what the owner called "depending on how periodic the waves are".

### The sand

- **Infiltration and the water table: Horn (2002), Turner and Masselink (1998).** Above the beach's water table the swash's sheet loses water into the sand (infiltration); below it water comes back out (exfiltration), and the lower beach face where the table meets the surface stays saturated and glassy (the seepage face) while the upper face goes matte within seconds of the sheet's leaving. The rate is the sand's hydraulic conductivity, which spans about three decades from fine sand to gravel, which is why a shingle beach shows no shine at all and a flat fine beach holds a mirror for the whole backwash. The engine has one `drain_s` a body and a 3 s film; the grain's own rate and the water table's line are a later pass's.

### A wall, a rock, a steep shore

- **Reflection: Miche (1951), Battjes (1974).** A slope reflects what it does not break: the reflection coefficient is about **0.1 ξ²** up to 1 (Battjes), so the 1:30 beach reflects nothing (ξ 0.19, r 0.004), the pocket 5%, and a rock face (ξ past 3) nearly everything: in front of it the incident and reflected trains stand, with crests twice the wave's height at the wall and nodes a quarter wavelength out.
- **Run-up, splash and overtopping.** As above for the run-up's ceiling; a breaking wave that meets a face throws water to several wave heights, and a rock lower than the run-up is overtopped and pours for the rest of the period. The engine draws none of these yet; an obstacle's effect is a function of the wave's height and period, the depth at its foot and its own size, and the design gives it a place.

## The design: one model, in which a place's wave follows from its ground

**What is made once a body, on the CPU, from its depth map** (the field, `gfx::WaterSurface::field`, read through the C1 spline that S100 gave it):

1. **The arrival τ of each train** — the eikonal with the local celerity and a **plane wave as its source**, so the crests come from a direction, bend and bunch. *Built for one train.*
2. **The energy flux's height** — along the eikonal's rays, d(E c_g)/ds = −D − E c_g ∇·ŝ: shoaling, the rays' convergence and spreading (K_r: the point focuses, the pocket spreads, the stack casts a lee), and Battjes and Janssen's dissipation, so a wave that broke on a bar is lower behind it. Solved by one sweep in the order of τ, stored as the ratio to the closed form H₀ K_s(d) so the texel's coarseness does not show at the waterline. With it the **fraction breaking** Q_b (where the foam is born, in place of a contour) and the **setup**. *Built by the second pass, with three corrections ([below](#the-energy-flux-field)): two ratios and not one, Q_b counted over the group's own waves, the setup drawn under half the run-up; and a second train.*
3. **Along the waterline, each stretch's own numbers**, carried onto the bank and out into the shallows by the nearest stretch: the **slope β** (`gfx::water_shore_slope_field`, *built*), and with (2) the bore's height there.
4. For a closed body, the fetch behind each place (S100's `grown`, kept).

**What a pixel (and a vertex) computes from the field and the clock, with nothing kept from frame to frame:**

- the crest's phase (t − τ)/T, its shape at the local depth and height;
- **the sheets on the beach**: every wave launches a sheet whose reach is its own (the group's shape times the place's R) and whose life is D = √(2E/g)/(β q T) periods; the waterline is the highest of the sheets still there (at most five), so on a gentle beach a new bore runs in over the last one's backwash and on a steep one the sand is bare between waves (`water_swash_front_at`; *built*);
- when the front last stood at a height — the wet sand's age — still a closed form: the least over the last twelve waves of the time since each one's sheet left (`water_swash_age`; *built*, held against a scan);
- the foam: sums over the last waves of what each left, as S100 has it.

**Why no history.** Everything above is a function of the place and the clock, because the train is periodic in its group of seven: a replay draws the same beach, the clock wraps with no jump, and nothing is stepped, stored or streamed. What would need a history: foam and sediment carried by a current that is not the train's (a rip, a longshore drift), water left standing in a hollow above the level (though a hollow's rim and the last sheet to overtop it are again a closed form), and a truly irregular sea. None is in the first passes; the cost of state would be a field a body updated every frame, and a replay that must be run from its start.

**An obstacle that is not in the depth map** (a boulder instance): a short list a body of discs (centre, radius, height above the level), made at load from the instances that stand in the surf and swash band, binned on the field's grid so a pixel meets at most two. From a disc and the place's wave a pixel gets: the front's delay and thinning in its lee, the rise on its seaward face (the sheet's speed head, u²/2g), a wedge of foam behind it on the uprush and a streak on the backwash, and for a rock in the surf the white water of a bore that meets it, by the wave's height against the depth at its foot. *A later pass; nothing of it is built.*

| A second train (next: the wind's peak beside a swell) | a second arrival and height: the field grows from 16 to 32 bytes a texel (32 MB for a 1,024-texel map) | the train's cost again on water pixels in the shallows, about 0.1 ms |
| Obstacle discs (later) | pixels in the band, two discs each | about 0.1 ms |

**Staged, with what each returns to the eye:**

1. **This pass**: a direction (crests oblique to the beach, turning as they come in; the swash arriving along the beach, not all at once), the slope's own swash (the pocket's sheets higher and brisker than the long beach's; bores overrunning the backwash on the gentle beach), a swell, the surf's height near a coastal outline.
2. **The energy-flux field**: surf that is higher on the point and lower in the pocket, a lee behind the stack and the point, a wave that stays broken behind a bar, foam born where waves actually break, the setup as a level; the wind's own train beside a swell, so the shallows keep their chop.
3. **The rocky shore and the boulders**: reflection and the standing pattern at a steep face, the run up it, white water where a bore meets rock, the lee, the pour-off; the discs.
4. **The sand**: infiltration by the ground's own grain (`swash-coral-sand`'s sheet sinking in where it stands), the water table's line (glassy below, matte above), the backwash's rills; and **wave ripples on a sandy bed under the water** (the owner, 2026-11-12): by the dunes' family of rule, driven by the orbital velocity at the bed — which the field's height, period and depth give at every place — and the grain size, so they are long and low under a swell, short under a lake's chop, and gone where the bed is rock or the wave breaks.
5. **The large wave**: a plunging breaker's lip and tube (geometry the height field cannot carry: an overhang), spray. The owner has no references for these yet.
6. **The lake's own scale**: capillary ripples with their minimum speed, catspaws under gusts, lapping.

## What the first pass built

- **A wave has a direction** (`gfx::water_arrival_field`'s `direction`). The source texels (at least 1/k₀ deep, or the deepest fifth) carry a plane wave's arrival, x · n / c at the source's shallowest depth, instead of zero. On the scene's beach the crests now come in 25° off the normal at 5.5 m of depth and turn to 12° at 1 m and 7° at 0.3 m, as Snell's law says (the test holds the along-shore slowness to 2% and the angle to 0.7° on a plane beach; measured 9 × 10⁻⁶ and 0.19°). The direction is the wind's for a wind sea — a closed body's too — and the swell's own for a swell. **The bank no longer conducts.** S100 crossed the bank at 10 km/s so that it took its shoreline's arrival; with a direction that carried the earliest arrival on a coast along the whole of it, and the first run of the Snell case found every place on the waterline reached within 2 ms of every other where 20 m of beach should differ by 2.7 s — the whole beach's swash in step again, with the lead running back out into the shallows. The sweeps now treat the bank as a wall, and it takes the arrival of the nearest water the train reached (`gfx::water_carry_nearest`, the chamfer the slope uses). **What it does not do**: the source is taken as a plane wave over the whole deep region, so a headland's lee is reached by the phase (the first arrival creeps round it) with the height it had: the lee in height is stage 2's.
- **Every stretch of shore has its own slope** (`gfx::water_shore_slope_field`, the field's fourth float): the gradient of the signed depth at the waterline's texels, averaged over the waterline within three texels, carried to every other texel by its nearest stretch (a 3-4 chamfer). A pixel's run-up is Stockdon's on that slope (`WaterSurface::reach_scale`), no higher than 1.97 H₀ and than the depth map's bank range can show (`reach_cap`).
- **A sheet lasts as long as its slope keeps it, and sheets overlap** (`gfx::swash_span`, `water_swash_front_at`, `water_swash_age`): the model above. The uprush of a free sheet is the projectile's to 10⁻⁹ m (`swash_tests.cpp`).
- **A swell** (`swell_height_m`, `swell_period_s`, `swell_direction_deg` on an open body): the train that shoals, turns, breaks and runs up is the swell's; the wind's table stays the surface offshore and calms where its own peak feels the bottom; the swell's train is drawn over the whole body.
- **The surf keeps its height at a coastal outline** (`water_outline_fade`): an open body's lift fades in from its outline only where the outline stands in deep water.
- **The mesh's sheet ends inside every pixel's front** (`k_water_mesh_inside`, 0.85). A pixel's reach has lobes of a tenth and a twentieth either way that a vertex cannot carry; where the mesh's smooth front stood past a pixel's lobed one, the water pixels between were bared — the bed drawn through the water's branch, which is not the ground's own picture — in strips with the mesh's straight edges, up to 1.4 m wide on a 1:30 beach. S100 had the same strips; they passed in a quarter second. With sheets that overlap the edge stands still to be looked at, and the first flight of this stage showed them as pale wedges on the sand. The mesh now stops under the sheet everywhere, and what lies between its edge and the front is the ground's own pixels, filmed.

What it cost and what the sheets show are on [the water page](../subsystems/water.md#the-swell) with the day's numbers.

## What the second pass built

The coordinator opened the first pass's sheets beside main's and told the owner what still read as an animation: low along the sand a straight mesh edge between a dark wet band and a bright film in every frame; every wave the last one's twin and every crest unbroken along the bay; foam laid at a contour on a smooth swell. This pass takes those three, in that order. The code's account is [the water page](../subsystems/water.md#one-sheet) ("One sheet", "The surf").

### One sheet

**What the edge was.** The sheet's body is the body's mesh and its last metre the ground's own pixels (the mesh ends inside every pixel's front, above). Measured on the first pass's own captures (1920 × 1080, the `along` camera): the ground beside the mesh's edge was one flat tone, 0.92, 0.86 and 0.83 of the dry sand's red, green and blue — Lekner and Dorf's darkening and little else — and the mesh's pixels across the edge were the *dry* sand's colour within a few codes. Two materials for one thing: **sand under the mesh's thin water was drawn dry** (the volume's bottom took a wetness only where the backwash had bared it), and sand under the ground's film was drawn wet under a film that mirrored along the sand's own shading normal by the BSDF's environment fit, where the mesh's water is a level mirror by Schlick's Fresnel with the sun refracted onto its bottom. At a low eye the second difference grows (the mirror is most of the picture) and at a high one the first is all of it.

**What it is now.** One function on both sides. The ground under a lifted body's sheet is drawn as `water_pixel_volume` draws that body's water at no depth — the same two shader functions over a zero-length path (`water_sheet_over_ground`): the level surface's Fresnel and sun glitter at the body's roughness, the sky or the traced scene along the level mirror, and under it the ground's own wet albedo lit through the surface — in the sheet's share of the pixel, which is whole under the front and drains with the film behind it (e-folding 3 s), so the band the sheet has just left is a mirror that goes matte, as `swash-coral-sand` and `swash-round-boulder` show it. And **the ground under a lifted body's water is wet**: the bottom's material takes the saturation of sand under water at every depth, since Lekner and Dorf's darkening is that of a rough surface under a layer of liquid whatever the layer's depth. The foam on the ground's pixels is drawn at the footprint the water's pixels take (the larger screen gradient of the position), where it used the footprint across the view and so was a crisp lace beside the mesh's grey mean at a low eye.

**What holds it** (`swash_scene_tests.cpp`, "the sheet on the sand is one surface across the mesh's edge"): the bay under a swell that surges (no foam), water of a known extinction and no scattering, the stand-in sky, two eyes half a metre and a metre and a quarter over the sand (along the swash, across it), six times over a period. From the ids, every pair of neighbouring pixels of which one is the body's water and the other ground the sheet is drawn on — the mesh's edge inside the sheet — and for each a bound on the step in codes **derived from the two sides' model**, L = U(1 − F) + S F: the two rays' Fresnel difference times the most (S − U) can be for a pixel under white, plus what the two bottoms' normals and a film's depth of water can take of U, through the encode's slope at the darker pixel's level, plus the dither and the rounding. No number in it comes from a picture. The same pairs with the sheet's shading off (`renderer.water.wet_film` false) break the bound, which is the edge as it stood.

**What it does not do.** The low tier's water has no volume to match and keeps the old film. A traced hit on the sheet's ground (a reflection of the beach in water) still shades it as a film. Under the bounce the ground beside the sheet reads the cache and the water's bottom does not, so beside a boulder the two can differ by the cache's term. Point lights are not on the sheet (they are not on the water either). And a windless body's still band keeps its S99 film: it has no sheet.

### The energy-flux field

The design's stage 2, with three corrections the build taught.

**The balance.** Along the train's rays — the gradient of the arrival the first pass made — the flux of energy E c_g is kept until breaking takes it: ∇·(E c_g ŝ) = −D. `gfx::water_flux_field` solves it once a body on the depth map's grid by one sweep in the order the train arrives, with the donor-cell scheme (what leaves a texel leaves through the faces its direction points out of; the direction on a face is the two texels' mean, so a plane beach's refraction coefficient telescopes exactly). It stores ratios to the closed form H₀ K_s(d), as designed, so the texel's coarseness does not show at the waterline — **but two, not one**: `incident`, the square root of the flux with nothing taken (K_r where rays turn, converge or spread, and what creeps round a headland into its lee), and `kept`, the share of that which breaking left. *Correction 1:* the design had one ratio. The swash needs the height that *reaches* a stretch of shore, before breaking — coastal engineering's H₀′ = K_r H₀, which every run-up formula is given — and that must be smooth across the waterline and onto the bank, where the broken wave's ratio falls to nothing; one channel cannot hold both.

**The dissipation.** Battjes and Janssen's (1978) bore, D = (α/4) ρ g f Q_b H_m², H_m = γ d, α = 1. *Correction 2:* their Q_b is the fraction of a Rayleigh sea over the limit, and the engine's train is not a Rayleigh sea: it is a group of seven waves of known heights, each of which the shader breaks where its own height reaches γ d. So **Q_b is counted over the group's own seven** (the same smoothstep from 0.9 to 1.05 of γ d the foam uses), the energy is the group's (its rms is 0.79 of its largest wave), and what is dissipated is consistent with what is drawn breaking. The balance is implicit in the texel's own flux (more flux, more of the group breaking), solved by bisection. On a bar the group breaks, the flux falls, and across the trough behind it stays what the bar left — the wave there is lower than the closed form of the depth and under the limit, so no foam is born until the depth asks again, in shallower water than a wave that met no bar. (Dally, Dean and Dalrymple's 1985 model for a regular wave, with its stable height 0.4 d, was the alternative considered: it needs a breaking flag carried along the ray. Not read; the group's count gives the reforming without it.)

**The setup.** Longuet-Higgins and Stewart's balance along the ray, dη/ds = −(1/(d + η)) d(S/ρg)/ds with S/ρg = E′(2n − ½), E′ the energy of the group as drawn (each wave capped at γ d), integrated in the same sweep from the source's own set-down: a set-down of a few centimetres to the break, a rise through the surf. *Correction 3:* drawn as a level, theory's setup through a surf is larger than the share of the run-up that Stockdon's fit gives to setup on a gentle beach (the fit's 0.35 β √(H₀L₀) is 7 cm of the scene's 31 cm on its 1:30 beach; theory's maximum (5/16) γ H_b is 29 cm). The run-up's top stays the fit's — it is the measured thing — and **the swash stands on the field's setup at its shore, kept under half its reach**: the waterline's low stand is lifted, more where the waves are larger. The along-ray form of the stress is the normal-incidence one; an oblique train's cross-shore component is smaller by cos²θ, and inshore the rays are nearly normal.

**What a pixel does with it.** The train's height is its deep-water height times `incident` times `kept`, shoaled by the closed form and capped; the foam's break test uses the same (**foam is born where the flux is being lost**, and nowhere a contour says alone); a place's run-up is Stockdon's with √(H₀′L₀) — its square root of `incident` — over its shore's setup. The field is read through the same nine-texel spline, now two float4 a texel: **32 bytes a texel where there were 16**.

**Held by derivation** (`water_tests.cpp`): on a 1:20 plane beach at 30° the field's `incident` is √(cos θ₀ / cos θ) with θ Snell's, to the arrival's own angle bound; on a 1:30 plane beach straight on the flux is deep water's to the break, and through the surf it is the balance's own solution (Runge–Kutta in centimetres) to the sweep's order — half a texel times the total variation of the solution's slope, the bound of an implicit step on an equation whose loss never falls as the flux rises; the set-down before the break is −E k / sinh 2kd to a texel's change of it; over a bar and a trough the flux across the trough is one number, the balance's, the wave there is under the limit, and it breaks on the beach behind in shallower water than with no bar; behind a wall on a level shelf the flux through a whole line across the map is the sea's less the wall's width at every distance (the scheme is conservative), the lee quiet behind the wall's middle and filling with distance.

### A second train

One regular train is a ruled sea. A wind sea's energy is spread over directions and frequencies, and its smallest honest picture past one train is two: **the peak, and a component at 8/7 of the peak's frequency** from the side, carrying JONSWAP's own ratio of the spectrum there to the peak's (0.37 of the peak's energy at γ = 3.3: heights 0.86 and 0.52 of the sea's), turned by the half width at half maximum of Donelan and Banner's spreading at that frequency (sech²(βθ) is a half where cosh βθ = √2: 26°), to the side the body's seed picks. Eight of its waves pass in the peak's seven, so the pair beats once a group — the sets a sea comes in — everything stays periodic in the group and on the water's clock, and since the two cross at an angle the beat's place along a crest differs from crest to crest: crests of finite length, no two alike. Beside a swell the second train is the wind's own peak wave (its period moved onto the group), so the shallows keep their chop. The second train has its own arrival field — it refracts by its own period — and shares the first's flux ratios (a field of its own would be eight more bytes a texel: its lee is the first's). Where the two come ashore each takes its share of the breaking height, so the crest they make where they coincide is the one that stands γ d; and **a wave of the group has broken where its crest plus the second train's elevation under it reaches the limit** — the phase of the second under the first's k-th crest is ((τ₁ − τ₂) + k T₁)/T₂, a function of the place and the wave — so the break starts on a stretch of the crest and runs along it, as `oblique-breaks-sand` and `shorebreak-plunging` show.

## The next stages, designed

### Stage 3: a shore of rock, and a boulder in the swash

**Built: the disc's closed forms in double and nothing more** (`domain/gfx/water_obstacle.h`, held by `water_tests.cpp`): the detour of the front round a disc (the tangent's way, against a scan of every way round), the pile on its face (the front's speed head, which is what the front had left to climb), and whether the sheet overtops it, is thrown by it or runs up it. **Not built**: the list of discs from the scene's instances, its table, and every shader term below — no picture changes.

**What is known at the place.** The sheet's depth and speed are closed forms of what the swash already carries: under a front that stands E above a place and climbs as a projectile, the front's speed there is u = √(2 g E) (what is left of its launch speed), the sheet's depth behind the front grows as Peregrine and Williams's (x_s − x)²/(9 g t²) (the engine draws half the front's height over the ground, to a quarter of R), and the Froude number of the uprush is above 1 until the front stalls. In the surf the bore's height is the flux field's and its speed √(g d). An obstacle's own numbers are its radius r, its height h_o above the level and the slope of its seaward face.

**The list.** At load, from the scene's instances that stand in a lifted body's surf and swash band (ground height within the body's reach of the level, or still depth under the break depth): one **disc** an instance (centre, radius, top's height; a long body such as a shelf's edge as a chain of discs along it), kept in a buffer beside the body's field and **binned on the field's grid** — the field's eight floats are full, so in a small table of its own: at most two disc indices a coarse cell (8 × 8 texels), 4 bytes a cell, so a pixel meets at most two discs with two reads. Nothing a frame on the CPU; nothing stored between frames.

**What a pixel does at a disc**, each a closed form of the clock and the place, in the order the reels show them:

1. *The front is delayed and wraps* (`swash-round-boulder`). Inside the disc's shadow — the wedge behind it along the sheet's way, ŝ of the swash's arrival — the front's arrival is later by the detour round the disc, (π/2 − 1) r at the centreline falling to nothing at the wedge's edge, over the front's speed there; the swash's phase takes the delay, so the front bends round the rock and the two arms close behind it as a curve. The lee closes after about three radii on the uprush.
2. *A pile on the seaward face and a throw where it is too steep to climb.* The sheet meeting a face stops against it: the surface rises by its speed head u²/2g at the stagnation point, falling round the sides as cos² of the angle from the sheet's way. If the face is steeper than the run-up can climb — its height over the pile, h_o − u²/2g > 0, and its slope past the surging limit of the local wave (ξ > 3.3) — the bore is thrown: a white fan above the pile for the tenth of a period the bore takes to collapse, its height the speed head times the share of the bore's height the face stands above. If the top is under the pile the sheet **overtops** it and the rock pours for the rest of the period.
3. *On the backwash, a crescent and a streak.* The retreating sheet parts on the rock's landward side: a foam crescent seaward of it (the hydraulic jump where the thin fast backwash meets the slower water held in the rock's lee, at Fr = 1) and wet sand at its foot that outlasts the band's drying (scour holds water).
4. *In the surf*, a rock that stands through the surface takes white water each time a broken wave of the group passes it (the foam's sum gains a term at the disc, by the wave's height over the depth at its foot) and casts the flux field's own lee if it is in the depth map; if it is an instance, the disc scales `kept` behind it over the same wedge.
5. *A shelf* (`surge-on-rock-shelf`) is the same list along an edge: the surge overtops where the shelf's top is under the pile, the sheet over it drains by the ground's own slope, and the notches between discs — the low places of the edge — pour for the period as short white falls, drawn as foam streaks down the edge's face.

**Reflection** off a steep shore (`chop-on-riprap`) is the fields' business, not the disc's: Miche's and Battjes's coefficient 0.1 ξ² of the incident height returns as a second arrival from the waterline (the eikonal run outward from the shore), and the standing pattern is the two trains' sum. It needs a third arrival channel.

**Cost, stated before building:** two table reads and at most two discs' closed forms for pixels in the band, about 0.1 ms on a frame half beach; the list and the table are kilobytes a body.

### Stage 4: the sand

**Built: the ripples' published forms in double and nothing more** (`domain/gfx/water_ripples.h`, a case in `water_tests.cpp`); no picture changes. The rest is design.

**Infiltration by the grain.** The sheet's edge retreats by sinking in as well as by running back (`swash-coral-sand`): above the beach's water table the sand takes water at its hydraulic conductivity K, and a film of depth h is gone in h/K. K spans three decades — about 10⁻⁴ m/s for fine sand, 10⁻³ for coarse, 10⁻² for gravel (Horn 2002's range, as the first pass cites it; not re-read) — so a 3 mm film lasts 30 s on fine sand, 3 s on coarse and a third of a second on shingle. The engine's one film time (3 s) and one drying time a body become the ground's own: the terrain layer's grain size gives K by Hazen's rule (K ≈ C d₁₀², not read) and the film's and the band's times follow, per pixel, from the layer blend the ground's material already has. Below the water table's line — the setup's level carried into the beach — the sand does not drain and stays glassy: the seepage face.

**Wave ripples on the bed** (the owner, 2026-11-12). Under waves the water at the bed moves to and fro with amplitude A = U₀/ω, U₀ = a ω / sinh(k d) (a the wave's amplitude at the place: the flux field's height, halved). The bed answers with ripples whose crests lie across the oscillation — along the wave's crests — and whose size the **mobility number** ψ = U₀² / ((s − 1) g D) sets (s = 2.65 for quartz, D the grain's diameter). Read on the coastal engineering reference page for wave ripples (secondary; the papers themselves not opened): Nielsen (1981), regular oscillation, λ/A = 2.2 − 0.345 ψ^0.34, and for field waves λ/A = exp[(693 − 0.37 ln⁸ψ)/(1000 + 0.75 ln⁷ψ)]; Clifton (1976), orbital ripples λ ≈ 0.65 · 2A and anorbital ones 400 to 600 D; Soulsby and Whitehouse (2005), steepness η/λ = 0.15 [1 − exp(−(5000 / (A/D))^3.5)], vortex ripples being those steeper than about 0.1. Worked for the engine's seas with 0.3 mm sand (`domain/gfx/water_ripples.h`, the forms in double, held by a case and drawn by nothing): under the beach scene's 1.1 m, 4.7 s sea ψ is about 58 at 5 m of depth and the ripples a third of a metre long; at 3 m ψ is about 135 and they are 0.22 m; by 2 m ψ is past 230, where Nielsen's regular form reaches zero — the bed is swept flat under the surf, which is where the foam hides it anyway; under a lake's 0.13 m, 1.3 s wavelets at half a metre ψ is 7 and the ripples 6 cm, steep and sharp. So: long and low under a swell offshore, short and sharp under a lake's chop, gone under the surf and on rock.

**How it is drawn.** As the dunes' ripples are, a normal on the bed, not geometry: in the volume's bottom (the body's side of `water_pixel_volume`'s inputs: the bottom's normal), a train of ripple crests along the level lines of the arrival field with wavelength λ(ψ) and steepness η/λ at the place — the phase the arrival's own value over the local λ c, with the dunes' defect lattice to break the ruling — tilting the bottom's normal before it is lit through the surface, so the ripples show as light and dark bands refracted and wavering under the waves, fading with depth by the water's own extinction. Where the bed's layer is not sand (the grain's diameter over 2 mm, or rock) there are none. The field already carries what ψ needs; the cost is one more closed form on water pixels that see the bottom, about 0.05 ms. **Not built in this pass**: the bottom's normal is `water_pixel_volume`'s, which the channels' row holds tonight.

## Where the research and the budget were traded

(As of the second pass; the first pass's two — one train, and a height with no refraction and no memory of breaking — are built.) Two trains stand for a spectrum: the sea's crests are short and differ, but the pattern is periodic in the group of seven and the two directions are fixed, where a real sea's defects wander. The second train shares the first's refraction and lee. The flux's dissipation counts breaking over the group's seven waves with one flux for all of them, so the group's small waves are lowered with its large ones where only the large ones broke. The setup is theory's along the ray and is kept under half the run-up, because the run-up's top is a field fit's. A lee is filled by the scheme's own diffusion and by the rays that creep round a headland in the eikonal, which stand in for diffraction without its phase. The uprush's share of a sheet's life is one number (0.4), where it should follow the sand's friction and what sinks in; a sheet's life is kept to five periods, past which the beach's motion is infragravity's and no train of one period draws it. The slope is the waterline's own, not the mean over the swash's excursion that Stockdon's β is, so on a beach with a berm the sheet's time is the lower face's. The run-up's ceiling on a steep shore is a rock slope's from breakwater tests, used for a natural rock for want of a better one. Surging on a steep shore is still drawn as the same sheet with no foam, not as a wave that stands against the rock.
