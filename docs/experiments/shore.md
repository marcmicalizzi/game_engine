# The shore: where a body's level meets the ground (S97, S94)

Roadmap S97 (the owner, 2026-11-06: "the better water boundary for bodies of water"). The design and the code are [water](../subsystems/water.md), "A body that fills" and "The shore"; this page is the grounding for the numbers the schema pins and the rules the resolve follows, and where the research and the budgets were traded.

## What a lake's edge is

A still body's edge is a **contour**: the line where the ground's height equals the water's level. Nothing about it is a polygon an author draws; a hollow filled to a level has exactly the outline its contour gives, which is why S97 floods the ground from a seed rather than asking for a shape. Two things lie at that contour on a real shore and are what the eye reads as "water meets ground":

1. **A band of wet ground above it.** Sand and gravel at a water body's edge are wet above the still level for two reasons: the **capillary fringe** — water drawn up the pores above the free surface — and the **swash**, where each wave runs up the beach and back.
2. **A line of foam on the water near it**, where small waves break and run up, moving up and back with the waves.

## The wet band's height: the capillary fringe and a lake's swash

**Capillary rise** above a free water surface depends on grain size. The textbook ranges (Silin-Bekchurin 1958, as tabulated in groundwater hydraulics courses, e.g. NPTEL's *Module 1: Introduction*): coarse sand 2–5 cm, sand 12–35 cm, fine sand 35–70 cm, silt 70–150 cm; laboratory columns of sand measured about 13.5 cm over five tests (GSA North-Central abstracts, 2012 and 2013, which question the larger textbook values for fine material). The fringe's top is not a sharp line: saturation falls from near full at the water table to the residual over the fringe, so the ground dries upwards.

**A lake's swash** is small: a lake's waves are a few centimetres to a decimetre high with periods of a second or two. Stockdon et al. (2006) give the 2% exceedance run-up on a natural beach as R₂ = 1.1 (0.35 β_f (H₀L₀)^½ + ½ [H₀L₀ (0.563 β_f² + 0.004)]^½), with H₀ the deep-water wave height, L₀ = g T² / 2π its wavelength and β_f the foreshore slope. It was fit to ocean beaches; for a lake's H₀ = 0.1 m, T = 1.5 s (L₀ = 3.5 m) on a foreshore of 1:10 it gives **R₂ ≈ 5.5 cm vertical**, about 0.55 m along the slope.

**Pinned: `wet_m` = 0.3 m** (default) — the top of sand's capillary range, with the swash's few centimetres inside it. A gravel shore wants a few centimetres; fine sand or silt up to 0.7 m; the field is the body's.

## How dark wet ground is: Lekner and Dorf

Lekner and Dorf (1988, after Ångström 1925): a water film over a dry albedo *a* returns (1 − r_e)(1 − r_i) a / (1 − r_i a) of the light, with water's diffuse external reflectance r_e = 0.066 and internal r_i = 1 − (1 − r_e)/n² = 0.4744 at n = 1.333; the factor is 0.4909 / (1 − 0.4744 a). It is the same relation the terrain's beach layer already uses (`terrain_material.slang`, `terrain_wet_darkening`), and the wet band takes the same two constants (`water.slang`, `k_water_wet_through`, `k_water_wet_internal`) on the pixel's own albedo: sand of albedo 0.4 is darkened to 0.61 of itself, a dark soil of 0.15 to 0.53. Saturation eases from full at the waterline to none at `wet_m` by a smoothstep, the fringe drying upwards.

**Where the research and the budget were traded.** The band is applied **after the lighting**, as a multiplier on the shaded colour, in the resolve's water modules: the terrain's material shader belongs to another row tonight (R136), and a pre-lighting change would have needed its sampling. The limit is plain: a wet surface is smoother and shines (Lekner and Dorf's film is specular too), and a post-multiplier darkens the sun's highlight on the band instead of sharpening it. The roughness is not changed. (Superseded 2026-11-08 by S99: the band is a saturation the ground's material takes before the lighting; ["Wet ground"](#wet-ground), below.)

## The foam line: what it is drawn from

**Reflectance.** Fresh, dense foam reflects about 55% in the visible (Koepke 1984, *Applied Optics* 23, 1816–1824: a fresh whitecap's reflectance, whose effective 22% is that times the share of a whitecap's life it is fresh); whole whitecaps measured since average about 40% across the visible (Dierssen 2019's review in *Frontiers in Earth Science*, of the measurements since Whitlock, Bartlett and Gurganus 1982, *Geophysical Research Letters* 9, 719–722, whose visible numbers it flags as uncertain). A swash's foam line is fresh foam. Pinned: **`k_water_foam_albedo` = 0.55** (0.4 drew the line darker than the wet sand under it on the basin, a grey-blue thread, 2026-11-07), lit as a level Lambertian surface by the sun as it reaches the water (its shadow included) and the sky's light.

**Where.** The foam at a lake's edge lives in the swash zone, whose width along the slope is the run-up over the foreshore slope: Stockdon's 5.5 cm on 1:10 is 0.55 m. Pinned: **`foam_m` = 0.6 m** (default). A sea's (S93's open bodies) is larger — a 1 m wave of 8 s on 1:20 runs up about 0.55 m vertical, 11 m along the slope — and the field is the body's.

**How.** A procedural mask, not a simulation: a line a tenth of `foam_m` wide stands between 0.3 and 1 of `foam_m` from the waterline at the largest wave's phase at the pixel (so it runs up and back once a wave period), a thinner trail of what the backwash leaves lies between it and the waterline, and value noise at a quarter of `foam_m` breaks both. Its strength is the waves' amplitude over 2 cm (a mirror has none) and it fades to nothing at `foam_m`. A line narrower than the pixel's footprint is widened to it and dimmed by the same share, so a far shore keeps its mean and does not alias.

## The waterline per pixel

The distance from the waterline that the shore fade and the foam are drawn by is **the depth over the slope of the depth map**: d / |∇d|, with the slope the larger of the one-sided differences either way, so at the waterline, where the map is clamped at zero on the bank, the water's side gives it. On a bank of constant slope it is exact; on a bowl it overstates the distance by the curvature over the fade's few metres (on the S97 basin, 1:3.5 at the waterline, a few centimetres). Four more reads of the map a water pixel.

## The swash

Roadmap S94 (the owner, 2026-11-07, with eight frames of a real beach: a sheet of foamy water runs up the sand to a ragged front and slides back, leaving the sand dark and glossy up to the highest line it reached, and the dark band dries from the top down until the next sheet, not always as far). The code is [water](../subsystems/water.md#the-swash); this section is the models, their constants and the trades.

### The wave a wind raises

A closed body has no swell: its waves are the wind's, fetch-limited. JONSWAP (Hasselmann et al. 1973) gives the peak frequency ω_p = 22 (g² / U₁₀F)^(1/3) and the Phillips constant α = 0.076 (U₁₀² / Fg)^0.22, the laws [the ocean page](ocean-surface.md#the-published-models) already cites. The swash takes **the peak period T = 2π/ω_p** and **the spectral significant height H_m0 = 4√m₀**, m₀ the spectrum integrated over [0.3, 6] ω_p (`gfx::swash_wave`); the test holds H_m0 within a fifth of the fetch-limited growth law of the Coastal Engineering Manual (II-2), g H_m0 / U² = 1.6 × 10⁻³ (gF/U²)^½, and T within 1% of the law's own. **5 m/s over 2 km: H = 0.131 m, T = 1.34 s** (the growth law 0.114 m); **10 m/s over 20 km: H = 0.87 m, T = 3.64 s** (the law 0.72 m): the spectrum's variance runs about a seventh to a fifth above the law's, inside the scatter of the fetch-limited data. A closed body's fetch is the smaller of `fetch_km` and its own longest reach (pinned: the diagonal of its depth map's square, which bounds every straight line over its water); an open body's is `fetch_km`.

### The run-up

Stockdon et al. (2006, eq. 19), already used above for the foam's reach: R₂ = 1.1 (0.35 β (H₀L₀)^½ + ½ [H₀L₀ (0.563 β² + 0.004)]^½), L₀ = gT²/2π. Worked by hand and pinned in `swash_tests.cpp`: a lake's 0.1 m, 1.5 s wave on 1:10 runs up **5.48 cm**; an ocean's 1 m, 10 s wave on a 0.08 foreshore **0.984 m**. β is the foreshore slope at the waterline, from the signed depth map (the mean slope across every texel pair the waterline crosses, kept to [0.01, 0.5]). R₂ is the 2% exceedance, so it is taken as the **largest** wave of a group's reach, not the mean's.

### The front's motion

**Ballistic swash.** After a bore collapses at the shoreline the front runs up the beach face as a projectile decelerated by gravity along the slope and returns accelerating: the shoreline's position is x_s(t) = u₀t − ½ g sin β t² (Shen and Meyer 1963; Hughes 1992 on a sandy beach; Baldock and Holmes 1999), a parabola in time. Friction and infiltration make the backwash longer than the uprush on real beaches. Pinned: **the uprush is the first 0.4 of the period** (`k_swash_uprush`; a choice inside the asymmetry the field measurements show), both halves parabolic, so in the wave's own phase u the profile is 4s(1 − s) with s = u/2q on the uprush and ½ + (u − q)/2(1 − q) after; the front runs from **0.2 R below the still level** (`k_swash_rundown`, the backwash's rundown) up to the wave's reach and back. Every wave starts and ends at the rundown, so the profile is continuous whatever two neighbouring waves reach. The swash period is the wave's: on a reflective lake beach the swash is saturated by the incident wave, one sheet a wave.

**Groups.** Waves come in groups (every few waves larger): pinned **seven waves a group** (`k_swash_group`), the reach of wave j of the group R · (0.55 + 0.45 (½ − ½ cos 2π(j + ½)/7)), 0.57 R at the group's ends and R in its middle. Periodic in the wave's index, which is what makes the last crossing a closed form (below). **Along the shore** the reach is jittered by value noise of the body-local point into [0.7, 1.3] and the phase drifts by up to 1.5 waves over the larger of 4 m and three swash widths, so the front is a ragged curve and never a straight line.

**On the water's clock.** The resolve's water clock wraps every 1,024 s, so T is rounded to make a whole number of groups fit (2.004 s for a 2 s wave: 73 groups), and the profile has no jump at the wrap (`swash_tests.cpp` holds it periodic to 10⁻⁹).

### When the front last passed (the derivation)

For ground at height h above the still level and the place's phase c = t/T + φ, wave k = ⌊c⌋ and u = c − k: wave k's front is at or above h where z_lo + (A_k − z_lo) · 4s(1 − s) ≥ h, i.e. 4s(1 − s) ≥ c_k = (h − z_lo)/(A_k − z_lo). If c_k > 1 the wave never reaches h. Otherwise with r = √(1 − c_k), s ∈ [(1 − r)/2, (1 + r)/2], and mapped back through the two halves, **u ∈ [u₁, u₂] = [(1 − r) q, q + r (1 − q)]**. The front last stood at h at the end of the most recent such interval:

- if wave k has reached h (u ≥ u₁): under the sheet while u ≤ u₂ (age 0), else **age = (u − u₂) T**;
- else the most recent earlier wave k − j (j ≥ 1) with c_{k−j} ≤ 1: **age = (u + j − u₂(k − j)) T**.

The reach repeats every seven waves, so waves k − 1 … k − 7 hold every reach there is: the search is at most seven, and a height none of them reaches was never reached (age ∞). The wetness above the fringe is then exp(−age / τ). Nothing is stored and every frame is a function of the clock. **Checked** (`swash_tests.cpp`): at a thousand random (t, h) the closed form against a brute-force scan of the profile backwards in steps of a 4,000th of a period over two groups, every one within a step, with some under the sheet, most drying and some never reached.

### How long the sand stays wet

The film a sheet leaves on sand drains into it fast: Turner and Masselink (1998) measured vertical flows of the order of 10⁻³ m/s through a beach face under individual swashes, so a few millimetres of film go in **seconds** — the gloss (pinned `k_swash_film_s` = 3 s). The sand under it stays near saturation much longer, draining to the damp of the capillary fringe over **tens of seconds** on sand: pinned **`drain_s` = 20 s** (a choice within the brief's "tens of seconds"; gravel drains in a few, silt and clay hold water for minutes, and the field is the body's). The darkening is Lekner and Dorf's as above, at the wetness the clock gives.

**The fringe under a swash is damp, not filmed.** Version 4's band darkened by the full factor at the waterline because it stood for the swash's few centimetres too. With the swash drawn, the band above the highest sheet is the capillary fringe alone, whose surface is tension-saturated grains with no free film: pinned **half the saturated factor** (`k_swash_fringe`, a choice: Lekner and Dorf's relation is for a film), smoothstepping out at `wet_m`. Without that, a 0.3 m fringe would cover a lake's few-centimetre swash at full darkness and the drying the owner described would not show. A body with no wind and no tide keeps the full band.

### Tides

**M2**, the principal lunar semidiurnal constituent, the largest on most coasts: period 12.4206012 h (its speed 28.984° an hour; Doodson 1921). An open body's level is level + tide_m sin(2π t / T_M2) on the world clock (the phase computed in double on the CPU). One constituent, so no spring–neap cycle (S2 would add it) and no diurnal inequality; a scene that wants a particular stage of the tide at its start sets its world time.

### Where the research and the budget were traded

The mesh is not extended above the still level (a flat surface there is behind the ground and never drawn); the sheet over the bank is drawn on the ground's pixels as saturated sand with the water's Fresnel reflection of the sky and the foam, not as a volume: a few centimetres of sheet are what the eye sees as a dark glossy film, and a tide of metres over the bank is the same with no depth colour — the limit is on [water](../subsystems/water.md#the-swash). The film's gloss is a reflection added after the lighting, not a lowered roughness of the terrain material (S99). The per-pixel cost is a search of up to eight waves (a square root each) on the pixels of ground in the swash band and on the water within its band of the shore.
## Wet ground

Roadmap S99 (2026-11-08): the darkening, the lowered roughness and the water film, from the saturation, before the lighting ([terrain](../subsystems/terrain.md#wet-ground), [water](../subsystems/water.md#the-swash)). What the owner's eight frames of a real beach show, and what each piece of the model answers: nearest the water a saturated sand that reflects the sky and the light like a thin film (the film); above it a band darker than dry sand but matte, its upper edge ragged and receding (the darkening, with the roughness barely moved, and S94's clock); the dry sand pale and rough; the film-to-damp boundary sharp, the damp-to-dry one diffuse.

**The darkening: Lekner and Dorf (1988).** A rough surface wetted keeps a water layer over its grains; light that leaves a grain is partly reflected back into the ground at the water's upper surface, totally beyond the critical angle, and the grain absorbs some of it again. Averaged over a diffuse field the wet albedo is a′ = (1 − r_e)(1 − r_i) a / (1 − r_i a), r_e = 0.066 water's external diffuse reflectance and r_i = 1 − (1 − r_e)/n² = 0.4744 at n = 1.333 (the section above has the factor). Pinned as `wet_ground.slang`'s two constants, the same as R99's beach and S97's band; the double reference computes them from r_e and n.

**Its weight: porosity.** Lekner and Dorf's picture needs pores to hold the water; Jensen, Legakis and Dorsey (1999, "Rendering wet materials", EGWR) separate water *in* a material (darker by the same internal reflection, which they simulate) from water *on* it (a dielectric layer, glossier, little darker). Pinned: the darkening's weight is *s* × porosity / 0.3, at most 1 — 0.3 the low end of a sand's porosity (0.3–0.5), so any sand or soil darkens fully at saturation and a rock of porosity 0.05 a sixth as much (it mostly films). `TerrainLayer.porosity` (default 0.5; the v35 pack's meadow 0.55) is the input; it was already read by Lagarde's rain rule.

**The roughness: measured and chosen.** Zhang and Voss (2006, *Applied Optics* 45(34), bidirectional reflectance of dry, wet and submerged particulate layers) measured a strong specular peak on wet layers at large illumination zenith angles that the dry layers lack, and darkening consistent with Lekner and Dorf; Zhang, Voss and Lewis (2002) found the peak "not obvious" on a wetted powder whose morphology stayed the dry one's — the peak is the film's, not the wet grains'. Nolet et al. (2014) fit beach sand's spectral reflectance against surface moisture up to about 24% volumetric with a physical optical model. **No measured roughness of damp sand as a function of saturation was found**, so the curve is stated: the damp substrate's roughness falls to 0.7 of its own at *s* = 1 (gentler than Lagarde 2013's porous rule, which takes up to 0.4 off, because the owner's frames show the damp band matte), and the film takes it to 0.05 by the film's weight. The film's weight is the larger of S94's film term (exp(−age/3 s), Turner and Masselink 1998's infiltration through a millimetre film) and the free water standing once the pores are past 85% full, ((s − 0.85)/0.15)², so the film-to-damp boundary is as sharp as the saturation and the damp band never shines. The film's Fresnel is water's, F0 0.0201 (n = 1.333), for the sky reflected along the mirrored normal; the BSDF's direct lobe stays at its single dielectric F0 0.04 (Lagarde, who lerps F0 towards water's 0.02 for a flooded surface, had a second F0 to lerp; `brdf.slang` does not, and the path tracer shares it).

**The capillary fringe above a still waterline, by grain** (the channels' banks). The ranges above (Silin-Bekchurin) and the laboratory columns of Lane and Washburn (1946) as Lohman (1972, USGS Professional Paper 708) and Fetter tabulate them, at a porosity near 41% after 72 days: fine gravel 2.5 cm, coarse sand 13.5, medium sand 24.6, fine sand 42.8, silt 105.5 (`gfx::k_capillary_fringe_m`). Each lies inside the Silin-Bekchurin range for its class and the coarse sand's matches the GSA columns' 13.5 cm; **the USGS paper itself refused the fetch (HTTP 403), so the five values were checked against the ranges and the one column, not against Lohman's page**, said here so the next reader checks them there. A channel's description names no bank material, so its banks take `k_bank_default`, fine sand's 0.428 m — a loam's rise lies between fine sand's and silt's, and the lower bound keeps a forest stream's damp strip to tens of centimetres of height. The saturation falls over the fringe by a smoothstep (1 at the water, 0 at the top, steepest midway): the shape of van Genuchten's retention curve, not its parameters, which would need each soil's (Wösten et al. 2001 tabulate them; FAO's Irrigation and Drainage Paper 56 Annex 5 shows one silt's profile over a metre).

**The spray band** over a rapid (0.25 m) and a fall (half the drop, at most 3 m) is a stated choice: no measured reach of a small fall's spray onto its banks was found in the time spent.

**Bared ground** (the swash's backwash, a falling tide) is now the ground's own material at the clock's saturation (its terrain material, its plain albedo over porosity 0.5, or the low tier's shallow tint), not a fixed sand of albedo 0.35.
**A bank is damp, never flooded** (2026-11-08, after the first v35 captures). The fringe gives saturation 1 at and below the stage, and the film's rule then laid a standing film wherever a bank's ground lay at or below the channel's surface: on v35's stream that was the whole floodplain beside it out to the reach's 26 m, every pebble of the gravel normal map glinting in the sun. A capillary fringe holds water in the pores, not on them, so a bank's saturation is now held at 0.85, the film's threshold (the swash keeps its film, and the channel's surface is water), and a point's height is taken as at least its run from the wetted edge over 25, the reach's slope, so the damp falls to nothing at the reach's edge on flat ground instead of stopping there.

## The wave as height

Roadmap S100 (the owner, 2026-11-08: the sea's swash "far too rapid and chaotic (and even forms loops sometimes instead of a steady wave), and there's no height change as the waves come in"; on the lake "a hard cutoff"). The code is [water](../subsystems/water.md#the-mesh); this section is the diagnosis, the models and the trades.

### The diagnosis

On the forest proof of concept's v36 the open `eastern-sea` has `wind_m_s` 7 and no `fetch_km`, so it takes the default 100 km: JONSWAP's peak is ω_p = 22 (g²/U₁₀F)^⅓ = 1.135 rad/s, **a 5.5 s period** (5.53 s, 5.63 s on the clock's whole groups) and H_m0 about 1.3 m. Of the four suspects:

- **(b) is the loops.** S94 gave each place a phase drifting by up to 1.5 waves over 4 m along the shore (`water_swash_place`, value noise) and a reach jittered in [0.7, 1.3] per place, so neighbouring places 4 m apart were up to one and a half waves out of step: independent oscillators, not a propagating wave. The front is the level set z_front(p, t) = ground(p); with the phase a function of place, that set folds back on itself wherever the phase gradient along the shore outruns the slope — the **loops**. A front that is a function of place with an independent phase cannot be a wave.
- **(a) and (b) together are the chaos and the rapidity.** 5.5 s is a fetch-limited wind sea's period, where a real ocean coast's swash follows the swell's 8–12 s; and with the phase drifting along the beach every few metres reach their top at a different instant, so the eye sees a flicker of independent sheets several times a period rather than one sheet a period. The period itself is the conditions' (a 7 m/s wind over 100 km); the chaos was the model's.
- **(c)** deep-water celerity at the shore made the four sines and the spectrum's crests run in at √(gλ/2π) to the waterline, where a real wave slows to √(gd) and its crests bunch.
- **(d) is "no height change".** The mesh was flat and the sheet a colour on the ground's pixels: nothing rose as the wave came in, and on the lake the flat surface met the ground at the still level's contour — the hard cutoff.

### The models

- **Dispersion at depth**: ω² = g k tanh(k d). Solved by Fenton and McKee's (1990) explicit k d = k₀d · coth((k₀d)^¾)^⅔ (exact in the deep and shallow limits; one Newton step after it still left 1.5 × 10⁻⁴ of ω² here, measured), then two Newton steps on k d tanh(k d) = k₀d: the residual of ω² is under 4 × 10⁻⁹ over T 1.5–10 s and d 2 cm–500 m (`water_tests.cpp`).
- **Shoaling**: energy flux conserved, H/H₀ = √(c_g0/c_g) = (2 n tanh k d)^−½ with n = ½(1 + 2kd/sinh 2kd) (linear theory; Dean and Dalrymple 1991), which in shallow water is **Green's law** (1838), H ~ d^−¼: H/H₀ → (4 k₀ d)^−¼, held to a percent at k₀d = 10⁻³ and 10⁻⁴.
- **Breaking**: the shoaled height is capped at **H = γ d, γ = 0.78** — McCowan's (1894) limit for a solitary wave on a level bed (Yamada et al. 1968 revised it to 0.83; 0.78 stays the engineering first estimate), the constant Battjes and Janssen (1978) take as the breaker index for a random sea's highest waves — and in the surf zone the broken bore's height is held at γ d to the shore (the saturated surf zone). A 1 m, 8 s wave breaks in 1.64 m of water, 1.17 m high half as deep again offshore (`water_tests.cpp`).
- **The crest's shape**: H (((1 + cos θ)/2)^m − its mean), m = 1 + 2b with b the share of the breaking height reached, so the crest sharpens and the trough flattens towards breaking (a stand-in for the cnoidal and solitary shapes of shallow water; the mean is Γ(m + ½)/(√π Γ(m + 1)), within a percent of 1/√(π(m + ¼))).
- **The arrival field**: the train's phase at a place is the time its crest takes to get there from deep water — the eikonal |∇τ| = 1/c(d), c = ω/k(d) the local phase speed, solved on the depth map's grid at load by fast sweeping (Zhao 2005; Godunov's upwind update, four sweep orders, three rounds), from every texel at least 1/k₀ deep (or the deepest fifth). Rays bend towards the shallows, so the crests come in along the depth's contours as real refracted swell does; across the bank the speed is 10⁴ m/s, so a place on the beach takes its shoreline's arrival — the swash's phase. On a straight 1:20 beach two places a metre apart across the shore differ by the integral of 1/c between them to 0.95%, and along it by nothing (`water_tests.cpp`).
- **The swash on the train**: S94's ballistic profile, group of seven and Stockdon's R₂ unchanged; the place's phase is now −τ/T, and the reach's along-shore variation is a smooth [0.85, 1.15] over two deep-water peak wavelengths (the crest's length under a spread sea) times the shore's facing (a closed body's waves face the shore its wind blows on: a quarter on a shore it blows away from, whole where it blows straight in). The closed-form age holds as it was.

### Where the research and the budget were traded

One train, the peak's, carries the near-shore height: a random sea's spectrum shoals and breaks wave by wave, and the visible breakers are its highest third, so the peak at H_m0 is what is drawn (the offshore spectrum crosses over to it between k₀d 1 and 0.35). The arrival field is first-order (the destination texel's slowness), about 1% of the travel time on the test beach at a quarter-metre grid. The crest's shape is a sharpened cosine, not a solved cnoidal or solitary profile, and the displacement is vertical only (no forward lean before the break, no plunging lip). The facing factor's gradient and an open body's outline fade's are left out of the normal. The bank's regime is the rim's ring spacing (eight metres on the eastern sea's 65,536-vertex mesh), so the sheet's edge is a triangle's, not a pixel's, and the ground pixels' swash shading (S94's, on the train) fills between.

## Stage B: waves that read as water

Roadmap S100, stage B (2026-11-10; the owner after stage A: "waves still look like they need a lot of work"). What stage A's own sheets and the forest's maintainer's list showed, and what each was: a closed body's waves were **four sines** (corduroy at a low `chop`, a diamond lattice at 0.65, regular at any setting; beside the reel `lake-wind-ripples` the real wavelets are decimetres long, steep, from several directions, and never repeat); a windy lake's shallows drew **faceted rings**, crests as concentric polygons with corners a few metres a side, and the swash's front zigzagged in triangles; the sea's uprush ended in a **white band a metre wide with a straight-segmented kerb** at its front; the foam was one uniform band, and no crest broke. The code is [water](../subsystems/water.md#the-mesh) ("The mesh", "The swash", "The spectrum"); this section is the models, their constants and the trades, in the order the work was done.

### The spectrum for every body

**One model.** Every body with a wind draws the fetch-limited JONSWAP table the open sea already drew ([the ocean-surface page](ocean-surface.md#the-published-models)): sixteen bands over [0.5, 4] ω_p, Donelan–Banner spreading. What differs between bodies is the fetch. An open body's is its `fetch_km`. **A closed body's is its own reach along its wind**: the longest run of its own water the wind crosses, found on its depth map by solving w · ∇F = 1 over water from F = 0 where the wind leaves the land (`gfx::water_fetch_field`: a first-order upwind difference swept once in the wind's order; over land the value is carried on with an e-folding of 20 m, so a beach's texels hold the fetch of the water at their foot and an island casts a lee), and no longer than `fetch_km`. Stage A took the diagonal of the map's square, which for a lake blown across its short side is the wrong water.

**A sea stops growing.** The fetch laws have no upper end, and stage A's note asked for one: a wind sea is fully developed where the dimensionless fetch gF/U² reaches about **2.2 × 10⁴** — where JONSWAP's peak law f_p U/g = 3.5 (gF/U²)^−0.33 meets Pierson and Moskowitz's fully developed peak, f_p U/g = 0.13 — so `jonswap_peak` and `jonswap_alpha` take the smaller of the fetch and 2.2 × 10⁴ U²/g: 110 km at 7 m/s (a 5.7 s peak), 224 km at 10 m/s. A longer period on a coast is swell, not a longer wind sea; a swell is a body field of its own, which this stage did not add (below).

**The lake, on paper, against the reel.** The forest's lake is about 0.9 km along its 7 m/s wind. The growth law of the Coastal Engineering Manual (II-2), g H/U² = 1.6 × 10⁻³ (gF/U²)^½, gives **H_m0 = 0.107 m**, and JONSWAP's peak law **T_p = 1.15 s**, a peak wavelength of **2.07 m**; the table's sixteen bands hold H_m0 = 0.113 m (`water_shading_tests.cpp`, "a lake's wind sea": within 6% of the law, the period within 0.1%) and run from 8.3 m down to **13 cm**. The Phillips constant at that fetch is α = 0.076 (U²/Fg)^0.22 = 0.024, three times a fully developed sea's 0.0081 — a young sea is steep — and the tail's mean square slope between the peak and 4 ω_p is α ln 4 = 0.034, against Cox and Munk's (1954) 0.003 + 0.00512 U = 0.039 for the whole surface at 7 m/s: **the table's bands carry nearly all the slope a 7 m/s surface has**, an rms slope of 0.19 (11°). That is the reel's picture: its wavelets are the bands of 13 to 50 cm, steep enough that one face mirrors the sky and the other does not, riding a 2 m peak wave 11 cm high. The four sines at the scene's 5 m and 3.5 cm had a twentieth of that slope, which is "low parallel bands at a longer wavelength, low contrast".

**Every wave is a narrow band, not a line.** Sixteen sines over three octaves of frequency are two or three to an octave of wavelength, and two or three sines crossing are a lattice: the diamond pattern again, at every scale at once. A band of a spectrum is not a sine: it is a carrier at the band's centre under a slowly varying complex envelope (Rice 1944 for narrow-band noise; Longuet-Higgins 1952 for the sea, where the envelope's Rayleigh distribution is the distribution of wave heights). So each of the sixteen is drawn as a (e₁ sin θ + e₂ cos θ), (e₁, e₂) two smooth value noises over cells **four wavelengths long** the way the wave travels and **2.5 wide** along its crest, travelling at the deep-water group speed, half the phase speed (`water_wave_add` in `water_waves.slang`; `gfx::k_water_group_along` and its neighbours). Four wavelengths is the table's own bandwidth: neighbouring bands are 1.30 apart in wavenumber, whose beat is 1/0.26 = 3.8 wavelengths, so the groups are what the bands left out between them would have made. The crest's 2.5 wavelengths is short-crestedness, the directional spread inside a band. The envelope's gain, √(3/2) · 35/26 = 1.649, makes each component's variance ½ (value noise over a smoothstep lattice keeps (26/35)² of its nodes' variance of ⅓), so a band keeps the variance a²/2 of the sine it replaces: over a 60 m patch at eight clocks the bands' variance is within 2% of the sines' (0.000821 against 0.000804 m², the same test). Nothing repeats: one band against itself forty wavelengths on correlates at 0.003, where a sine is itself again. **On the clock**: the envelope moves a whole number of cells a clock period (the wave's cycles over eight, rounded) over a lattice periodic in that number, so the water's 1,024 s clock wraps with no jump (the test holds the sum at 0 s and 1,024 s to 10⁻⁹ m). The envelope's own gradient is in the normal (a twentieth of the carrier's), so the normal is still the height's gradient (held by a central difference to 2 × 10⁻⁴).

**The lee.** A closed body's field carries, beside the depth and the arrival, `grown`: the share of the body's fetch the wind has blown over to reach the texel. Where the sea is younger than the table's, the local peak is at ω_p · grown^(−1/3) and below it JONSWAP falls as exp(−(5/4)(ω_p′/ω)⁴), so each wave's amplitude there is its own times exp(−(5/8)(ω_p/ω)⁴ (grown^(−4/3) − 1)) (`gfx::water_grown_share`): the long waves go first, and at a tenth of the fetch the peak is under 1% and the shortest band over 90%. The shore the wind blows from is ripples and then glass; the shore it blows to has the whole sea. The train's height and the swash's reach take the growth law's H ~ F^½ (`water_shelter`), which replaces stage A's facing factor (a quarter on a shore the wind blows away from, by the depth's gradient): the fetch is the physical quantity the facing stood in for. Left out: the Phillips constant's slow rise towards the lee (a factor grown^−0.11 in amplitude) and the shift of the peak enhancement.

**What the mesh carries, and why the facets keep their contrast.** The lift is given the mesh's spacing as its footprint: a wave shorter than twice the spacing is left out of the displacement and one of four spacings is whole, exactly the rule a pixel's footprint applies to the normal. On the forest's v40 the 65,536-vertex budget puts the lake's vertices 4.97 m apart and the sea's 22.7 m (the depth maps' headers, written by the first run: texels of 1.24 m and 5.68 m), so **the lake's sea is below the mesh, all of it**, and so is nearly all of the sea's offshore — its 48 m peak is a twentieth kept — while the rings graded from 0.40 m and 1.0 m at the outlines carry the trains in to where they break. That is the right split: displacement the mesh cannot carry is aliasing — each vertex takes one sample of a wave it cannot draw, which is where the rings of facets came from — and the reel's density of wavelets is a shading problem. The shading keeps the facets' contrast because the normal is evaluated per pixel from the whole table: at 1.7 m of eye height a pixel ten metres out sees the surface at 10°, where a slope of ±11° swings the mirrored ray from the bright sky overhead to the dark far shore, and Fresnel's reflectance from 0.9 to 0.1. **What a pixel cannot hold** (a far lake, where the footprint takes the short bands out of the normal). The first forest captures showed what leaving it out does: thirty metres off, the lake under 7 m/s was a mirror with the far shore's trees standing in it, and the sea a grey sheet — the footprint is measured on the level surface, where real waves turn their faces to the eye, so the picture lost every band shorter than a few pixels and with them the roughness that makes windy water dark and streaked. So 70% of the slope variance the footprint left out comes back **at the pixel's own scale**: a band-limited ripple three footprints long, two octaves of the envelope's lattice cross-faded as the footprint grows, travelling down the wind at that wavelength's speed (`water_micro_normal`, `k_water_micro_share`). It is detail at the wrong wavelength with the right slope — the facets a pixel averages cannot be resolved, their statistics can — and at a grazing view its cells are foreshortened into the horizontal streaks far wavelets make. The other 30% is added to the GGX lobe's α² (a Beckmann surface's α² is its mean square slope), so the sun's glitter on far water is as wide as the wavelets are steep instead of a mirror's point (`water_roughness`). The share is a stated choice.

### The shallows

**Why the crests turned corners: three causes, counted.**

1. **The field was read bilinearly.** A crest is a level line of the arrival field, and a bilinear read's gradient jumps at every texel's border. Walked round a bay's 0.2 m depth contour in millimetre steps (`water_shading_tests.cpp`, "a crest that crosses a bay's depth contours turns no corner": a round bay shelving 1:15 on a metre grid), the bilinear read's crest direction jumps at **203 places in 170 m — one a texel border — by up to 17.5°**. Those are the polygons: on the forest's lake the texels are 1.24 m and on its sea 5.68 m, "corners a few metres a side".
2. **The mesh was given waves it cannot draw.** The lake's train breaks 14 cm deep with a wavelength of T√(g d) = 1.35 m; the lake's rings stood 4.97 m apart. A quarter of a vertex a wavelength is not a crest, it is one random sample a vertex, in rings, because the mesh is rings.
3. **The uncovered bank was parked at once.** A rim vertex the front did not cover went 5 cm under the ground whatever its neighbour did, so the triangle between a covered vertex and a parked one stood up as a ramp with a straight edge: the kerb, and the zigzag.

**The fixes, each by derivation.**

- **The field is read C1**: a quadratic B-spline over the nine texels round the point (`water_field_at`), whose gradient is continuous, so a level line has a tangent everywhere. The same walk turns by at most 0.018° a millimetre, under the bound a C1 field allows — four times the field's largest second difference over its smallest gradient on the ring, times the step, 0.19° — and by no jump: **0 corners**. No tolerance was widened: the test is the count against a bound computed from the field. The spline smooths as well as interpolates (a texel's value is ⅛ + ¾ + ⅛ of its row), which moves the field's waterline by the depth's curvature over a texel squared, millimetres on a beach; the resolve's own depth read (the map's image, bilinear) is unchanged.
- **The rings are closer at the outline.** A crest coming in runs along the rings, so what it needs is rings close together, not more vertices a ring. A body with a wind has its rings `near_spacing` apart at the outline — a quarter of the bore's wavelength where the wind's wave breaks, T√(g H/0.78)/4, within [0.25 m, the spacing the body asked for] — each gap 1.15 times the one outside it until it is the budget's spacing, inwards and over the rim alike (`water::Mesh::near_spacing` and `grade`; the depth map's file, version 3, carries the three numbers to the renderer). A geometric series: a few rings more, no vertex more a ring.
- **The lift holds each wave against the mesh it is on.** For the table's waves, the far spacing. For the train, the spacing as the train crosses it: the rings' along the ray from the centre and the ring's own across it, so a crest that runs along the rings is carried down to four ring gaps a wavelength and one that crosses them obliquely is left to the normal before it can alias (`water_height`'s `mesh`). What the mesh does not carry is still shaded: the normal and the foam are the same function per pixel.
- **The sheet's surface goes on under the ground.** Past the front the surface continues at the slope it had — half the front's deficit, down to the park's 5 cm — so the mesh meets the ground **along the front itself**, at a grazing angle, and not along a triangle's edge (`water_sheet_surface`). The float and double evaluations now agree everywhere, and the pool test compares every vertex (it left out those within a centimetre of the front).

### The break and the foam

**The break.** The train's height is capped at the breaker index, H = 0.78 d (McCowan 1894), where stage A put it; what is new is that each wave of the group is asked on its own. Wave k carries the group's shape G(k) (0.57 to 1), so it breaks where G(k) H Ks(d) = 0.78 d: the group's larger waves further out. Seaward of the largest wave's break line nothing is foam.

**Whether a wave foams at all.** Two published thresholds. A wave too small entrains no air: whitecaps start with the first breaking wavelets, Beaufort 3 (3.4 to 5.4 m/s; the scale's own description), so the foam is none under H = 2 cm and whole from 6 cm (`k_water_foam_min_height`, `k_water_foam_full_height`: a stated ramp between the scale's "glassy" and "scattered whitecaps", not a measured curve). And a wave may run up without breaking: Battjes (1974) sorts breakers by the surf similarity (Iribarren) number ξ = tan β / √(H₀/L₀) — spilling under 0.5, plunging to 3.3, **surging or collapsing past 3.3**, where the wave slides up the beach whole — so the foam fades from ξ = 2 to none at 3.3. The lake above on a 1:10 shore has ξ = 0.44: its wavelets are steep and spill. A 3 cm ripple on the same shore foams a sixth as much; a long low swell on a steep shingle bank, not at all.

**The foam is a function of the clock, and no stamp map was built.** Stage A's plan for this stage kept a stamp per texel of when the sheet last covered it, written by a pass each frame, and said to say why if time alone could not give it. It can. The train is periodic in its group of seven, so "which waves passed this place broken, and how long ago" is a sum over the last waves, each a closed form of the clock and the place: wave k − j's crest passed (u + j) periods ago, u the train's phase here; it had broken by here if G(k − j) H Ks(d) ≥ 0.78 d; its foam has decayed by exp(−(u + j) T/τ). Up the beach the same sum starts from the moment each wave's front passed the height, u₁ — the root of the ballistic profile the wet sand's age is already taken from. Three waves back is e⁻⁴ of a 5.6 s sea's foam; a lake's second-long waves overlap and the older are under the newer. State would be needed for foam that is not a function of the train — a wake, a wave off a rock, foam carried by a current — and none of those is drawn yet; the wet sand's age stays S94's closed form for the same reason. The world clock decides everything, so a replay draws the same foam.

**The decay, and how much.** An e-folding of **4 s** for a metre of surf (`k_water_foam_decay_s`): Callaghan, Deane, Stokes and Ward (2012) measured individual whitecaps' foam decaying in 0.2 to 10.4 s and area-weighted means of 1.4 to 4.8 s; Callaghan (2013) a whole-whitecap timescale of 5.3 s. Their spread is the breakers' scale — a small plume's bubbles are shallow and gone in a second — so the e-folding is 4 s · √(H / 1 m), within [1, 4] s, and the density a wave leaves is H / 0.5 m, whole from half a metre (`water_foam_decay`, `water_foam_amount`: stated shapes between the measurements' ends, not fits). The first forest captures had neither and drew the lake's 11 cm wavelets, a second apart, as a solid white band along the whole shore: every wave left a whole density that had lost a quarter by the next. With them the lake's shore is flecks (a fifth of a patch, gone in 1.3 s) and the sea's a raft.

**Decaying is thinning, not fading.** A patch of foam does not go grey; it is eaten into a lace and then into streaks as the bubbles drain. The foam's pattern is two octaves of ridged value noise (near 0 along the ridges), a pixel is foam where the pattern is under the density, and so as the density decays the open cells go first and the ridges last (`water_foam_lace`, `water_foam_cover`). Each wave leaves its own pattern (its index in the group seeds it), so successive bores do not dissolve into the same shapes. The cell is 0.6 H, 6 to 60 cm, with an octave a third of it under it: a lake's flecks, a sea's rafts (1.5 H, the first choice, drew the sea's foam as camouflage blobs; and the pattern's values crowd towards its ridges, so the threshold a density sets rises slowly at first, D (0.45 + 0.55 D³), or a quarter-dense patch is four tenths white). A pattern finer than the pixel is its mean. The albedo is S97's 0.55 (Koepke 1984).

**The roller, the front's line and what is left.** On the water the wave coming in carries a roller on its face (the last tenth of the phase before the crest, where it has broken), and the foam it leaves starts whole at the crest. On the beach the collapsed bore's foam is the front's **thin line** — half a wave height wide, 3 to 25 cm, strongest as the front sets off and gone as it stalls at its top, and never on the way back — and behind it the lace the front left where it passed, less of it the higher it was left (the foam a bore carries thins as it climbs). Stage A's band a quarter of `foam_m` deep with a 35% trail is gone, and with it `foam_m`'s part in a windy body's foam: the reach is the swash's own.

**The waterline is the front's, per pixel, and the water reflects to its edge.** The mesh's sheet is its vertices'; the front is evaluated at every pixel. A water pixel whose own front has not reached its ground is bared ground, as the ground's pixels beside it are filmed sand where the front has passed them, so the line between water and beach is the front's and not a triangle's. And a lifted body no longer fades its reflection out over `shore_m` from the waterline: that fade hid a flat mesh's hard line against dry ground, and beside a film of wet sand — which mirrors the sky — it drew the water's edge as a dark band with no sky in it (stage A's fifth complaint, "tinted glass"). Fresnel alone decides: looking down, thin water shows its bed; along the surface, it mirrors to the last centimetre, as the reels' lake edges do (r05's pebble shore).

**The front is scalloped.** Its reach varies along the shore by a tenth either way over about half the swash's width (0.6 R/β, 0.5 to 6 m) and half that again at a quarter of the scale, on top of stage A's ±15% over two peak wavelengths (`water_reach_variation`'s `lobes`). The lobes stand where they are from wave to wave, as a beach's cusps do, which keeps the wet sand's closed-form age exact (the reach of every wave of the group at a place is one number times the group's shape). They are a pixel's: the mesh's sheet takes the smooth variation alone, and where the pixel's front lies inside the mesh's the water past it is millimetres deep.

**The period's levers.** `fetch_km` up to full development, as above. **A swell was not added**: an open body's `swell_height_m` and `swell_period_s`, a train of their own that shoals and breaks in place of the wind sea's peak, is the lever for a coast's 8 to 12 s sets; the surface record has the word for it (`gfx::WaterSurface::swell`) and nothing sets it. It is a schema change to the scene's water block and a second table offshore, and it was left for a row of its own rather than half done here.

Three things the 4K frames show that are defects and not limits: **a dark rim along the sea's waterline** (a water pixel the front has bared draws the bed darker than the filmed sand the ground's own pixels draw beside it, so the two meet in a brown line); **the shallows inshore of the break are a flat sheet** (the table's wavelets are weighted out where the train takes over, and the train has no ripple of its own, so a hundred metres of knee-deep water has no texture but its foam); and **the lake's wet margin shows nested curves**, the depth map's contours, where the bared and the thin water meet a texel at a time (the resolve's own depth read is still bilinear; the field's is not).

### Where the research and the budget were traded (stage B)

One train still carries the near-shore height, and its crest is a sharpened cosine with no forward lean and no plunging lip: the break is drawn by its foam and its height, not by a jet. The foam does not move with the water under it (no drift with the wind, no slosh with the orbital motion): it is born, sits, and thins. The roller is a band of the phase, not a volume. The lobes do not change from wave to wave. A body that fills still grows no rim and no graded rings (its lattice is uniform). The rings are graded from the outline, which is the author's polygon: where the waterline lies well inside it the fine rings are in the wrong place and the train is left to the normal. The envelope is value noise, whose lattice is square in the wave's own frame; under one band it would show, under sixteen it has not.
