# E33: hard-surface assets — a procedural kit in Blender against image-to-3D

- **Question ([10 §10.5](../plan/10-roadmap-risks.md#105-experiments-to-run-before-committing), E33):** are hard-surface assets — Desert Survival's ruined brick walls, later Island City's buildings — better served by a procedural kit an agent builds in Blender and textures from CC0 or generated PBR sets than by image-to-3D (Tripo, Meshy, TRELLIS), whose outputs the owner found smoothed in geometry and texture? And, beside it, the owner's working rule recorded in [07 §7.7](../plan/07-content-pipeline.md#77-ml-asset-generation-novelty-content) (direction note, 2026-09-24): the services do well up to about person scale and struggle above it, because a generation spends a fixed detail budget on the whole object — so is the softness a **size** effect, an **architecture** effect, or both?

## Setup

### The kit: `ruined_wall.py`

A ruined masonry wall generator in Blender's Python, one script with two profiles (below, the brick profile; then [the ashlar profile](#the-second-profile-desert-sandstone-ashlar)), run headlessly through [`tools/blender-run.ps1`](../content-generation.md#blender-toolsblender-runps1) (the wrapper is the one piece in the repository; the scripts live under `D:\workspace\game_engine_local\blender-kits\ruined-wall\scripts\`, because the repository takes no Python). Every choice is drawn from a PCG64 stream named by the seed and by what it is for, so a draw added in one place moves nothing elsewhere, and noise is a hashed integer lattice in numpy rather than Blender's.

- **Kit pieces.** UK bricks, 215 × 102.5 × 65 mm, as real geometry: a grid box whose outer band is projected onto a **4 mm chamfer** (an L1 projection onto the inner box, so an edge vertex lands mid-chamfer and the strips are planar), in eight variants × four seeded sub-variants — whole (156 triangles), worn (10 mm chamfer, three times the surface noise), one chipped corner (180), two (204), split (two pieces and a rough crack, 268), spalled face (a noisy depression up to 11 mm deep on the face turned outward; a finer grid, 720), half bat and three-quarter bat with a fractured end. A fracture is a bisect of the piece, capped, poked twice and its inner vertices displaced by noise. Per brick: position jitter (1.5 mm along, 1.5 mm in depth, 4 mm for a loose brick at a ruin edge), rotation jitter (0.5°, 2° loose), which long face shows, its own offset into the displacement noise (1.6 mm at 8/m plus 0.6 mm at 30/m along each vertex's outward direction), and the three atlas cells its faces sample (the **tint index**: a cell carries one of four tints, because the engine reads no vertex colours).
- **Bond.** 75 mm courses (65 mm brick, 10 mm joint) in running, English or Flemish bond (a parameter; the measured assets are Flemish), two wythes, 215 mm thick, ends clipped to closers; the corner interlocks its two walls course by course (the corner square belongs to one wall on even courses and to the other on odd ones).
- **Ruin as a rule.** An erosion mask `m = 0.5 fbm(u, z) + 0.9 (z/H)^1.5 + 0.5 exp(-d_free/0.7) − 0.35 exp(-d_corner/1.0) [+ a breach] + 0.15 per-brick noise` removes every brick with `m` over `1/(erosion × preset)` (1.25 for the section, 1.1 for the 10 m wall, 1.3 for the corner), then a support pass from the ground up removes any brick with less than 35% of its length on a kept brick below. The 10 m wall's breach is a V-shaped term down to 0.42 m. Kept bricks with nothing above or a missing neighbour are "loose" and get more damage and jitter.
- **Debris.** 45% of the removed bricks fall. Two modes: `--debris rule` (a deterministic placement: side, distance 2 cm + Exp(0.2 m), spread growing with fall height, resting on a large face, piled on a 4 cm heightfield) and `--debris sim` (Blender's rigid-body simulation: each brick dropped from beside where it left the wall onto a passive ground and a box proxy of the kept bricks, 240 frames, 10 substeps, then baked to static geometry; a body that comes to rest more than 0.8 m clear of every wall rolled away and is dropped — 14% of them). **The simulation is deterministic here**: `--sim-check` runs it twice in one process and every asset's rest poses were identical (maximum difference 0.0), and two separate processes wrote byte-identical GLBs, as the rule mode does. The measured assets use **sim**; the rule is the one that is deterministic by construction on any machine and any Blender, which the simulation is not promised to be.
- **Mortar.** A box per brick cell (a run where the bond lets cells touch; in Flemish bond they rarely do), recessed 8 mm from both faces, lowered 6 mm below the brick tops where nothing sits above, and cut at the mortar texture's tile boundaries, because the engine's material sampler clamps UVs (below).
- **Export.** One mesh, two materials (brick atlas, mortar), glTF metallic-roughness, single-sided. Brick normals are one per vertex, weighted by the square of face area, so a flat face keeps its own normal and the chamfer carries the turn, with no split at an arris for the LOD to stop at; mortar is flat-shaded. **`_CANONICAL_ID`** is written on every point (the vertex index of the joined mesh, which the exporter copies to every UV and normal split of that point — the control-level name [geometry](../subsystems/geometry.md#canonical-vertex-identity) wants); Blender writes an integer attribute as FLOAT, which the importer takes (whole numbers below 2^24), and the content build reads it as `vertex_id_source: authored`. GLB, the `.blend` beside it, a `.stats.json`, and a provenance sidecar written by the wrapper.
- **Three sizes**, seed 7, Flemish bond: `section2m` (2.0 m × 28 courses), `wall10m` (10.0 m × 36 courses, one breach) and `corner` (4 m and 3 m walls × 40 courses, meeting at an interlocked corner). The "largest dimension" in the tables includes the settled rubble, which is why the 2 m section measures 3.3 m.

### The second profile: desert sandstone ashlar

`--profile ashlar`, the same script and the same seed discipline, laid along the same footprints (`section2m`, `wall10m`, `corner`, and a `doorway`), with everything a sandstone ruin needs where the brick profile has its brick:

- **Blocks from a declared size distribution, in irregular courses.** A course's height is drawn per course from 0.26 / 0.30 / 0.34 m (probabilities 0.30 / 0.45 / 0.25); a block's length per block from N(0.60 m, 0.10 m) clipped to 0.38–0.85 m, its depth from U(0.28, 0.32) m; 12% of cells are through-stones 0.36–0.46 m long running the wall's full 0.64 m depth; each course starts at a random offset, so head joints fall where the lengths put them. The wall is two wythes and a 40 mm core; the joint is **12 mm of lime, weathered back 30 mm**, laid as joints — a bed strip under each block, a head strip at its end, a core between the wythes — so nothing of it stands behind a block's face and a deep spall shows stone. **Soft beds**: 30% of courses are soft, their blocks set back 10–22 mm from the face and their layer recess 1.6 times deeper.
- **Weathering is per block, drawn from a declared distribution** (the owner's specification: not uniformly rounded stones, some sharp edges left). Each block's **exposure** is `x = clip(0.30 z/H + 0.25 [nothing above it] + 0.15 [open sides] + 0.20 exp(-d_end/0.8) + 0.12 exp(-d_corner/0.6) + 0.10 [front wythe or through-stone], 0, 1)` — top courses, the broken top line, free ends and corners, missing neighbours and the windward face (`--windward front`, the default) erode most, low and sheltered blocks least. Its erosion amount is **e ~ Beta(6m, 6(1 − m)) with m = 0.20 + 0.60x**, except that with probability **0.25 − 0.17x** it keeps **crisp arrises** (e = 0.03). Each of a block's eight corners gets its own radius, e × 40 mm × U(0.6, 1.4) (and × 1.22 on the windward face), 12% of corners are **fresh breaks** at 2 mm, and the radius is interpolated trilinearly across the block and raised by up to 60% where all three axes are in the edge band — so **corners round more than edges and edges more than faces**, and no two edges of a block agree. The faces then take **(1 + 5e) mm of noise** at about 10/m, **pits** of up to (2 + 8e) mm at 30/m, a **spall** on the outward face with probability 0.10 + 0.40x (10–30 mm deep, 6–15 cm across), and on vertical faces **bedding layers** recessed by up to (4 + 16e) mm in world height with a period of 5–11 cm; the windward face takes 1.5 times the noise, pits and layers. **Fresh breaks of the ruin are sharp**: a loose block at the ruin's edge loses a corner to a plane cut after the weathering with probability 0.35, a fallen block with 0.5, and the cut is not rounded. Every block is built for its place, so no two are alike, and the seed governs all of it. Realized on the three cc0 assets: e has mean 0.31–0.41 and deciles 0.03 / 0.26–0.45 / 0.63–0.68; 16–19% of blocks crisp, 6–22% with a fresh break, e correlating with exposure at r = 0.38–0.46 (each asset's `.stats.json` has `erosion_distribution`, the declared parameters, and `erosion_realized`).
- **Ruin and debris as for the brick** — the erosion mask, the broken top line, the support rule, fallen blocks settled by the rigid-body simulation (identical in both check runs of every asset) — **and a sand skirt**: a heightfield drifted against the walls, 0.34 m high on the windward side and 0.22 m on the lee with ±40% noise, falling off as exp(−d/0.45 m) into a 12 mm sheet that fades out 1.6 m from the wall, with small ripples, in its own material. It half-buries the lowest course and the fallen blocks.
- **Textures, both variants**: CC0 block faces cropped at random from Poly Haven's `cliff_side` (layered brown rock, 1.83 m at 4K, 2,238 texels/m native) and `rock_boulder_dry` (pale rock, 1.8 m, 2,276), tinted toward tan and ochre (Poly Haven has no plain CC0 sandstone surface; its sandstone sets are block walls), `beige_wall_001` for the lime (a 1.5 m crop made tileable, 1,365 texels/m) and `dense_sand` for the skirt (1,138); generated, two Krea 2 sandstone surfaces (tan, ochre) and a desert sand, with the brick's lime image for the joint (the prompts are in `blender-kits\ruined-wall\e33-ashlar-textures.json`; 3 images, 418 s under the GPU lock after a 340 s wait). A block face samples a 0.75 × 0.36 m cell at **1,280 texels/m** in a 4,096² atlas of 32 cells, four tints each.

### Members for a building assembler: the corner, the doorway and the footprint

Ruined buildings will be assembled from the kit by a seeded assembler placing wall sections at right angles, so the generator lays units along a **footprint** rather than a single run, and every preset is one: a list of wall segments (origin, direction, back direction, length, whether each end is free), **corner markers** per segment end (the corner square at that end is this segment's on courses of the marker's parity and its neighbour's on the others — the interlocking quoins), and **openings** (`{wall, u0, u1, top, bearing}`: nothing is laid below `top` over the opening, units straddling a jamb are clipped to it, and a **lintel** bears `bearing` metres on each jamb — precast, in the lime's material, for the brick; a weathered stone for the ashlar; the support rule keeps a lintel only while both jambs carry it). The support rule measures a unit's bearing in plan, so it holds at any right-angled corner of any footprint, not only at a shared origin (and reproduces the brick profile's corner byte for byte). Each profile exports, beside the straight runs, the **L-corner** (`corner`, `ashlar-corner-*`) and a **doorway** (`doorway-cc0`, a 1.0 × 2.1 m opening in a 3 m brick wall; `ashlar-doorway-cc0`, 1.1 × 2.1 m in 3.2 m of ashlar); both doorways built with 0 warnings and are not measured here.

**How an assembler drives it**, without changing the script: write a footprint as JSON — `{"walls": [[name, [x, y, z], [ux, uy, 0], [vx, vy, 0], length, [free_start, free_end]], ...], "corners": {name: {"start": 0|1, "end": 0|1}}, "openings": [...], "courses": n` (brick) or `"height": m` (ashlar), `"erosion": k, "breach": {...}}` — and call `tools/blender-run.ps1 -Script ruined_wall.py -- --profile ashlar --footprint building.json --seed <building seed> --name <building>`; `footprint_from_polyline(points, closed, openings)` in the script builds that JSON's content from an outer-face polyline with right angles (each segment's back to the left of its direction, each interior vertex a corner owned by the earlier segment on even courses). What it does not do yet: angles other than 90°, openings other than rectangular, floors and roofs, and one wall height per call. The assembler that was built instead places members rather than calling the generator per building; the members it needs are the [second iteration's](#second-iteration-2026-09-24-the-kit-as-members-for-the-ruin-assembler), and the second arm of the corner described here was laid inside out (same section).

### Textures, two variants

- **(a) CC0**, fetched by `fetch-cc0.ps1` from Poly Haven (every asset CC0 1.0, https://polyhaven.com/license) with each file's URL, Poly Haven's MD5 (checked) and our SHA-256 recorded in `source.json` and carried into the sidecar: three brick sets and one sand set for the mortar, 4K JPEG diffuse, OpenGL normal, roughness and displacement.

  | set | author | physical size | native texels/m | used for | applied at |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | `yellow_brick` | Rob Tuytel | 2.0 m | 2,048 | 72 brick cells (183 bricks found, 72 distinct used) | 2,048 texels/m (1:1) |
  | `brick_wall_07` | Dimitrios Savva | 1.7 m | 2,409 | 72 cells (42 bricks found; 30 repeated mirrored) | 2,048 (0.85×) |
  | `brown_brick_02` | Rob Tuytel | 1.0 m | 4,096 | 72 cells (14 bricks found; 58 repeated mirrored) | 2,048 (0.5×; its large rough bricks rescaled to the UK format) |
  | `dense_sand` | Dimitrios Savva | 1.8 m | 2,276 | the mortar, tiling every 1.8 m | 1,138 (0.5×, a 2,048² map) |

  With real bricks in the geometry, a brick texture is a set of brick *faces*, not a picture of a wall: `atlas.py` finds the bricks in each set's displacement map (courses from the row profile's minima, head joints from each course's column profile, cells with much area at joint depth dropped), and resamples each brick's rectangle of the diffuse, normal and roughness maps into a 440 × 133 cell — one 215 × 65 mm face at **2,048 texels per metre** — packed with 8 texels of edge padding into a 4,096² atlas of 216 cells. A brick's stretcher faces sample one cell, its beds another (stretched 1.6× across the depth; they are rarely seen), its ends and fractures a third. The sets' displacement maps are used for this and nothing else: glTF core has no height slot and the engine no parallax.
- **(b) Generated**: four albedos from the owner's Krea 2 two-stage workflow through `tools/generate.ps1 image -Raw` (1,456², seeds derived from the names, 118–163 s each, 537 s under the GPU lock): three flat clay-brick surfaces (ochre, faded terracotta, pale mud brick) and one lime-and-sand mortar, each prompted as "seamless flat surface texture … seen straight on … flat even overcast light, no shadows, no highlights" (the list is `blender-kits\ruined-wall\e33-wall-textures.json`, with the exact prompts). A cell is a random brick-sized rectangle of a clay image (taken as 0.40 m across, 3,640 texels/m, resampled to the same 2,048), mirrored at random; the mortar image is taken as 0.80 m (1,820 texels/m) and made **tileable** by a periodic cross-fade with itself shifted by half. **Normal and roughness are derived from luminance** by a documented filter: height = G(lum, 1.5 px) − G(lum, 24 px), periodic Gaussians, normalised to unit deviation; tangent normal = normalize(−k ∂h/∂x, k ∂h/∂y, 1), k = 0.25; roughness = 0.80 − 0.08 h + 0.10 (mean − lum)/σ, clamped to [0.55, 0.98]. The terracotta image came back with a visible 3 × 3 repetition of blotches (Krea's reading of "seamless"); a brick-sized crop hides it.

Every kit asset exists in both variants from the same seed — the same bricks to the vertex, the mortar cut at the other source's tile size — so the two texture sources are compared with everything else held: `section2m-cc0` against `section2m-gen`, and so on.

### The ML side

The owner's own image-to-3D brick and wall experiments, copied unchanged to `D:\workspace\game_engine_local\e33-inputs\` (its README gives their provenance) and from there, byte for byte, to `e33\ml\` under portable names: three hand-scale pieces from Tripo (a brick, a brick block, a sandstone brick), four walls (two runs of "brick wall" and a "desert brick wall"), two "ruined brick walls", an "ancient ruin wall", a "brick pyramid" (all Tripo) and Meshy's "desert ruins". For the size test, three more hard-surface Tripo props named by what they are, from `generated\tripo\`: the E10 wooden crate, the E10 oil lantern (the file Tripo named `antique+lantern`, byte-identical to `oil-lamp.glb`) and the airplane wreck of the large set — one hand-scale and one prop-scale object that are not architecture, and one large one that is not either. (The large set's skyscraper, office tower, cathedral and mosque are not measured here; the E10 harness's `e10\tripo-large-2026-09-23\` report has their LOD and picture numbers.)

**Scale.** An image-to-3D mesh comes out normalised, its largest side about one unit. A brick wall depicts its own scale: a standard brick course is 75 mm. `calibrate.py` renders each mesh orthographically, face on, as albedo alone (base colour through an emission shader, Cycles on the CPU) and takes the course pitch as the first autocorrelation peak down 48-pixel strips of the face; the median over 31–39 strips gave pitches with an interquartile range of at most one pixel and peak strengths of 0.31–0.73 for the eight walls, the pyramid and Meshy's ruins. The three hand-scale pieces have two to five cartoon-sized courses, too few for the autocorrelation (peaks 0.18–0.23, wrong periods), and were counted by eye (3, 5 and 2 courses). The crate and the lantern take the E10 subject list's `size_m` (0.8 m and 0.3 m) and the wreck the desert-overlook scene's fit (48 m long). So the walls are **8 to 22 m long** and the "bricks" **0.28 to 0.37 m** — hand scale, as the owner said; every per-metre number below rests on this calibration, and every scale-free number (texels across the object, the relative turn) does not.

### What is measured, and how

- **Through the engine**: `engine-content build --cache` into a scratch derived-data cache (`e33\ddc\`, 0 warnings for all twenty); `tools/e10-harness.ps1` over the kit's two folders, the eleven and the three props (reports `e33\harness-procedural\`, `harness-ml\`, `harness-ml-props\`, and `harness-ashlar\` for the second profile; default fit framing, 640², 1 px against 0.05 px); and `engine-view --mesh <glb> --offscreen --frames 60 --capture <png> --camera-path <fixed camera> --width 800 --height 800 --no-lights --shadows rt` from two fixed cameras per asset: a three-quarter view that fits the bounding sphere (30° round from the face, 12° up; 45° from outside the corner for the corner) and a **close view 1.6 m (calibrated) from the wall face**, found by a ray from outside at 35% of the height — the view of a player standing beside it, where texel density and sharpness are judged. Each camera is an `engine.camera-path.v1` with one key, written by `preview.py`.
- **By `measure.py`**, on the mesh as the file stores it with positions welded (a UV or normal split is not an edge), in metres:
  - **dihedral**: the share of welded manifold edges whose two faces' normals differ by more than 30, 60 and 85 degrees — what the row asks for; it reads a 90° arris behind a chamfer as two 45° creases, so it is paired with
  - **normal turn in 1 cm**: over 60,000 area-weighted surface points, the angle between the normal there and at the surface point nearest a point 1 cm away along a random tangent (a BVH query) — independent of triangulation; a sharp arris turns 90° within a centimetre, a rounded one of radius r about 1/r radians — and the same at **0.25% of the object's own size** (the relative turn), which is what a resolution fixed relative to the object would keep;
  - **texel density**: per triangle √(UV area × texture texels / world area) for the base-colour texture, the median over sample points a viewer can see (at least one of eight occlusion rays escapes; the kit's bed faces and collar joints are hidden), with its ratio to the owner's targets of **1,000 texels/m** (a wall a player stands beside) and **2,000** (a hero piece);
  - **roughness variation** (the standard deviation of the roughness map at the sample points) and the normal map's mean deviation from flat;
  - **baked lighting**: the albedo's luminance at 20,000 points regressed on the surface normal and on an ambient-occlusion estimate (eight cosine-weighted rays, occluded within 5% of the bounding diagonal) — R², the correlation with occlusion (negative: darker in cavities) and the luminance of upward faces over downward ones — plus the same against the normal *map* in world space (each triangle's tangent frame as the engine's resolve builds it), for lighting painted to match relief the mesh does not have.

**The desert comparison** — the ashlar profile beside the brick wall and the owner's generated pieces it should be judged against (the sandstone brick, the ancient ruin wall, the desert brick wall, Meshy's desert ruins) — is **`D:\workspace\game_engine_local\e33\e33-contact-sheet-desert.png`**, engine captures from the same fixed cameras as every other row. ### Size or architecture

**Size.** Among the image-to-3D meshes every measure of fine detail falls with the object's size, whatever the object is:

- **Texel density** falls from 2,270–3,460 texels/m for the hand-scale pieces (the bricks and the lantern), to 750–790 for the 0.8 m crate and the 2.5 m pyramid, 190–430 for the 8–18 m walls, 133 for the 48 m wreck and 65 for Meshy's 22 m ruins — because what is fixed is the texture: **texels across the object** stay within 630–6,500 over a 170-fold range of size (2K maps for the small pieces, 4K and 8K for the large), so density goes as one over size.
- **Sharpness at 1 cm** falls with it: the share of surface points whose normal turns more than 60° within a centimetre is 9–28% at hand scale and for the crate, 5% for the pyramid, 0.5–5% for the walls, 0.1–0.2% for the wreck and Meshy's ruins. **At 0.25% of their own size**, though, the same meshes turn more than 60° at 1–3% of points when small and 3–14% when large — the large walls are, relative to their size, *as sharp or sharper* than the bricks. So the softness is not a failure to make edges: it is a **fixed relative resolution**, an edge rounded to a fraction of the object, which on a 0.3 m brick is under a millimetre and on a 14 m wall is 3–4 cm — the width of a brick's arris and of the mortar joint itself.
- **Not architecture**: at hand scale the bricks score with the lantern (texel density 2,270–3,290 against 3,460; turn in 1 cm 10–15% against 28%), and at large scale the wreck, which is not architecture, scores with the walls or below them (133 texels/m, 0.2%). The owner's reading holds exactly — the pieces came out "decently", the smallest wall (8.0 m, `tripo-brick-wall-1`) "not horribly" (the best wall on density, 432 texels/m, and second on turn, 3.7%), the larger walls and ruins smoothed — and it is a size effect.

**Architecture is where the size effect bites**, because architecture is the content that is large *and* made of small, sharp, repeated detail whose size does not scale with the building: a brick's arris is a few millimetres and a joint a centimetre whether the wall is 2 m or 20 m. A fixed relative budget spends 3–4 cm on a 14 m wall — one joint's width — and a person standing beside it needs millimetres. So: **both, with size the cause and architecture the case**. The kit does not have the problem at all, because its detail is a property of the brick, not of the wall: every kit asset turns more than 60° within a centimetre at 13–15% of points and carries 2,000–2,040 texels/m on its brick faces at every size, from the 2 m section to the 10 m wall and the corner, and its cost grows with the wall's area instead (76,000 triangles for the section, 600,000 for the 10 m wall, 450,000 for the corner).

### Baked lighting

The kit's albedos carry none: R² of luminance on orientation, on occlusion and on the normal map's detail are all 0.000–0.012, and upward faces are within 1.2% of downward ones, for both texture sources — the CC0 bricks were photographed flat and de-lit, and the generated clay surfaces were prompted for flat light and are sampled per brick at random.

The image-to-3D meshes carry it in two ways. **At hand scale it follows the mesh**: the brick, the brick block, the sandstone brick and the crate explain 12–23% of their albedo's variance by orientation, and three of them 20–21% by occlusion (correlation −0.45: darker in cavities), their upward faces are 11–29% brighter than their downward ones, and their luminance follows the normal map's up component at r = 0.33–0.46; the wreck does the same (R² 0.12 and 0.24, up faces 28% brighter). The lantern is the exception (0.00–0.01). **At wall scale it is painted at the brick scale**, onto a mesh that no longer has the relief: the albedo-only renders (`e33\calibrate\<asset>.albedo.png`) show the joints painted as dark shadow lines and the rubble shaded dark underneath and light on top, and the geometric tests barely see it (R² 0.00–0.09, occlusion correlation −0.30 to +0.04) because the cavities it darkens are not in the geometry; the normal-map test sees a little of it (R² up to 0.06, r up to 0.14 with the mapped up direction). The measure is sound where the relief is in the mesh and weak where it is not, which is exactly where it matters; the pictures say the rest.

### The ashlar profile against the desert pieces

Desert Survival's ruins are the ashlar profile's, and the pieces it replaces are the owner's desert ones. At wall scale it carries **1,272–1,274 texels/m** on its block faces — 1.27× the 1,000 target for a wall a player stands beside, 0.64× the 2,000 hero figure (the cell is a 0.75 m block face, so 2,000 texels/m would need an 8,192² atlas or fewer cells) — against 287 for the ancient ruin wall, 406 for the desert brick wall and 65 for Meshy's ruins: **3 to 20 times** the density of the walls it would stand in for. It is **rounder than the brick kit by design** — the share of surface turning more than 60° within a centimetre is 4.8–8.1% against the brick's 13–15%, because most of its blocks are weathered — and still **1.7 to 80 times** as sharp as those walls (2.9%, 0.5%, 0.1%), with the crisp blocks and the fresh breaks the owner asked for keeping arrises the ML walls do not have anywhere; at 0.25% of its own size it turns more than 60° at 6.7–13.6% of points, in the range of the large ML walls, which is where their detail stops and its does not. Its albedo carries no lighting (R² 0.000–0.002 on orientation and occlusion, 0.00–0.03 on the mapped normal's detail), its roughness varies (σ 0.114–0.121), and its normal maps carry the rock's grain (15° mean deviation from the CC0 rock scans, 3.5° from the luminance-derived maps of the generated surfaces). The generated sandstone reads as sandstone — warm, fine-grained, layered — where the nearest CC0 surfaces (a layered brown cliff and a pale boulder, tinted) read as rock; that variant is the one to look at first. The owner's sandstone brick, at 0.28 m, is the one generated piece that meets the target (3,294 texels/m, 15% sharp at 1 cm) — and bakes its lighting (R² 0.15 and 0.21, upward faces 13% brighter): hand scale again.

**Its LOD collapses further than the brick kit's, and passes the same checks.** The harness's coarse cut draws **20–27%** of the finest cut's pairs, against the brick kit's 37–43% and the ML walls' 7–21%: a block is about three times a brick's length and four times its height, and most are rounded, so the simplifier has more to merge before it reaches a block's silhouette. Four of the six pass the picture check (FLIP 0.0100–0.0166); the two corners fail it marginally (0.0202 and 0.0242), as three of the brick kit's six did, with the same diagnosis — detail carried by the geometry, and the atlas read as fragmented — and they are the two that combine the set's high triangle counts (460,000) with a larger share of the frame than the 10 m walls (16% against 10%). 0 warnings, 12 LOD levels each, the smallest atlas island 18.5 texels of a 4,096 atlas on the corners and 130–136 elsewhere.
The ashlar profile costs about the same as the brick wall for the same length: 93,000 triangles for the 2 m section (its sand skirt 14,000 of them), 526,000–529,000 for the 10 m wall and 460,000 for the corner, 24–52 MB of container, 0 warnings; generating it takes 8–26 s a wall on the CPU (the 10 m wall's 267 blocks, each built for its place, 13 s of it).

### What the pipeline took, and what it could not

- **All twenty measured assets imported with 0 warnings** and built containers, and so did the six ashlar walls and the two doorways; the kit's `_CANONICAL_ID` arrived as authored ids, and `tools/generate.ps1 verify` passes all fourteen kit sidecars `tools/blender-run.ps1` wrote, against the JSON Schema the build generates from `schemas/provenance.schema`. The UV-degenerate repair ([geometry](../subsystems/geometry.md#uv-degenerate-triangles-the-repair)) folded 5–18 islands per kit asset: the chamfer's corner triangles (the L1 projection makes a small facet at each of a brick's eight corners) have UV areas that round to nothing in half floats. Harmless, and a finer corner rule in the kit would remove it.
- **The material sampler clamps UVs, and at the build measured it had no mips** (`gfx::create_sampler`: `CLAMP_TO_EDGE`, `maxLod = 0`; the resolve sampled level 0). A tiling texture cannot be repeated by UVs past 1, so the kit's mortar is cut at every tile boundary and mapped per tile, and a 4,096² brick atlas seen from a distance aliased. **The mips arrived on main the same evening, after these measurements** (`1f76005`: built BC7/BC5 textures with their full chains, a trilinear 16× anisotropic sampler, `SampleGrad` in the resolve; [texture](../subsystems/texture.md)), and the kit's GLBs embed their images, so their next build gets them; the new sampler still clamps, so the tile cut stays until a repeat mode exists. None of the captures here has the built textures.
- **No vertex colours and no height map.** The importer reads neither, so a per-brick tint is a choice of atlas cell (216 cells, four tints) rather than a multiply, and the CC0 sets' displacement maps have nowhere to go; glTF core has no height slot either.
- **The harness misreads a kit's atlas as fragmented.** Every brick face is its own chart, so 71% of vertices are on a seam and the atlas's "used" share is 1,400–45,000% (216 cells shared by thousands of bricks) — and the diagnosis for the kit's marginal FLIP failures says "fragmented atlas: repack". The islands are large (median 50,000 texels of a 4,096 atlas) and the LOD collapses to 37–43% of the finest cut, well inside the 75% line; what the coarse cut loses is the bricks' own shading, the "detail carried by geometry" case. Reused charts are a kit's normal state, and a validator reading atlas statistics has to know it. The ashlar profile's larger block faces make its atlas read as less fragmented (31–34% seam vertices, median island 147,000–268,000 texels), and its two failing corners got the same diagnosis.
- **The glTF exporter's defaults** needed two settings: `export_attributes` (else `_CANONICAL_ID` is dropped) and backface culling on the material (else every material is written `doubleSided`).

## What surprised me

1. **The ML meshes are sharp — relative to themselves.** The expectation, and the owner's word, was "smoothed": no sharp edges anywhere. At 0.25% of their own size the large walls turn more than 60° at up to 14% of points, as often as the kit does at 1 cm. The softness is entirely a matter of absolute scale: the services put a fixed amount of detail into whatever they are asked for, and a wall is simply too big for it.
2. **The large walls have strong normal maps and flat roughness.** Five of Tripo's six walls carry normal maps that turn the surface by 20–25° on average (the kit's by 4–7°): the detail the geometry lacks was baked into the normal map, which is the classic repair — but at 190–430 texels/m it is detail at the scale of a brick, not of an arris. Their roughness barely varies (standard deviation 0.03–0.06 over the wall; 0.01 for the brick and the brick block) against the kit's 0.09–0.12.
3. **The brick kit's LOD collapses less than the ML meshes'.** A continuous smooth wall collapses to 7–21% of its finest cut; the kit's separate bricks, each chart its own island, stop at 37–43% — and the ashlar profile's larger, rounder blocks at 20–27%, inside the ML range. All pass; the kits' far tier (below) is where the rest is recovered.
4. **Blender's rigid-body simulation was repeatable** — identical rest poses twice in one process and byte-identical GLBs from two processes — so the settled-debris look did not have to be traded for determinism on this machine. It remains a property of one Blender build on one CPU, not a promise.
5. **The generated textures made a cleaner wall than the photographs** — new-looking bricks with uniform grain — and the CC0 sets a more convincing ruin, because they carry real weathering. Both sit at the same density; the choice is art direction, not fidelity.

## What it decides

- **Hard-surface architecture is authored as procedural kits, not generated, in one generator with a profile per material**: the brick profile for Island City, the ashlar profile for Desert Survival's ruins. For walls and buildings the brick kit meets the owner's texture target (2,000 texels/m on the bricks, the hero figure; 1,138–1,820 on the mortar, above the 1,000 for a wall a player stands beside), has real roughness variation, bakes no lighting into its albedo, and is as sharp at 10 m as at 2 m; image-to-3D walls miss the 1,000 target by 2.3–5.2× (Tripo) and 15× (Meshy) and round their arrises to centimetres. **Conditions**: (1) a kit's declared texel density reaches a distance only through the texture pipeline's mips and block compression, which exist on main since `1f76005` (after these measurements, which have neither), and its tiling materials need a repeat address mode or keep being cut at tile boundaries, as the kit's mortar is; (2) a kit needs a **far tier** — a flat or blocky wall with the same atlas and a baked normal map — because brick-by-brick geometry costs about 30,000 triangles for each square metre of standing wall, rubble included (the 10 m wall is 600,000 triangles and 64–68 MB of container) and its LOD stops at the bricks; the cluster LOD pays for the near field, not for a ruin 2 km away; (3) a kit is laid along a footprint, so the building assembler the plan names (07 §7.6) calls the generator per building with a footprint of segments, corner markers and openings rather than a new script ([above](#members-for-a-building-assembler-the-corner-the-doorway-and-the-footprint)); (4) the kit's scripts need a home: Blender became a content dependency in this experiment, and whether its scripts live in `game_engine_local`, in a content repository or in the tree (which would mean accepting Python in it) is the ADR the row names.
- **Image-to-3D keeps what it is good at**: hand-scale props — the bricks, the lantern — meet the texture target unrepaired (2,270–3,460 texels/m, 1.1–1.7× the hero figure), within the same 50,000-triangle budget, and props at a person's height are near the line (the crate 0.79×). Above that it is volume for a flythrough, as 07 §7.7's direction note says. **Its albedos carry the lighting they were generated under** (at hand scale up to 23% of the variance by orientation, upward faces up to 29% brighter), which a de-lighting repair would have to remove before such props sit beside kit assets under the engine's lights.
- **The owner's rule, measured**: quality falls with size because the budget is fixed per object (texels across the object stay within 630–6,500 over a 170-fold range of size, and edges round at a fixed fraction of it), and architecture is the content that suffers because its detail does not scale with the building. Person scale — about 2 m, where a 2K–4K budget still gives 1,000 texels/m — is where the line falls.

It does **not** decide the far tier's form, the texture pipeline's formats, whether the generated or the photographed textures are the art direction, or anything about buildings beyond walls (Island City's facades are a grammar over kits of this kind, [07 §7.6](../plan/07-content-pipeline.md#76-procedural-generation-volume-content), not measured here).

### The grey faces: a corner's second arm was laid by a reflection

The generator lays a wall as (origin, u, v, length), v pointing from its front face to its back, and placed every unit by a rotation whose columns were (u, v, up) or a signed permutation of them. For the corner's first wall, +x with its back toward +y, that frame is right-handed; for its second, +y with its back toward +x, it is **left-handed — a reflection**. A reflected unit keeps its shape, but every face winds the other way, and exported single-sided (the kit sets backface culling so the exporter does not write `doubleSided`, "What the pipeline took" above) its normals point into the stone: the engine drew the inside of each stone's far faces, lit as if facing out, and the resolve's Fresnel term at a grazing normal turned them pale blue-grey. Counted on the exported meshes by the signed volume of every closed part (`kit_describe.py --check`): **95 of the ashlar corner's 239 stones and 903 of the brick corner's 2,286 bricks were inside out**, in both texture variants — the second arm and the quoins it owns on alternate courses — and **0** in every straight wall and doorway. E33's own engine captures of both corners already show it (the left arm of `e33\captures\ashlar-corner-cc0-front34.png` and `corner-cc0-front34.png`), unremarked; in the assembled ruin every corner has a second arm, so every building showed it. It was not a material, an atlas island, a texture or the renderer. The fix is in the generator: a unit's along-wall axis is turned end for end where (u, v, up) is left-handed (`along_axis`), so every placement is a rotation, and the generator refuses to export a reflected unit; every member of both new kits has **0** inside-out parts. On the way, a second texture defect: a stone longer than its 0.75 m atlas cell clamped its UVs at the cell's edge and smeared one column of texels over the rest of its face — invisible on most blocks, obvious on the doorway's 1.7 m lintel; a unit longer than its cell now takes the whole cell, stretched (the lintel 2.3 times).

### What changed in the generator, and why

- **Members in the assembler's frame.** `ruined_wall.py --member section|corner|inside-corner|doorway|window|debris` lays one member where [ruins](../subsystems/ruins.md#the-kit) expects it: first socket at the origin, the wall along glTF +x, outside toward +z, the ground at y = 0, so every member's `offset` is zero and its `yaw_deg` 0. An outside corner's incoming arm arrives travelling +z with its outside at −x, an inside corner's travelling −z with its outside at +x; both leave along +x.
- **Joined ends.** A wall end is now free (a ruin's broken end, as before), a corner, or a **socket**: no erosion term at all (neither the free end's nor the corner's), and the end units **reach the socket plane** — their cell's half joint on that side is stone — cut flush to it (a vertex the weathering pushed past it is put back on it: a few hundred per member). Two members then butt stone to stone, their weathered arrises making the joint's groove, and a member whose neighbour is gone or lower ends in dressed stone. The first pass closed each socket with half a head joint of lime instead, as a joint inside a wall is; the engine captures showed every exposed socket end as a flat slab of lime, drawn pale blue-grey like the inside-out faces, and it went (`captures\first-pass\`). What a flat socket cannot hide is the joint's straightness: every join is a vertical joint through all courses, where masonry would stagger it. A toothed socket (alternate courses projecting) would, but only if every member toothed alike and no member were ever sunk against a neighbour, and the assembler sinks members.
- **One coursing per kit.** Bed joints meet across a join only if both members laid the same courses, so the ashlar's drawn course heights come from a kit-wide `--course-seed` (7) and each member's blocks from its own seed. The brick's courses are fixed at 75 mm anyway; its module is a whole number of Flemish periods so every member begins and ends the bond at the same phase and each join is the same stopped end on both sides.
- **The module.** Ashlar **2.0 m**. Brick **1.35 m**, four Flemish periods: 2 m leaves a 31 mm sliver in every other course at every join (dropped, a hole through the wall), 2.025 m (nine UK brick modules) is not whole centimetres, which the assembler computes in, and 2.70 m (eight periods) closed no L or U in a 32 m tile once its rubble's margin was added (the smallest L is 16.2 m a side, 33 m across its bounding circle).
- **Sections of one and two modules** (ashlar 2 m and 4 m, brick 1.35 m and 2.7 m), not the first kit's 10 m wall: the assembler lays a collapsed wall in its shortest sections anyway, and its measurement found the cull pass paying per (instance, cluster) pair, which a 13,000-cluster member multiplies.
- **Height variants: h100, h50 and h25**, for sections and for both corners (the assembler picks a corner by the lower of its two walls). All variants lay the same courses to the full height and the ruin rule brings them down: the mask's height term is **shifted down** to the variant's height rather than scaled, so its top is broken over as many courses as the full one's (scaled, the term climbs a whole course faster at a quarter height than the noise can follow, and the first quarter-height sections came out with ruler-straight tops). The full-height target is 0.92 of the laid wall, so even the h100 members lose part of their top course. A member's noise is finer along the wall and weaker, and its per-stone share larger, than the free-standing presets', which are two to ten metres and whose 0.9/m noise hardly changes over a member. Measured, one of the eight h25 members still came out two whole courses (ashlar 2 m: p10–p90 of its top 0.62–0.64 m); the others break over one to three courses.
- **Openings of one module**, the lintel and the sill kept whatever the ruin rule says (the assembler places an opening only where the wall stands): a **doorway** (ashlar 1.1 m in 2 m, lintel bearing 0.3 m on each jamb; brick 0.9 m in 1.35 m, so each jamb is one stretcher) and a **window** (ashlar 0.8 m, sill at 0.85 m, head at the first course above 1.75 m; brick 0.9 m, sill 0.9, head 1.8). A window's sill is a stone (ashlar) or a precast sill (brick, in the lime's material like its lintel) in the course under the opening, projecting 40 mm from the outside face.
- **An inside corner**, the outside corner's L with its front and back exchanged: the weathered, windward face is the concave one.
- **Debris members.** Ashlar: five fallen stones from the kit's own size distribution, each weathered as exposed stone and broken one to three times (half a block, a mean block, a long block, a through-stone, a fragment). Brick: heaps of 6, 12, 24 and 40 bricks piled by the rule's heightfield. Each rests on the ground at the origin, lowest point at y = 0. The kit description measures a piece's **radius** (its farthest vertex from the vertical axis) and its **volume** (the closed surface's), and weights the pick **inversely by volume**, so on average every piece brings the same volume of stone and the small ones are the common ones.
- **Neither rubble nor sand on any member** (`--member` implies `--debris none` and no skirt).
- **Fracture slivers.** A fresh-break plane through a weathered face could cut off a zero-volume island, which the winding check flagged; a fracture now keeps only its largest connected piece.

The free-standing presets (`--preset`, `--footprint`) still build what they built, except the reflection and the long-stone UVs, which were defects in them too.

### What the assembler's kit schema cannot say

The kit description fits `engine.scene.RuinKit` as it is, and every member loads and assembles; these are what the kit knows and the schema has no field for, reported for the [ruins](../subsystems/ruins.md) module rather than worked around in the kit:

1. **A member's extent past its sockets.** A window's sill projects 40 mm from the outside face and a brick heap is 1.9 m across, but the assembler's margin comes from the debris spread and the drift alone, and nothing checks a member's mesh against a neighbour's or the tile. `kit.measure.json` has every member's bounds.
2. **Height is one number; a ruined top is a line.** The measured top at each socket differs from the member's median by up to half a metre (the ashlar 4 m h50 section stands 2.07 m at one end and 1.46 m at the other), so two neighbours chosen for the same asked height can still meet at a step of a course or two. A top per socket would let the rule choose neighbours whose tops meet.
3. **Coursing is shared, and nothing says so.** Bed joints meet at a join only because every member of a kit laid the same courses (one `--course-seed`); a kit mixing coursings would load and assemble silently with a step at every join. A kit-level coursing the loader checks would say it.
4. **Sinking shifts the courses.** `max_sink` sinks a member by any number of centimetres, and its bed joints then step against both neighbours' by as much; in 0.26–0.34 m ashlar courses anything but a whole course shows at both joins. With the course heights in the kit the rule could sink by whole courses, or prefer the gap.
5. **Debris has a pick weight, not a mass.** The stones weigh 27–181 kg and the brick heaps 16–95 kg (volume × 2,300 or 1,900 kg/m³, in `kit.measure.json`); a physics that moves rubble will want the mass, and `weight` means something else.
6. **A flat socket makes a straight joint.** Every join is a vertical joint through all courses, where masonry staggers them. A toothed socket would hide it, but only as a profile every member shares and with no sinking, which the schema cannot declare and the rule does not respect.

And one for the renderer: every member embeds the same atlas (a debris stone's GLB is 13.5 MB, nearly all of it the three block maps), and the scene loader uploads each mesh's images on their own ([renderer](../subsystems/renderer.md)), so a building of the kit's 19 distinct members can hold the same textures 19 times on the GPU, where the derived-data cache already keys the built textures by their bytes.
