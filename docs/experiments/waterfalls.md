# Water that leaves its bed: the grounding and the design for S104

Roadmap S104 (2026-11-12). **A page, not code**: nothing in the tree changes with it. The owner's direction for all water that day: "waves, streams, water in general needs to conform to the terrain it's on ... something more representative of the real mechanics behind it ... with the effects of gravity, surface tension, friction, obstacles". The bodies' half is S102's and the channels' obstacles are S103's (both on branches of their own as this is written; S103's page ends at "What leaves the bed", where this one starts, and its rule that nothing is stored from frame to frame is kept here).

**Where the engine stands.** There is no waterfall. A stretch of a channel that drops at least as far as it runs is drawn as the same ribbon, tilted, with a free fall's speed in its phase and white water by that speed ([water](../subsystems/water.md#the-ribbon)): it lies on the face, it has no path of its own, and nothing shows through it. The forest proof of concept's authors on the result: "a cascade is still one white chute"; a riser "is a tilted glassy sheet: it does not read as falling water". Their request list has asked for "a free-falling sheet that leaves its lip for a plunge pool; spray and mist; a notch in a flat rim; falls where the rock makes them" since 2026-11-10.

**The design in one sentence.** Water is a projectile that its bed may hold up and cannot pull down: the cook marches each column of a channel's water from the lip along a ballistic path against the ground's own profile, so that the water leaves where the bed falls away faster than the path, lands where the path meets rock or water, and slides where it has landed on a face; the flight is a sheet mesh whose vertices carry the water's thickness and travel time; the pixel turns the thickness and the fall's distance over its break-up length into a coverage, a whiteness and a carried texture by closed forms of the clock; what is not covered shows the rock behind through the ray a channel's water already traces; and the landing is a source of air, waves and spray that the water below and the mist read. Nothing is kept from frame to frame.

Four parts: [what the shots show](#what-the-shots-show), [the grounding](#the-grounding), [the design](#the-design), [what to ask of the next reels](#what-to-ask-of-the-next-reels), then [the sources and how far each was read](#the-sources-and-how-far-each-was-read). Most of the hydraulics papers refused the fetch or sit behind a paywall; nothing here presents a search result's summary as a paper, and what is from memory or derived on this page says so.

**What the shots settle.** (1) Below its crest a fall's texture moves as free fall, and stretches. (2) The white share falls from two thirds to a fifth down the visible fall while the streaks' spacing stays. (3) What shows through is the rock, fixed in place. (4) Unsteadiness is local: clumps a fraction of the width across that hold their shape for a few tenths of a second, not a surge. (5) A tall thin fall widens slowly over its first half and five times as fast over its second, and its lower part swings by a fifth of its width over seconds. (6) Between a chute and a curtain the difference is the drop against the water's depth: half a boulder of drop under deep water is a chute, a ledge of one to three metres under centimetres of water is a curtain.

## The grounding

Symbols: q the discharge a metre of lip (m²/s), d_c = (q²/g)^(1/3) the critical depth, v_c = √(g d_c) the critical speed, H the drop from the lip to where the water lands, θ the face's angle from the horizontal, s the distance fallen, v the water's speed, t = q / v the sheet's thickness, σ = 0.072 N/m the surface tension, ρ and ρ_a the densities of water and air.

### The lip

- **The brink.** A subcritical stream passes through its critical depth a few depths above a free overfall and is drawn down to **0.715 d_c at the brink** (Rouse 1936, *Civil Engineering* 6(4), 257: the value confirmed by a search's summary of later papers that quote it for a horizontal or mild rectangular channel; the paper was not opened). The pressure there is no longer hydrostatic: it is atmospheric on both faces of the nappe that leaves (the same summary). The mean speed at the brink is the discharge over that depth, **v_b = v_c / 0.715 = 1.40 √(g d_c)**, a velocity head of 0.98 d_c. The engine has used both since S98 (`brink_speed`).
- **What changes it.** The ratio depends on the bed's slope against the critical slope and, on steep beds, on roughness (later work, by the same summary): a supercritical reach goes over as it is, which is S98's rule. A lip that is a notch narrower than the channel is a contraction: the flow is critical in the notch and its q is the channel's discharge over the notch's width.
- **A lip is not level.** The brink depth over each part of a lip is the upstream surface less that part's height, so the discharge a metre of lip goes as the 3/2 power of the local head: a lip with relief of the order of the brink depth sends most of its water through its low parts, and a part that stands above the surface is dry. That is the **segmented** fall, and the lobes in `falls-wide` are spaced two or three critical depths apart.

### The path

- **The nappe is a projectile.** A parcel leaving at v_b along the bed's slope θ₀ follows x = v_b cos θ₀ τ, y = v_b sin θ₀ τ + g τ²/2. The two faces leave from the lip's edge (the lower nappe) and from the surface above it (the upper), the brink depth apart, and close as the sheet thins: **t = q / v**, by continuity, with v² = v_b² + 2 g s. A sheet 0.15 m thick at the brink of a 0.3 m²/s stream is 2 cm thick ten metres down, moving at 14 m/s. Air drag on it is 0.1 m/s² at that speed and thickness (a skin friction of 0.005 on two faces), a hundredth of gravity: while it is whole, a sheet falls as if in a vacuum, which is what `falls-wide` measures.
- **Behind the nappe.** Under a nappe that lands on a level floor, water is held against the face to a depth **y_p = h (d_c / h)^0.66** (Rand 1955's drop-structure fits, with the landing distance 4.30 h (d_c/h)^0.81 and the jet's depth 0.54 h (d_c/h)^1.275 the running-water page took from Chanson's account; this one from memory of Chow's and then confirmed, with the other three, by a search's summary of a hydraulics reference that reproduces Chow's four equations in the drop number D = (d_c/h)³: y_p/h = D^0.22). It fills the whole drop when h = d_c, 63% of it at 2 d_c, 48% at 3, 34% at 5, 22% at 10. So below about three critical depths of drop more than half of what would be the fall's back is water standing against the face: the paddler's pour-over, S103's hole, not a fall.
- **Leaving against clinging, for a stream.** A bed can push water up and cannot pull it down. On a convex crest of radius R the pressure on the bed is ρ d (g cos φ - v²/R) (the curved-flow correction of every open-channel text; derived here), so the water lifts off where v² exceeds g R cos φ: a rounded crest carries the water round to the angle at which that holds, and the lip is there. Past a lip, a plane face of angle θ lies under the parcel's path until the path meets it again, at a drop of

  **y_r = 2 v_b² tan² θ / g = 3.91 d_c tan² θ**

  (the parabola through the lip against the line y = x tan θ; the constant is 2 / 0.715²; **derived here**). For a 45° face that is 3.9 d_c, for 60° 11.7, for 70° 29.5, for 80° 126. A face higher than y_r is struck: the water leaves the lip, lands on the face and runs down it as a thin fast film, a **horsetail**, or lands on a tread and leaves again, a **tiered** fall; a face lower than y_r is cleared: a **plunge**. The same face is a plunge under a river (d_c 1 m clears 30 m of a 70° face) and a horsetail under a brook (d_c 0.1 m strikes it 3 m down), which is the common observation that falls change kind with the season. Mabin's (2000) rule for calling a fall a plunge, the lip no more than a quarter of the height out from the pool (Wikipedia's waterfall page, read), is the same geometry.
- **On the face** the film is a steep chute, which the running-water page already has: Manning's speed for the depth the discharge leaves (d = (q n / √sin θ)^(3/5)), air to 0.9 sin θ, thin water. At 0.05 m²/s on 60° of rock at n 0.02 that is 1.7 cm at 3 m/s where a free fall of the same height would be at 12: **a horsetail's texture runs a quarter as fast as a plunge's**, which is how the eye tells them. Where the face is convex across the flow the film spreads until it is too thin to run as one (below): the **fan**.
- **Clinging by surface tension, for a trickle.** Duez, Ybert, Clanet and Bocquet (2010, *Phys. Rev. Lett.* 104, 084503; the arXiv text read) poured a film 1 to 5 m/s fast round edges of radius 0.03 to 2 mm: a film bends round an edge and runs back along the underside below a threshold Weber number **We_c ∝ (ℛ r_i / e₀²)(1 + cos θ₀)**, We = ρ U² e₀ / σ, with e₀ the film's thickness, r_i the edge's radius, ℛ = r_i + e₀/2 and θ₀ the contact angle; "dripping occurs more easily for spouts with thicker edges", and never off a sharp or water-repellent one. Wet rock is hydrophilic (1 + cos θ₀ near 2) and a natural lip is rounded at centimetres, so for a film of millimetres the threshold is some metres a second: a film that thin goes round the lip and down the face, overhang or not. The text gives the scaling and no constant (the values are in a figure), so **no number is taken from it**.
- **A curtain against a hole.** A hole in a thin sheet opens at the Taylor-Culick speed √(2σ / ρt); the falling sheet outruns it while ρ v² t / σ > 2, which with t = q / v is **ρ q v > 2σ** (the critical Weber number of 2 for a sheet is in a search's summaries of the sheet-stability papers; the curtain form is Brown's 1961, **from memory**). At one metre a second that is q above 1.5 x 10⁻⁴ m²/s, a seventh of a litre a second for each metre of lip. Above it surface tension does not break a sheet; turbulence does (below). Under it there is no sheet at all: the water gathers into rivulets a couple of centimetres apart (the capillary length's scale; from memory) and runs down the rock.

### Break-up

- **The length a sheet stays whole.** The turbulence a jet leaves with roughens its faces until the disturbances meet in the middle. For a round jet, L_b / (D F²) = 1.05 / C^0.82 with C = 1.14 T_u F², T_u the turbulence intensity at issue and F the Froude number there (Ervine, Falvey and Withers 1997, by a search's summary). For a nappe Castillo's group writes the same form on the thickness, **L_b / (B F²) = K / (1.14 T_u F²)^0.82**, with K quoted between 0.4 and 0.95 in the summaries found, and gives Horeni's (1956) **L_b = 6 q^0.32** metres as the one earlier expression, "correct" near the 0.25 m²/s of laboratory tests and measured on jets issuing horizontally (search summaries; **neither Castillo's papers nor Horeni's was opened**: one copy returned 404, one 403, one an empty page).
- **For a brink** B = 0.715 d_c and F² = 2.74, so the form is L_b = 1.96 K d_c / (3.12 T_u)^0.82: a multiple of the critical depth that depends on the lip's turbulence alone. Requiring Horeni's 3.85 m at 0.25 m²/s (d_c 0.185 m) with a laboratory T_u of 0.013 gives **K = 0.77** (this page's arithmetic, inside the quoted range), and then L_b is **21 d_c at T_u 0.013, 10.5 at 0.03, 6.9 at 0.05, 4.7 at 0.08, 3.9 at 0.10**. `falls-wide` agrees in order: the crest is whole for 4 to 7 m where the critical depth is about a metre (113 m³/s over 30 to 46 m), so 4 to 7 d_c, a T_u of 0.05 to 0.10, which is what water arriving through a boulder rapid would have. Horeni's power form and this one part company for small streams (at d_c 5 cm Horeni gives 2 m, the scaled form 0.2 to 0.5 m); the scaled form is taken because it is dimensionally whole, and a reel with a scale decides.
- **Strands.** The disturbances that break a sheet are along the fall as well as across it: before it is drops it is ligaments, as `falls-wide` shows and no paper found here measures. By continuity, water of sheet thickness t gathered into round strands λ apart covers **√(4 t / π λ)** of the width: as the fall speeds up and t thins, the strands thin and the gaps open, with the spacing fixed, which is the measurement (the white share falling from two thirds to a fifth while the streaks' half width stays).
- **Drops.** A strand of diameter d_s pinches into drops about 1.9 d_s across at a spacing of 4.5 d_s (Rayleigh's most unstable wavelength; from memory), and a drop moving through air faster than it can bear is torn: the largest stable drop has ρ_a v² d / σ of about 12 (Hinze 1955; from memory), 3.7 mm at 14 m/s and 8.9 mm at 9. Rain agrees: 0.5 to 4 mm, fragmentation capping drops near 6 mm (Villermaux and Bossa 2009), spherical under 2 mm (Wikipedia's page on drops, read).
- **A drop's terminal speed.** About 8 m/s at 3 mm; under 1 mm a drop is within 5% of its terminal speed 2 m after its release and a 2 mm drop after 5.6 m (the same page). Gunn and Kinzer's (1949) table, **from memory** (a search confirmed the paper and its range, not the values): 0.27 m/s at 0.1 mm, 2.1 at 0.5, 4.0 at 1, 6.5 at 2, 8.1 at 3, 8.8 at 4, 9.1 at 5. With quadratic drag, v² = v_t² + (v₀² - v_t²) e^(-2 g s / v_t²): a drop forgets the speed it was shed at over v_t² / 2g, 4 m for a 9 m/s drop. **So the lower part of a tall fall does not accelerate**: below its break-up length plus ten metres or so its water falls at 7 to 9 m/s against the air around it, a hundred-metre fall takes ten seconds where free fall would take four and a half, and its drops go where the air goes.

### The foot

- **Into a pool.** A jet entering water takes air down when it enters faster than about a metre a second (0.9 m/s for a plane jet: Wang, Bertola and Chanson 2016, by a search's summary; S98's inception speed). The two-phase jet widens at a half-angle of 12.5°, and its bubbles reach a depth of **V_i D_i / (2 U_t tan 12.5°)** with U_t = 0.22 m/s the rise of any bubble over a millimetre (Clanet and Lasheras 1997, as Dev et al. 2024 restate it; the 2024 abstract read, the formula by a search's summary). The relation was measured on jets of millimetres and "overpredicts ... for larger jets": 5 m for a 5 cm sheet at 10 m/s. For a fall, then, **the bubbles go to the pool's floor**, and the white water's life is the floor's depth over the rise: 9 s under 2 m. Dev et al.'s own finding is that the cloud is shallower the more air it holds, by buoyancy. How much air a nappe takes down (Ervine's entrainment ratio, remembered as of the order of one volume of air for one of water on a tall thin fall) **could not be confirmed** and is not pinned.
- **The pool.** Scheingross and Lamb (2016, 2017) model a plunge pool's depth as a balance of the sediment supplied and what the jet can lift out, on flume runs and a survey of 75 falls; pools deepen fast and then stop (search summaries of the abstracts). Wikipedia's page on plunge pools, read, has no number. The pool's shape is a solver's and is not derived here.
- **Onto rock.** No pool, so no bubble cloud: the water keeps the part of its speed along the surface it strikes and loses the part into it, which goes to **splash**. A drop thrown at v_s lands within v_s²/g. The literature of dam spillways calls this splash a rainfall round the impact and says its distribution "has not been well characterised" (a search's summary of a 2023 paper the fetch tool refused to read); no ejection speed was found, so the splash's reach is **a choice** below.
- **Downstream.** What leaves a pool leaves over its rim, a control: critical there, S98's standing waves where the Froude number allows, and S101's air riding out at the pool's own speed and depth.

### The air

- **The wind a fall makes.** Nothing citable was found (the searches returned generated overviews that name a "waterfall wind" and measure nothing). The mechanism is momentum: a whole sheet drags only a boundary layer (0.24 m thick 20 m down a 20 m/s sheet by the flat plate's law, about 0.6 m²/s of air a face); a broken fall is drops, and a drop cannot fall faster than its terminal speed through the air round it, so the air inside a spray is driven towards the water's speed less the drops' terminal speed. For 0.1 m²/s broken into a curtain 2 m thick and arriving at 14 m/s, sharing the water's momentum with the air in the curtain gives a downdraught of about 6 m/s (2.4 u² + 100 (u + 7) = 1,400 for each metre of lip; **derived here, unchecked against any measurement**). At the foot it can only go outwards: an outflow along the water and the ground, falling off with distance.
- **The spray's reach.** Coarse splash is ballistic and local (above). Fines ride the outflow: a 0.1 mm droplet settles at 0.27 m/s, so from 2 m up in a 3 m/s outflow it lands 20 m off, and smaller ones evaporate first. So there are two zones: a **splash zone** of metres, wet as rain, and a **mist zone** downwind of tens of metres, damp.
- **What grows there.** Rock in a fall's spray is its own habitat: the "spray cliff" communities of the southern Appalachians, "constantly wet rock faces within the spray or splash zones of waterfalls" with algae, mosses, liverworts and a few herbs; in British Columbia cryptogams were 4.4 times as diverse in spray zones as beside them while vascular plants were fewer, and a fall's cool outflow carries its mosses and lichens "far beyond the reach of spray" (search summaries of the Virginia and NatureServe community descriptions, a 2024-25 Wells Gray report and a University of British Columbia page; none opened). No transect of cover against distance was found.
- **The mist row.** R47's capability draws a medium in froxels after the resolve, and its nearest relative to a fall is the **crest plume** ([volumetrics](../subsystems/volumetrics.md#the-crest-plumes)): each dune crest sheds a ribbon of sand downwind, a half-Gaussian whose density is conserved as it spreads and decays over 2.5 s of the wind, binned on the CPU into a grid round the eye and summed per froxel, with the dust's albedo and phase. S96 asked R47 for "a froxel density source at each plunge ... a column of droplets rising from the foot to about the fall's height ... its density scaling with the impact speed's square" ([flowing-water](flowing-water.md#mist-a-request-to-r47)); it is not built. What a fall hands the medium, in the plumes' own terms: a **line source along the landing line** (its ends, the discharge broken to fines, the outflow's speed and direction at the foot, the ambient wind to add), shed as a plume that leaves outwards and downwind, rises a little and decays by settling (seconds for the splash, tens for the fines), white and forward-scattering where the dust is tan; and, for a tall fall, the **lower veil itself** past the point where its drops are at terminal speed, as a curtain source in place of geometry.

### Unsteadiness

- **Pulses.** The flow over a natural lip arrives turbulent, and its largest eddies are about a depth across and pass in a depth over the speed. The shots give clumps a quarter of the fall wide that swell over about a second of footage, and hold their shape for 0.3 s. A surge of the whole lip (roll waves on the steep approach) would put neighbouring patches in step, and they are not.
- **The veil's sway** is the air's: once the water is drops, a gust of speed U moves a drop sideways by U (T - τ_d (1 - e^(-T/τ_d))) in a time T aloft, τ_d = v_t / g (0.7 to 0.9 s for drops of 2 to 5 mm), which for 3 m/s over 2 s is 4 m. A fall that is whole to its foot does not sway; one whose lower half is drops swings by metres with the gusts, at the gusts' period of seconds, which is `falls-locked`.
- **Nappe oscillation**, the hum and the horizontal bands of a weir's sheet, is a resonance between a thin smooth nappe and the air shut in behind it: 5 to 12 Hz on fountains falling 0.4 to 0.8 m, at frequencies of a whole number and a quarter over the fall time (Casperson 1993 and Lodomez et al. 2018, by a search's summary; the open copy returned a cookie wall). A natural lip is rough and its cavity open at the sides, so it is not drawn.

### Which falls occur where

- **By discharge and drop.** With d_c for the discharge and H for the drop: under 3 d_c a step is drowned in its own tailwater (a hole); from there to the break-up length (4 to 10 d_c for a natural lip) the sheet is whole to its foot, a **block** where it is wider than high and a **curtain** where higher; past it the foot is strands and drops; past it by tens of metres the lower fall is spray at terminal speed, a **ribbon** that sways, and on the highest falls with little water nothing reaches the foot but mist. Wikipedia's list (read) is the same set seen by shape: ledge (block, classical, curtain), plunge ("completely losing contact"), horsetail ("mostly maintains contact"; chute and fan), cascade, punchbowl, tiered, segmented.
- **By the rock.** The classical fall is a resistant bed over a weak one: the splash and the pool's eddies cut the weak bed back into an alcove, the cap stands as an overhang, the water falls clear, and blocks of the cap lie in the pool (Gilbert 1890's Niagara; Wikipedia's account, read, with retreat "as high as one-and-a-half metres per year"). It is not the only kind: a stability analysis found Niagara's cap would hold an undercut of tens of metres where the notch is at most ten, and many falls in uniform rock have no undercut at all and retreat by drilling one pool after another (Hayakawa and Matsukura; Scheingross et al. 2017; search summaries). Athabasca's cap is quartzite over limestone (Wikipedia). A **hanging valley** is the other common origin: a tributary's floor left high above a glacial trough.
- **What the solvers know.** The formation gives every bed a resistance, and a resistant member already "stands as a riser at 80°" over a weak one that "lies back" ([rocks](../subsystems/rocks.md)); the landform solver cuts a hanging valley's step where a tributary meets the trunk's section ([landform](../subsystems/landform.md)); the channels solver reads **knickpoints from the ground's profile** (a run falling at 45° or more over three widths and dropping a metre: [channels](../subsystems/channels.md#reaches)) and lays steps with 38° risers on step-pool reaches precisely so that "no riser is steep enough for the water to leave its bed". So the engine already has falls in the right places for two of the three reasons, and its step-pools stay steps: 3.91 d_c tan² 38° is 2.4 d_c, under the three of a fall.

## The design

### What it rests on

**A channel's flow is steady** (S99: "a channel's level is static"; S103 rests on the same), so a fall's path, its thickness along the path, where it breaks and where it lands are functions of the place: the cook computes them once. What moves is the texture the sheet carries, its pulses and its sway, and each is a function of the world's clock at a period the flow or the wind gives. **Nothing is stored from frame to frame**, and nothing needs to be. What would need state, and is left out: a fall that swells after rain, foam that collects in a pool's corner, rock that dries when the wind turns the spray away.

**The same bytes from every toolchain.** The cook's march is + - x ÷ and a square root: the ballistic path in closed form, the drops' slowing by a stepped integration of v dv/ds = g (1 - v²/v_t²) and not its exponential, the break-up length a constant times d_c for each of three turbulence classes (so no power is taken at the cook), and the tests are on integer millimetres. The exponentials and the noise are the shader's.

**Three places, as S103 divides them**: the **cook** (CPU, once: the march, the sheet's mesh, the fall's record), the **GPU scene** (the record beside the channel's material, as a body's plunges ride beside its material today), the **pixel** (closed forms).

### Where a fall is

Each column of the ribbon at a knickpoint is marched on its own, with its own share of the discharge (the 3/2 power of its head over the lip, normalised to the section's), down the ground's profile at the lattice's spacing:

1. **On the bed**: S101's march (the energy equation against friction) gives the speed. The column **leaves** at the first sample where the parabola launched there, along the bed, at that speed, lies above the ground at the next sample: the bed has fallen away faster than a projectile would. On a rounded crest that happens part of the way round, which is where the lip is.
2. **In the air**: the parabola, until it meets the ground's profile or the level of the water below (a body's level, or the channel's own surface past the foot). The flight's drop is H.
3. **The test.** A flight is a **fall** when **H is at least 3 d_c** (more than half its back is air, by Rand's standing pool) **and its discharge is a sheet's** (d_c at least 1 cm, 3 l/s for each metre of lip: a choice above the curtain's 0.15 l/s, since a film that thin goes round a rounded lip). The face's angle needs no rule of its own: it is in where the parabola lands (y_r = 3.91 d_c tan² θ for a plane face). A flight shorter than 3 d_c stays what it is today, a riser of the ribbon with S101's jump and S103's hole. A column under the sheet's discharge is **wet rock**: no geometry, the face under it saturated through S99's record.
4. **Landing**: in water, a plunge (below, the foot). On rock, the column keeps the part of its speed along the surface and goes on as step 1, down the face (a horsetail, with S101's chute for its speed, depth and air) or along a tread to the next lip (a tiered fall); the part of its speed into the rock is the splash's.

So one march gives the plunge, the horsetail, the tiered fall, the segmented fall and the wet face, and **the same lip changes kind with the discharge** as real ones do.

**What the description must carry.** The water capability may not read the channels capability (ADR-0027) and the description is their contract. `engine.scene.WaterChannel` gains, in its next version, a **`falls`** list written by the channels solver for each marked knickpoint (`ChannelLink::knick`):

| Field | What | Why the water cannot find it |
|---|---|---|
| the lip's station and the foot's | where along the points the knickpoint begins and ends | the points are a metre or more apart and carry the ground's fall, not its shape |
| `profile` | the ground's height under the line at the lattice's spacing (0.25 m), from two widths above the lip to two past the foot, in millimetres | the leave and land tests need the face at the lattice, not at the points |
| `lip` | the lip's height across the width at nine or more places | a segmented fall, a notch, where the sheet is thick |
| `discharge_m3s` | at the lip | the section gives a speed and a depth for a bankfull bed; the fall needs the water |
| `tail` | what the foot is: a body's name; the channel's own pool with its level and depth; rock | S101 and S103 already ask for a tread's own depth; the pool's depth is the white water's life |
| `lip_reach` | the reach's code above the lip (it is in `reach` already) | the turbulence class: 0.03 above a pool-riffle or a plane bed, 0.05 above a bedrock chute, 0.08 above a step-pool or a cascade (choices; L_b 10.5, 6.9 and 4.7 d_c) |

The alternative is for the water generator to sample the ground itself, as it does for a body's depths ([water](../subsystems/water.md#the-mesh)), and take only the stations, the discharge and the tail from the solver; the channels row chooses. **Asked of the solvers beyond the list**: a plunge pool cut into the ground at a fall's foot, with a depth (the landform's or the channels'); the overhang and the alcove where a resistant bed caps a weak one, as rock (the cliffs generator's: the terrain is a height field and holds no overhang; the water needs neither to fall clear, since the path is the water's own, but a camera behind the fall does).

**The ribbon above and below.** Above, S98's brink stands: the ribbon speeds up to v_b over four critical depths and, once S103's third stage puts heights in the vertices, draws down to 0.715 d_c. Its last row is the lip's, and the sheet's first row is the same positions. The ribbon's own triangles through the flight are not made. Below, the ribbon is the pool's surface from the face outwards, **under** the sheet and behind it; the sheet meets it at the landing line, which for a 10 m fall of 0.3 m²/s is 2.9 m out from the face, where today's plunge sits at the face's foot. S98's plunge stretch (a decay over 0.4 h) gives way to S101's march started from the landing's jet and air.

### What is drawn

**The sheet's mesh.** One mesh a fall, cooked as the ribbon is and of its layout: rows along the path, close together at the lip where it curves (a quarter of the brink depth) and up to a metre apart lower down, columns the ribbon's own at the lip; 4,096 vertices a fall at the most (a choice). A vertex carries what a ribbon's does and means the same by it: **UV.x the travel time** from the channel's head, continued through the flight (so the phase's gradient is still the speed and direction, the resolve's `water_channel_flow` unchanged), **UV.y the water's thickness** there, q over the speed (a body's "depth under the vertex"), and the sheet's normal. The distance fallen is the lip's height less the pixel's, the lane's discharge the thickness times the speed: no new attribute. The surface follows the upper nappe for the first two brink depths, where the sheet's thickness is seen from the side, with its two edges closed by a strip, and the centre line after; it is two-sided where the landing is more than a metre out (a camera can stand behind it). Columns that are wet rock are left out, so a segmented fall has gaps in its mesh.

**Past the break-up length** the mesh widens by 1.75% of the distance fallen each side to ψ 2 and by 9% a side past it (`falls-locked`'s two halves; choices from one shot with no scale) and, where the scene has a wind, by the sway's reach: it is the **envelope** of where the veil can be, and the coverage puts the water inside it. That keeps the instance rigid (a deformed one would lose its cones and its cached cluster structures, and the sway would be paid in the pool every frame). On a slide the water is on the bed and the ribbon is kept.

**Coverage.** The pixel has the distance fallen s, the thickness t, the speed v, its place across the lip a and the phase φ (UV.x less the clock). With ψ = s / L_b:

| Part | Where | Coverage | Carried pattern |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sheet | ψ under 0.5 | 1 | none in the coverage; in the normal, streaks across at the lobes' spacing |
| Strands | ψ from 0.5 to 2 | √(4 t m / π λ), at most 1, with **λ = 2.5 d_c** between 5 cm and 3 m (the lobes of `falls-wide`; a choice from one shot) | a ridged noise in (a / λ, φ / T_p): strands that are born at the lip, hold their place across it and stretch down the fall as the phase's gradient shrinks |
| Drops | ψ over 1, faded in to 2 | 1 - e^(-1.5 t_w / d), t_w the water's thickness at the drops' slowed speed and d = 4 mm (between the 3.7 mm a 14 m/s drop can bear and rain's cap; a choice) | grain drawn out along the fall by v / 50 (the eye's and a camera's blur; a choice), so drops read as short streaks |

So 0.3 m²/s falling ten metres stays opaque to its foot (a 2 cm sheet's worth of 4 mm drops covers 99.9%), and 0.01 m²/s is a third covered by then: a small fall turns to lace and a large one does not, with no switch. Every pattern goes through the running-water row's band limit: a strand narrower than two of the pixel's footprints is its mean coverage, so the far fall is a steady fraction and never a shimmer.

**What is covered is water of two kinds**: glassy for ψ under 0.1 (the tier's own surface over a depth of t: the lip is the one place a fall is a clear sheet) and white from there, whole by ψ 0.5 (S98's foam: a diffuse surface of albedo 0.8 under the sun, one shadow query, and the sky).

**What is not covered is what is behind**, and here the engine's shape decides the method. A partly covered surface in a visibility buffer is usually an alpha test, and in the traced structures an alpha test is a candidate loop at every hit: the forest's resolve was 39.5 ms under traced shadows against 5.0 under the maps at 11520 x 2160, the difference the leaves' masks, and it took the opacity micromaps and their two-state answer, over three stages, to bring the sun's rays from 14.8 ms to about 5 ([renderer](../subsystems/renderer.md#opacity-micromaps)). A micromap is baked once from a mask that does not move; a fall's coverage moves with the clock, so every candidate on a fall would be "unknown" and run the loop. So **the sheet is opaque geometry** everywhere it is rasterized or traced, one id a pixel, the same under `--raster rt` as under the rasterizers, and the pixel **composites**: coverage times the water, plus what is left times the surface found by one ray from the sheet's point along the view, which is the ray a channel's water already traces for its bed ([water](../subsystems/water.md#what-is-under-the-water); S101's third pass made it trace everything as solid first and take a terrain tile at its word). The rock behind is shaded as the bed is, wet. The ray is skipped where coverage is over 0.97, and where the fall is too far to matter (below). A device that does not trace, and the low tier, blend to the face's mean colour from the record. The cost of the choice: a strand has no silhouette of its own against the rock (it is a coverage, not an edge), and the pixel's depth is the sheet's even where the rock shows.

**The sheet's shadow.** A lacy sheet should cast a lacy shadow. First: the triangles whose cooked mean coverage is at least a half cast, the rest do not (a static flag a triangle, nothing in a ray's loop). Later, if the eye asks: a fall's triangle as a candidate in the masked shadow loop with the coverage as its mask, reached only by rays that cross a fall.

**The texture's speed.** Every pattern is a function of the phase, so it moves at the water's own speed by construction and **stretches** as the speed grows, where S98's flow map was built so that a reach's detail would not stretch. The phase wraps every 8 s as the ribbon's does, and the patterns are periodic in it.

**Pulses.** The thickness is multiplied by m = 1 + 0.3 n(a / 2.5 d_c, φ / T_p, clock / T_p) with **T_p = 2 √(d_c / g)**, twice the lip's large eddy (0.64 s at d_c 1 m, 0.2 s at 0.1 m): thicker clumps a few depths across leave the lip, fall as shapes, lengthen, and have changed by the time they have fallen their own length (the third argument), which is the matcher's 0.3 s; a noise of that cell swells once in about two cells, the shots' one a second. The 0.3 and the 2 are choices held to those two measurements. More water in a clump is more coverage in the strands, so the lace breathes.

**The sway.** Past the break-up length the veil's centre is moved across by the wind: δ = U (T - τ_d (1 - e^(-T / τ_d))) with U the wind record's component across the fall half a flight ago, T the time the drops have been falling (the phase's difference from the break-up's), τ_d = 0.8 s. The foot lags the middle as a gust passes, so the veil swings and does not slide. The component along the view moves the landing line, and the foot's source reads the same function. A scene with no wind has no sway; a whole sheet never has one.

**The foot is a source.** The cook writes the landing as a segment with the jet's speed and thickness there and the pool's depth D under it.

- **In a channel**: the segment starts S101's march in place of the riser's foot: the toe's air at its ceiling (0.3 for a sheet; 0.5 where the fall arrives broken, since it brings its air with it: a choice), rising out by that row's law at the pool's own speed and depth.
- **In a body**: `gfx::WaterPlunge` becomes a segment with a reach **R_f = √(R₀² + 2 v₀ R₀ D / U_t)**: the foam's travel in a radial outflow v₀ R₀ / r during the bubbles' rise D / U_t, with R₀ the plume's half width at the surface (half the jet's thickness plus D tan 12.5°), v₀ the tenth of the impact speed S98 already uses, at most 1.5 m/s, and U_t 0.22 m/s (derived here from the grounding's two numbers). For 10 m into 1.5 m of water that is 2.6 m, where today's rule (a quarter of the height) gives 2.5: the rule was near, and now it follows the pool's depth and the jet.
- **Rings and the boil**: in the body's normal only, waves leaving the segment at the pulses' period (wavelength g T_p² / 2π, falling off as the root of the distance) and a dome over the plume. A choice of look; the last of the foot's stage.
- **On rock**: the ribbon restarts at the landing with the speed along the surface, thin and white, and S99's bank record takes a **splash reach** of v_s² / g with v_s 0.3 of the impact speed, at most 5 m (a choice: no ejection speed was found), in place of today's "half its drop, at most 3 m".

**Wet rock.** Three sources, all through S99's record and none new in kind: the face under a column too thin to be a sheet; the face behind a plunge and either side of it within the splash reach and the sway's; the ground round a foot on rock. The same zones, as a mask, are what the cover solver reads for moss and what the placements read for a spray cliff's plants: a request to those rows, with the grounding's two zones (splash: metres, wet; mist: tens of metres downwind, damp).

**The mist** is R47's, fed by the landing segment as a plume source and by a tall fall's lower veil (the grounding has the terms). Where the veil is handed over, the sheet's mesh ends: past the point where the drops are at terminal speed and coverage is under a tenth, a curtain of air and water is a medium and not a surface.

**The far form.** A fall is seen from kilometres as a white line that does not flicker. The cluster DAG takes the sheet down to a few quads; the band limit has already made its coverage a steady mean; the ray is dropped where the fall's width is under about eight pixels and the record's face colour stands in. What the engine has no rule for is **a mesh thinner than a pixel** (a 1 m fall at 3 km is 0.8 px at 3840 across 90°; a far river has the same trouble): rasterized as it is, it breaks into dashes. The recommendation is that the channels solver also writes each fall into the ground's far colour as a streak of its mean coverage, so that the far terrain carries the line and the mesh may drop out under two pixels; widening the coarsest clusters to hold a pixel is the alternative. Open, and the far stage's first question.

## Built: stages 0 and 1

(2026-11-12, night, batch 63.) The fall test and the cook, in `domain/channels` and no shader:
[channels](../subsystems/channels.md#where-the-water-leaves-its-bed-s104) has the rules, the file's
layout and the reader. What the build decided against this page's design, and why:

- **The `falls` list is a file beside the plan, not a field of `WaterChannel`.** A column's path
  is tens of vertices and a lip nine columns, so a fall is kilobytes (about 11.7 KB a run, and
  0.83 ms a run to cook on one thread: the channels page's "Measured"); the
  description is text an author pastes. The cook writes `falls-<plan>-<options>.channel-falls` under
  the plan's own directory with `k_falls_version` of its own, and the plan, its format and
  `ChannelLayer` do not move. The water may not link `domain/channels` (ADR-0027), so the layout is
  the contract, given byte by byte on the channels page with `read_falls` as the reference reader;
  how the water finds the file for a block (the plan's key on the block, or a scene field) is the
  water row's to choose.
- **The march reads the plan's cut bed, not the ground.** The profile is `ChannelLink::bed` at the
  line's points (0.5 m) and linear between, as the cut is, not the lattice's 0.25 m: the bed is what
  the tile set cuts and the water stands in, it is known without the ground, and the same plan cooks
  the same bytes wherever it was made. Each column's bed is the cut's section risen at its place
  across the line. The leave and land tests are exact against that polyline (a quadratic's root a
  stretch), so a vertical step sampled as one stretch of half a metre lands where the closed form
  says.
- **Every link is tested.** The forest's island streams over their sea cliff drain under the
  description's threshold and carry no water block (the W117 finding of the night); the cook reads
  every link of the plan and says which falls the description draws.
- **The fall test is on runs of the bed's own steps**, the stretches the cut makes fall at 45
  degrees: where the solver put a fall (a knickpoint) or failed to spread a drop (`steep_left`).
  The test itself is this page's: three critical depths of flight and a centimetre of critical
  depth.
- **What a stage 0 and 1 cook does not do yet**: the drops' slowing past the break-up length (stage
  2: the cook carries L_b by the reach's turbulence class, **K = 0.77 being this page's own fit**,
  and the free jet's path to the landing, so a fall taller than its break-up length plus a few
  metres lands faster here than it will); the brink's draw-down over four critical depths (S103's
  heights in the ribbon); a plunge pool's depth (the plan's water at the foot is `stage` of the
  thalweg, so `tail_depth` is that, not a pool's); air drag on the sheet (a hundredth of g while it
  is whole, above).
- **Nothing is pinned from a frame.** The shots' measurements above were made on the first cut of
  the sheets, before they were re-cut by source frame on 2026-11-12; none of them enters stages 0
  and 1 (the break-up length's K is fitted to Horeni's number, not to a frame; the veil's widening
  and the pulses are stage 2's and 5's). They are to be measured again on the re-cut set before a
  stage pins one. Looked at again tonight, shapes only: `falls-locked` is a tiered fall, an upper
  fall from the notch onto a ledge about a fifth of the lower fall's height, the lower veil widening
  and landing on rock, then a cascade off the ledge — the march's tiered kind.

**Sources read again tonight** for the three things the cook decides by: the brink's 0.715 and its
conditions (a subcritical approach in a horizontal or mild rectangular channel, atmospheric
pressure on both faces of the nappe, the pressure leaving hydrostatic three to four critical depths
upstream) from Chanson's account of the free overfall in IAHR's media library (2021; the page,
read whole, gives 0.71 measured and 0.715 from theory "verified experimentally", and cites Rouse
1936 and Henderson 1966), with Rouse 1936 itself still not opened (a search's summary of later
papers names it as the source of 0.715 for a horizontal rectangular channel); the free jet as a
projectile from the brink's depth and speed (a search's summary of drop-box papers: the free-falling
nappe's trajectory "works" from the 0.715 brink for a subcritical approach, and a supercritical
approach needs its own brink condition, which is S98's "over as it is" that the cook follows), with
Wahl, Frizell and Cohen 2008 (*J. Hydraul. Eng.* 134(2), 256-260, on a commonly cited jet
trajectory equation being wrong for a sloped launch) not opened (the publisher refused the fetch):
the cook's form is the projectile's in the launch's own slope, x = v cos θ t, y = v sin θ t + g t²/2,
derived and held to it by the tests to rounding; and the critical depth d_c = (q² / g)^(1/3), the
definition in every open-channel text (Henderson 1966; from memory). **The break-up length**,
which the cook carries and does not decide by, was looked for again: Castillo's papers still
refused the fetch (CORE and MDPI 403, two `upct.es` copies 404), and a search's summaries of them
now say more than this page's first reading did — the rectangular nappe's form is written with
φ = K_φ T_u, **K_φ about 1.24** for a three-dimensional nappe where this page took Ervine's 1.14
from the round jet, and the fitted **K is about 0.85 at T_u 0.012 (an inclined crest) and 0.95 at
0.013 (a flat top)**, with 0.40 to 0.80 suggested elsewhere. Taken together those give a break-up
length about 15 % longer than `k_breakup_depths` at T_u 0.05 (8.0 against 6.9 critical depths).
The three multiples stay **this page's fit** (K = 0.77 with 1.14) until a copy opens; stage 2,
which draws by them, is where they are pinned or replaced.

## What to ask of the next reels

The three fall shots gave one law (free fall below the crest) and almost no length. What would give the rest, most useful first:

1. **A scale and a clock in every shot**: a person at the lip or the foot, a staff, a known bridge; the footage at real time, said so, or with its frame rate.
2. **A fall from the side, the camera still**: where the water leaves the lip, how far out it lands, the two faces of the sheet at the lip, the air behind it. No shot shows a fall's path.
3. **The foot, from above or the side, for twenty seconds**: how wide the white is against the fall, how far downstream it lasts, the boil, the rings; one foot in a pool and one on rock.
4. **One fall of ten to thirty metres, square on, still, ten seconds, not smoothed**: the heights at which it is whole, strands and drops; the period of a pulse; the period of the sway.
5. **The same fall at two discharges** (before and after rain, or a spring and a late-summer reel of one place): whether it changes kind, and how the lace opens.
6. **A horsetail**, close enough for its texture's speed to be read, beside a free fall of about the same height.
7. **A step of half a metre to two metres from the side**, with the water's depth above it in view: the drop at which air appears behind the sheet.
8. **A thin curtain over a ledge, close**: the strands' spacing, where the film clings and where it leaves.
9. **A tall thin fall in wind**: how far the veil moves and how fast.
10. **A fall from one to five kilometres**: what the eye is given at the far form.
11. **The mist, lit from behind, from the side**: the plume's height and reach, the wet band on the rock, what grows in it.
12. **From behind a fall**, if any reel has it.
