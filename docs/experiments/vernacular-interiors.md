# Vernacular house plans, stairs, openings and hearths: the grounding for interiors and the rural filler (G66, G72)

Written 2026-11-14, before the interiors' generator and the rural filler were designed
([dwellings](../subsystems/dwellings.md); plan 14
[§14.3](../plan/14-structures.md#the-fillers) and [§14.4](../plan/14-structures.md#144-walkability-and-collision)).
It answers four questions: which plans a small rural house takes and how big its rooms are; what
stair a person climbs; where doors and windows sit in a room; where the hearth and its stack stand.

**How the sources were read.** Every web source below was read through a fetch tool that returns a
model's summary of the page, not its text, so "read whole" means the whole page was put to the
question, not that a person's eyes crossed every line. Every PDF tried came back unreadable
(Approved Document K itself, Brunskill's Cumbria paper, the Norfolk Historic Buildings Group
glossary, Alcock's Devon paper), and Historic England's *Domestic 1: Vernacular Houses* refused the
fetch (403). **Nothing here was read in Brunskill's handbook itself**, which is the standard work on
plan families; the plan types below are the ones the open pages describe. A row marked *snippet* was
seen only in a search result's summary and is a lead, not a source. A row marked **authored** is this
page's own number: no opened source gave it.

## Plan types

| Type | What the sources say | Source, and how far read |
|---|---|---|
| One room (the cot) | A single cell, entered directly, a fire against an end wall. A cruck cottage is one or two bays. Poplar Cottage (c. 1630–50): a heated room and an unheated service room below, two chambers above, a smoke bay about 4 ft long at the gable, a chimney built into the bay later. | designingbuildings.co.uk, *Cruck* (whole); wealddown.co.uk, *Poplar Cottage* (whole); an archaeology report on a single-cell Swaledale cottage (*snippet*) |
| Hall and parlour (two units) | The **hall** is the larger, general room and may be cooked in; the **parlour** the smaller and more private, commonly slept in. An interior door joins them. The outer door opens into the hall. A loft, where there is one, is reached by a stair or ladder **in the hall**. Colonial American houses of the type are 16–20 ft (5–6 m) deep and 20–40 ft (6–12 m) wide, often one and a half storeys, with gable-end chimneys in the south and a central one in the north. | en.wikipedia.org, *Hall and parlor house* (whole, twice: by the research pass and again for this page) |
| Two rooms, Scotland | The **but** is the outer room, kitchen or antechamber; the **ben** the inner, a bedroom. No sizes. | en.wikipedia.org, *But and ben* (whole) |
| Cross-passage, and the longhouse | Two opposed outer doors make a passage across the house; the hall is on its upper side with the hearth at the hall's lower end, the service end (or, in a longhouse, the byre) on its lower side; an inner room may lie beyond the hall. Stacks replace open hearths from the mid-16th century. By 1700 most longhouses had an upper storey, a stair and a chimney. Measured: Emsworthy 15.0 × 5.0 m **internal**, walls 0.75–0.85 m; Dinna Clerk 14.63 × 3.66 m; Houndtor 1, 14.2 × 4.3 m overall with a hall of about 6 × 4 m. | en.wikipedia.org, *Hall house* and *Longhouse* (whole); dartmoorexplorations.co.uk, *Emsworthy* and *Dinna Clerk* (whole); legendarydartmoor.co.uk, *Long house* (whole). A passage 9 ft wide at Beetor (*snippet*, the listing page refused) |
| Lobby entry | From the late 16th century: two or three cells, the door opening onto the side of an axial stack with back-to-back fireplaces, the stair beside or opposite the stack. | *snippets* only (a Brunskill-related chapter, a county record's thesaurus, the NHBG glossary). **Not built**: see below |
| Two-up two-down | Two rooms on each floor. The early byelaw terrace: a front living room and a back room with **the stair between them, parallel to the street**; later the stair turned and a hall was added. Whittaker's Cottages (Ashtead, 1860s): each 12 ft wide and 20 ft long, the front room with the only ground-floor fireplace, the unheated back room for storing and preparing food. | en.wikipedia.org, *Byelaw terraced house* and *Two-up two-down* (whole); wealddown.co.uk, *Whittaker's Cottages* (a search result's quotation of the page) |

**What structure sets.** A cruck bay is 12–16 ft (3.6–4.8 m); a small cottage one or two bays, a
family house three or four (designingbuildings, *Cruck*, whole). A cruck house is "usually no more
than" 6 m (20 ft) wide (buildinghistory.org, *Vernacular*, whole); a cruck truss about 18 ft deep
(legendarydartmoor). So a house is **one room deep** and its clear depth lies between 3.66 m and
5.0 m in the measured longhouses, to 5 or 6 m overall in the hall-and-parlour figures.

**Households.** The mean household of pre-industrial England is 4.75 or a little under (Laslett,
*snippet*: the paper would not open). Used only to centre the filler's table on a household of four
or five in a hall and parlour.

## The stair

| Quantity | Value | Source, and how far read |
|---|---|---|
| Private stair, rise | 150–220 mm | Approved Document K as given by designingbuildings.co.uk, *Stairs* (whole). The document itself would not open |
| Private stair, going | 220–300 mm | the same |
| Pitch | at most 42° | the same, and two stair makers' guides (whole) |
| Twice the rise plus the going | 550–700 mm | the same |
| Headroom | 2.0 m; a loft stair 1.9 m at the centre and 1.8 m at the side | the same; self-build.co.uk (most of the page) |
| Width | no minimum for a private stair; about 860 mm clear suggested | dabstairs.com (whole) |
| A winder's going | measured 270 mm from the narrow end | the same |
| IRC R311.7 | riser at most 7¾ in, tread at least 10 in | a stair maker's code guide (whole). Headroom 6 ft 8 in and width 36 in are *snippets* |
| Blondel | twice the riser plus the tread is a step's length | en.wikipedia.org, *Stairs* (characters 20,000 to 120,000 of 161,000). The page gives no figure for the length; the 630 mm commonly quoted was not confirmed |
| Historic cottage stairs | steep, with little headroom; an 18th-century attic stair winds in the space where a stack narrows, with no handrail; a ladder or companionway to a solar | buildingconservation.com, *Timber staircases* (whole); *Hall house* |
| The walker (the engine's) | riser at most 0.25 m (style default 0.20), going at least 0.28 m, headroom at least 2.1 m, a doorway at least 0.8 m by 2.0 m | plan 14 §14.4 |

**The rule built.** A going of **280 mm** (the walker's least, inside Approved Document K's range) and
a riser of **at most 210 mm**: at 280 mm of going, 210 mm is the largest rise that keeps twice the
rise plus the going at 700 mm, and it gives a pitch of 36.9°, under 42°. The risers are the storey's
height over 210 mm, rounded up: 2.4 m floor to floor is twelve risers of exactly 200 mm, the plan's
style default. Headroom is the plan's 2.1 m, 0.1 m over the regulation's.

**What that costs, and what was traded.** The historic stair is a winder beside the stack or a
ladder: steeper than a walker's and turning on treads that taper to nothing. The walker's 0.28 m
going at the walking line and the collision proxy's 5 cm cut hold neither, so **the stair built is a
straight enclosed flight between two walls**, which is also what the early two-up two-down had. Twelve
risers run 3.08 m, and with a level landing of a metre at each end (**authored**: a doorway of 0.84 m
beside it and 8 cm of jamb each side) the stair's strip is 5.08 m long. A house with an upper floor
is therefore **at least 5.1 m clear in the direction its stair runs**: Emsworthy's 5.0 m, the top of
the measured range, where Whittaker's Cottages are 12 ft (3.66 m). The filler builds upper floors
only on houses that deep; a smaller house is one storey. Lofts under the roof (the one-and-a-half
storey house the sources call common) are **not built**: a loft stair needs the roof's slope in the
headroom rule, and that is a row of its own.

| Quantity | Built | From |
|---|---|---|
| Where | a cot and a hall: against the gable, under the ridge. A cross-passage hall: **backing onto the passage**. A two-up two-down: the front room, on a side wall. ## What is not built, and why

- **The lobby-entry house.** Its stair is a winder against the stack; with the walker's stair it
  becomes a different house. It wants the winder (a tapered tread the collision proxy holds) first.
- **Lofts and half storeys**, as above.
- **The byre end.** A longhouse's lower room is built as a service room: a shippon's drain, its wide
  doorway and its beasts are a game's.
- **Furniture** (plan 14's G66 names it): no row here grounds a bed's or a table's place.
