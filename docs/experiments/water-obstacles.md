# Running water meets its ground and its obstacles: the grounding and the design for S103

Roadmap S103 (2026-11-12). **A page, not code**: nothing in the tree changes with it. The owner's words that started it: "waves, streams, water in general needs to conform to the terrain it's on. ... we might not be able to do the full computation in budget, but it's the general idea we should be aiming for. and if we have water physics like that across the board, with the effects of gravity, surface tension, friction, obstacles, etc. i think that helps in all water instances." And of the reference reels: "how river/stream water should move, especially around obstacles. ... obstacles becomes a function of the wave or stream strength, depth, and how it hits the obstacle." The bodies' half of that (a wave from deep water to the top of a beach) is S102's. This is the channels' half.

**Where the engine stands.** The channels solver knows, for every half metre of every reach, the discharge, the slope, the section (width, depth, mean speed), the bed's grain sizes (D16 to D84) and where every clast lies ([channels](../subsystems/channels.md#bed-sediment)). The water drawn over that is a ribbon with a speed and an air content marched along its line, a standing relief and an advected detail ([water](../subsystems/water.md#channels); the running-water row S101, whose pages are on a branch of its own as this is written). What the ribbon has no notion of is **the thing in the way**: a boulder, a step, a bar, a constriction, a bend, a bank. S101's own list of what a person still objects to says it: "no white water behind a boulder, because the ribbon does not know where one stands".

**The design in one sentence.** A steady flow meeting a fixed obstacle makes a **standing** form, so everything an obstacle does to the surface is computed once: the cook keeps the clasts that stand at least a quarter of the local depth, writes each as a small record into a grid over the channel, moves the ribbon's vertices by the closed forms below where a form is taller than a few centimetres, and a channel's pixel evaluates the same closed forms for its normal, the velocity its texture is carried by and its air from the few records its cell lists; nothing is kept from frame to frame.

The page has four parts: [what the shots show](#what-the-shots-show), [the grounding](#the-grounding), [the design](#the-design), [what to ask of the next reels](#what-to-ask-of-the-next-reels). How far each source was read is said where it is used and again in [the sources](#the-sources-and-how-far-each-was-read): most of the primary papers are behind a paywall or refused the fetch, and nothing here presents an abstract as a paper.

**What the shots settle, with no number needed.** (1) White water is anchored: it stands behind a stone, at a step's foot, at a constriction, and the water between is clear. (2) The forms stand and the texture runs through them. (3) An obstacle's mark depends on how deep it lies: out of the water, a wake with white in it; just under, a breaking crest over it; deep, nothing. (4) Foam outlives the form that made it by seconds and travels: it marks where the water has been. (5) At distance all of it is texture. **What they could not settle**: any wavelength against a speed, a wake's angle, a pillow's height or width (no shot looks at an upstream face), a hole's depth, and any time in real seconds.

## The grounding

Symbols: U the approach speed (the depth's mean at the obstacle's place), d the depth there, k the obstacle's height over the bed, D its width across the flow, g gravity. Fr = U / √(g d). The **relative submergence** is d / k.

### The approach flow's regime

- **The Froude number** separates flow that a wave can travel up (Fr < 1, subcritical: an obstacle is felt upstream of itself) from flow that sweeps every wave down (Fr > 1, supercritical: an obstacle is felt only inside a wedge behind it). The **critical depth** of a discharge q a metre of width is d_c = (q²/g)^(1/3), where the specific energy E = d + U²/2g is least and equal to 1.5 d_c. Textbook (Chow 1959; Henderson 1966); the engine has used both since S98.
- **Uniform flow** is Manning's, v = d^(2/3) S^(1/2) / n, which the ribbon's march starts from ([water](../subsystems/water.md#the-ribbon)). In water only a few grains deep Manning's n is not a constant: resistance rises steeply as d / D84 falls below about four. Keulegan's (1938) logarithmic law, U / u* = 6.25 + 5.75 log10(R / k_s), is the deep form; Ferguson's (2007) variable-power equation joins it to the shallow form, √(8/f) = a1 a2 (R/D84) / √(a1² + a2² (R/D84)^(5/3)) with a1 = 6.5 and a2 = 2.5 (**the constants from memory**; a search found the paper's record and no equation). On a step-pool or cascade most of the resistance is not grain friction at all but **spill**: tumbling over steps and the jumps behind them (Comiti et al. 2007; Wilcox and Wohl 2006: abstracts by search). That is why the march caps a mobile bed's mean flow at the critical speed (Grant 1997, on the running-water page).
- **Across a section** the speed follows the depth (the ribbon's 1/6 power law of the local depth since S98); **down a profile** it follows the logarithmic law, so the surface runs about 1/0.85 of the mean (the hydrographer's float coefficient; from memory). The stagnation forms below use the surface speed U_s = U / 0.85, because it is the surface water that meets the rock's face.

### An obstacle, by how deep it lies and how fast the water is

**The published classification.** Shamloo, Rajaratnam and Katopodis (2001, *J. Hydraulic Research* 39, 351) put hemispheres on a flume's bed and found "four different regimes of flow ... classified based on the relative depth" d/h, which they varied from about 0.6 to 4.3, with behind the body "a recirculation region (closed wake) with a length of about 2D" and an open turbulent wake after it (the abstract, read on IAHR's library page; the paper is members-only). The regimes' boundaries are second hand, from a later paper's summary found by search (its own page refused the fetch): above about **4** the surface does not feel the body; between **1.3 and 4** surface waves appear; between **1 and 1.3** the shear layer mixes the whole depth; below **1** the body stands out of the water. Tsakiris's thesis on a single spherical boulder (University of Iowa; the abstract read) agrees on the wake: a recirculation 1.4 diameters long behind a fully submerged boulder, a smaller one behind a barely submerged one, and **none** behind a partly submerged one, whose shallow wake is "a Kármán vortex street resulting from the transverse shear layers shed from the boulder flanks"; in all three a weak horseshoe vortex and a pair of trailing vortices, and two to three times the turbulent energy. For arrays, Papanicolaou et al. (2001) took spacings of 6, 2 and 1 diameters for isolated, wake-interference and skimming flow (a search's summary, not the paper).

Those boundaries are a flume's, at its Froude numbers. What the surface does needs the speed as well, and the classical theory supplies it. By regime:

**Deeply submerged (d/k above about 4, or the crest's own Froude number small).** The waves a submerged body raises on a stream fall off as e^(-κ f), with κ = g/U² and f the depth of water over the body (Lamb, *Hydrodynamics* §247, the submerged cylinder, whose train has the amplitude 4π κ a² e^(-κ f) for a cylinder of radius a: **from memory**, not opened). κ f = g (d - k) / U² is one over the square of a Froude number of the **water over the crest**, Fr_c = U / √(g (d - k)), and that one number orders the whole submerged range: at Fr_c = 0.36 the factor is 0.0004, at 0.7 it is 0.13, at 1 it is 0.37. So a stone a metre under a pool makes nothing (the slab in `riffles-and-pools`), and the same stone under a fast deep run makes long smooth standing waves. What a deep obstacle does send up is its wake's turbulence, as **boils**: patches of upwelling about a depth across, arriving every two or three depths of travel (bursting scales on the outer variables, the depth and the surface speed: Nakagawa and Nezu 1978, and Jackson 1976 for the link from a burst at the bed to a boil at the surface, both by a search's summary; the two-to-three figure is from memory).

**Just submerged (d/k between 1 and about 4, Fr below 1).** Three things, in order of rising Fr_c:

- **A dip over the crest.** By continuity and Bernoulli over a bump of height k, the surface falls by k Fr² / (1 - Fr²) to first order (Lamb §246; the form S101's relief already uses with a bump of a quarter of the depth). In one dimension the bump **chokes** the flow when it is higher than k_c = d (1 + Fr²/2 - 1.5 Fr^(2/3)): the specific energy upstream less the least the discharge can pass with (the statement Δh_max = E₁ - E_c with E_c = 1.5 d_c is in every open-channel text; the normalised form is derived here from it). At Fr 0.5 that is 0.18 d and at 0.7 only 0.06 d, which is why a near-critical riffle is disturbed by every cobble. A single stone does not span the channel, so water goes round it and the one-dimensional bound overstates; it is the bound for a **step**.
- **A train of standing waves downstream**, stationary because their phase speed against the current equals the current: tanh(κ_w d) / (κ_w d) = Fr², which in water deeper than about half a wave is λ = 2π U² / g (0.16 m at 0.5 m/s, 0.64 m at 1, 1.44 m at 1.5, 2.6 m at 2), longer as Fr nears 1 and **without a solution above it**: a supercritical flow has no standing train, only oblique waves (below). Kennedy (1963, *J. Fluid Mech.* 16, 521) is the classic use of it, for the waves over antidunes (the abstract by search; the paper's copy refused the fetch; the dispersion relation is Lamb's). Behind a single stone the train is the transverse part of a **ship's wake seen from the ship**: crests bowed across the flow inside a wedge, falling off as the inverse square root of the distance.
- **A hole.** When the water over the crest is itself critical the sheet shoots down the lee and meets the slower water behind in a **hydraulic jump**: a trough behind the stone, a white roller facing upstream, and the train after it. A weir passes freely (critical on its crest) until the tailwater over the crest is more than about two thirds of the head; with the head (d - k) + U²/2g that is U² > g (d - k), **Fr_c > 1**. That criterion is this page's, built from the broad-crested weir's modular limit, and is the first thing the flume scene checks. The jump is Bélanger's, d₂/d₁ = (√(1 + 8 Fr₁²) - 1)/2; by its inflow Froude number it is undular below 1.7 (no roller, under 5% of the energy lost), weak to 2.5, oscillating to 4.5, steady to 9; its length runs from about four downstream depths for a weak jump to six for a steady one (Peterka 1958 and Chow 1959, as tabulated on Wikipedia's hydraulic-jump page, read). The paddler's vocabulary names the same things: a **hole** where water pours over a submerged object and the surface flows back upstream, a **wave train** after it, **haystacks** where the waves break (Wikipedia's whitewater page, read).

**Emergent in subcritical flow (d/k below 1, Fr below 1).**

- **The pillow.** The water that meets the upstream face stops, and its speed's head becomes height: at the stagnation point the surface stands **U_s² / 2g** above the approach level (Bernoulli): 5 cm at 1 m/s, 20 cm at 2, 46 cm at 3. In potential flow round a cylinder of radius R the speed on the stagnation line is U (1 - R²/r²), so the rise is (U_s²/2g)(1 - (1 - R²/r²)²): the whole of it at the face, 0.44 of it one radius out, 0.21 at two. On the face the speed is 2 U sin θ, so the surface is above the approach level only within 30° of the stagnation point: **the pillow is about half the boulder's width wide** and stands off it by about a radius.
- **The shoulders.** The same potential flow doubles the speed at 90° and would drop the surface by 3 U_s²/2g; the flow separates before that (a real cylinder's surface pressure bottoms near -1 to -1.5 of the head). The water is drawn down and fastest beside the stone, and if 1.5 U there outruns the long-wave speed of the lowered water (about Fr above 0.6) a short oblique jump stands off each shoulder: the two bright lines of the V.
- **The horseshoe.** The approach flow's shear rolls up at the foot of the face and wraps round the stone (weak, in Tsakiris's measurements). It scours the bed and is not seen at the surface; nothing is drawn for it.
- **The wake.** Behind the stone a zone of slow and returning water about **two widths** long (Shamloo et al.'s 2D; Tsakiris's 1.4), bounded by two shear layers, the **eddy lines**. In shallow water the bed's friction decides its kind: with the shallow-wake parameter S_w = c_f D / d (c_f the bed's quadratic friction coefficient), a **vortex street** below about 0.2, an **unsteady bubble** to 0.5, a **steady bubble** above (Chen and Jirka 1995, *Fluid Dynamics Research* 16, 11: abstract by search; for depth Reynolds numbers above 1,500). With c_f = 2 g n² / d^(1/3) from Manning's n, a metre boulder in 0.3 m of water at n 0.04 has S_w = 0.16 and a two-metre one 0.31: **a stream's large boulders hold attached eddies and its smaller stones shed streets**. The paddler's **eddy** and **eddy line**, "a swirling seam between the eddy and the main current, sometimes with whitewater" (the whitewater page).
- **Foam gathers in the eddy** because the eddy's surface converges and its water stays: its residence is the bubble's length over the return speed (a quarter of U or so), seconds, against the fraction of a second the main flow takes to pass. No number was found for a stream's foam; the shots give 2.6 s or more of footage.

**Emergent in supercritical flow (Fr above 1).** The stone cannot be felt upstream, so the adjustment is abrupt: a **detached hydraulic jump** stands in front of it, a bow wave, its toe a hyperbola in plan whose arms run out along the Froude angle either side; with shallow fast water against a wide face the water instead climbs the face as a thin sheet, a **wall-jet-like bow wave** (Mignot and Riviere 2010, *Physics of Fluids* 22, 117105; Riviere et al. 2017, *J. Hydraulic Engineering*: abstracts by search; both full texts refused the fetch, so **the standoff distance and the criterion between the two forms are not taken from them** and are left for the build row to read). What the abstracts do say: which form appears depends on both the Froude number and the depth over the obstacle's width; the wall-jet form is the commoner one in the field, round bridge piers in supercritical rivers; and it pulses, by "reverse spillage on the obstacle face", at a Strouhal number of its own. Behind the stone there is no recirculation to speak of: two oblique waves from the shoulders close behind it into a **rooster tail**.

### A step and a constriction

- **A step** is on the running-water page already: critical at the crest, drawn down to 0.715 d_c at a free brink (Rouse 1936), a nappe that lands 4.30 h (d_c/h)^0.81 out as a jet 0.54 h (d_c/h)^1.275 deep (Rand 1955, by Chanson), a jump with Bélanger's depths, air at the toe by the inflow Froude number, and the bubbles rising out at 0.25 m/s, an e-fold every U d / 0.25 of travel. The reels agree in kind: clear within one or two boulder widths of the white. What that page does not have is **the row of stones the step is made of**: in `boulder-cascade` the water does not come over the step as one sheet but through four gaps. Each gap is a short constriction (below) and each keystone an emergent or just-submerged obstacle (above); the step's white water is the sum of theirs.
- **A constriction and the tongue.** Narrowing a subcritical flow speeds it and lowers it; if the narrowing is enough the flow passes through critical in the throat and leaves supercritical. The surface of an accelerating flow is smooth (the turbulence is strained along the flow), which is the **tongue**: the dark glassy V pointing downstream into a rapid. Where the supercritical flow is turned by a wall or a boulder's shoulder it throws an **oblique standing wave**. A small disturbance stands at the angle whose sine is 1/Fr, exactly as a Mach line (38° at Fr 1.63, 60° at 1.15, 90° at 1); a finite turn θ makes an oblique jump at a steeper angle β with tan θ = tan β (√(1 + 8 Fr² sin² β) - 3) / (2 tan² β + √(1 + 8 Fr² sin² β) - 1) and the depths Bélanger's with Fr sin β for Fr (Ippen 1951, *Trans. ASCE* 116, 268, as Chow gives it in his chapter on supercritical flow: **from memory**; the paper is behind ASCE's paywall; a search's secondary gives its largest turn as 0 to 33° for Fr 1 to 3, and Ippen and Harleman 1956 as confirming the positions to 2%). The waves from the two sides cross on the centre line a distance (w/2) / tan β below the throat and reflect: the diamonds of a wave train, which is where the white is. `gorge-rapids` shows the train starting at the throat and running 1.3 widths.
- **A bend** lifts the outer bank's water by U² w / (g r) (the centripetal balance; textbook) and puts the fast thread against the outer bank, which the cut's asymmetric section already does to the depth.

### What surface tension does at the eye's scale

- **No wave on water travels slower than 0.23 m/s**, the minimum of the gravity-capillary phase speed, at a wavelength of 1.7 cm (Lamb §267; the numbers as on Wikipedia's capillary-wave page, read). So **in water slower than 0.23 m/s nothing stands at all**: a stone in a pool is passed in silence and the surface is glass up to its meniscus. This is a rule, not a look.
- Above it there are two stationary waves for every speed, λ = π (U² ± √(U⁴ - 4 g σ/ρ)) / g: a **capillary** one, short, and a **gravity** one, long. At 0.25 m/s they are 9.7 mm and 30 mm; at 0.3, 5.7 and 52 mm; at 0.5, 1.9 and 158 mm; at 1 m/s 0.5 mm and 0.64 m. A capillary wave's group velocity is greater than its phase velocity and a gravity wave's less (the same page), so the capillary ripples stand **upstream** of a twig or a stone and the gravity waves **downstream** (Rayleigh's fishing-line pattern; Lamb §§271-272: from memory). Viscosity kills a 5 mm ripple in about a third of a second, a few centimetres of travel, so the upstream fan is a hand's width and is seen only between about 0.23 and 0.5 m/s and only from within a few metres.
- **The Kelvin wake**: a small obstacle in deep slow water trails a wedge of half-angle arcsin(1/3) = 19.47° whatever the speed (Thomson 1887; Wikipedia's wake page, read, which notes the result is for deep water and neglects surface tension). In shallow water the angle depends on the depth's Froude number and is greatest at 1 (Havelock 1908, by a search's summary); the standard account, **from memory**, is that it holds near 19.5° to a depth Froude number of about 0.6, opens to 90° at 1 and closes as arcsin(1/Fr) above. A riffle's cobbles sit near 1: their Vs are wide, as in `clear-riffle-cobbles`.
- **Foam lines and films.** Clean water's bubbles burst at once; a stream's carry surfactant enough to last seconds, and a film damps capillary ripples, which is the glassy slick inside an eddy. No constant was found, and none is pinned.

### The texture between obstacles

- **Boils**: about a depth across, every two to three depths of travel, stronger over a rougher bed (above).
- **A riffle is rough because it is shallow over coarse grain.** The measure is the relative roughness D84 / d: on a pool-riffle reach the solver caps it at 0.3, on a plane bed and a step-pool at 0.8, steeper at 1 ([channels](../subsystems/channels.md#bed-sediment), from Montgomery and Buffington 1997). With the response k Fr²/(1 - Fr²) and Fr near 0.7 a riffle's surface carries a relief of the order of its largest stones' height; a pool, at Fr 0.1 over the same stones, a hundredth of it. Through a pool and a riffle the surface is lowest over the pool and rises into the riffle's head (Thompson and McCarrick 2010, by a search's summary). **Tonina and Buffington's** flume work on pool-riffle beds (2007, 2011, *Water Resources Research*) was looked for as the brief asks: what a search returns of it is hyporheic exchange driven by the bed's pressure, not the surface's forms over steps and pools, and it was not opened.
- **Streaks along the flow**: foam and fine texture are drawn out along the stream because the surface speed rises away from every stone and bank; the strands of S101 are that.

### What leaves the bed

Short, since the waterfall is a later row and the three fall shots are its measure. A sheet leaving a lip at speed v₀ falls as x = v₀ t, y = g t²/2, thinning as q over its speed (the texture stretches downward, as `falls-wide` shows). It stays a sheet for a **break-up length** of about 6 q^0.32 metres, q in m²/s (Horeni 1956, as quoted by Castillo's group for rectangular nappes, by a search's summary; laboratory discharges of 0.25 m²/s and more): 2.9 m at 0.1 m²/s, 4.8 m at 0.5, 6 m at 1. So a step of 0.7 m never breaks up, and a ten-metre fall of a forest stream is strands and drops for its lower half or more, which is the sequence in `falls-wide`. The mist at the foot is the drops' finest fraction carried by the air the fall drags down; no model was read.

## The design

### What it rests on

**A channel's flow is steady**: its stage is fixed (S99: "a channel's level is static"), its section and its clasts are the plan's, so every obstacle meets the same water every frame. Every form in the grounding is then a **standing** form: a function of the place, the obstacle and the flow, not of time. What moves is only what the forms carry, the fine texture and the foam, and that is carried by a velocity field which is itself standing. The few things that pulse (a vortex street, a hole's surge) do so at a frequency the flow gives (a street at 0.2 U / D; a surge with the roller's slosh, √(g / L) over 2π), so they are functions of the world's clock. **Nothing has to be stored from frame to frame**, and nothing is. What would need state, and is left out: a floating leaf's path, a flood wave, a walker's splash.

So the work divides in three: the **cook** (once, CPU, deterministic) classifies obstacles, derives each one's constants and moves the ribbon's vertices; the **GPU scene** holds the records in a grid; the **pixel** evaluates closed forms.

**The same bytes from every toolchain.** The ribbon's GLB is pinned, and the cook now needs an exponential (e^(-1/Fr_c²), the wake's decay), the root of tanh(x)/x = Fr² and an arcsine. None may come from the C library: `core/math/deterministic.h` has them or gains them, or the cook uses rational fits stated on the water page. The classification itself is integer millimetres against the plan's own numbers.

**Across the board.** The owner asked for the idea in every water. The record and the emergent forms serve a rock standing in a body's swash as they stand, with the wave's orbital speed for U and a clock in the phase; whether a body reads them is S102's shore's to take or leave, and nothing here depends on it.

### Which clasts are obstacles

A clast is an obstacle when **its height over the bed is at least a quarter of the undisturbed depth at its place** (d/k of 4, the published bound for the surface feeling it) **and it is at least 0.2 m on its b axis**. A clast stands 0.36 of its b axis over the ground on average ([channels](../subsystems/channels.md#the-clasts)), so on the forest's plane-bed reach (0.33 m deep) that is every clast of 0.23 m and more, and one stands out of the water from about 0.9 m. Everything smaller is the **statistical** relief S101 draws, whose bump becomes the reach's own D84 as that row already arranged (`water_channel_bed_bump`): the two terms share the bed between them at 0.2 m, with no stone counted twice. The split moves with distance: an obstacle whose width is under two of the pixel's footprints on the water fades out of the normal and into the roughness by the fourth pass's band limit, and its air stays, so far white water is a streak and not a sparkle.

**How many.** On a lognormal bed the share of the surface under clasts of at least 0.7 d is the distribution's tail. For the test valley's trunk (D50 49 mm, D84 112 mm, 0.35 m deep) that is 2.7% of the bed, 0.25 an obstacle a square metre against the 27 clasts a square metre drawn: **about one clast in a hundred**. On a plane bed or a step-pool, where the solver lets D90 reach 0.8 of the depth, it is nearer one a square metre, and every step's keystone and every cascade's cluster stone (zones 4 and 5, D84 to D97.7 of the reach, 0.2 to 2 m) is one. For the forest's 2.5 million clasts the estimate is **30,000 to 100,000 obstacles, a few thousand of them emergent**; the build row's first number is the count.

### How the description carries them, and how a pixel finds them

**The contract.** The water capability may not read the channels capability (ADR-0027), and the description is their contract: `engine.scene.WaterChannel` gains, in its next version, the reach's `d50_m` and `d84_m` a point (S101's request) and an **`obstacles`** list written by the channels solver from the same plan it lays the clasts from: for each, its centre, its b axis and its width across the flow, its top's height over the bed, its zone and the clast's own seed, as integers (millimetres), about 40 bytes of JSON each. This is the request S101's page already makes ("the clusters' stations in the description"), widened from the cluster stones to every clast that passes the rule for the deepest water the reach carries; the water side applies the rule again at the local depth. The alternative, a derived file named by the plan's hash beside the cuts, keeps the scene file small and is the channels row's to choose.

**The record** (48 bytes, built at the cook, mirrored once in `shaders/scene.slang` and pinned in the size table): the centre and the flow's heading there, the half width R, the crest's height k, the approach's d and U, and the **derived constants** the forms need, so the pixel computes none of them: the regime, the stagnation head, the standing wavenumber and amplitude, the hole's depth and length, the wake's length and kind, the toe's air, a seed.

**The grid.** A hashed grid over the channels' own frame (the first channel's instance's, as the banks' tree uses), cells of 2 m. Each obstacle is entered in every cell its influence reaches (two widths upstream, three either side, ten downstream), and a cell keeps the **eight** strongest at its centre. For 100,000 obstacles: 4.8 MB of records and about 2 MB of cells, resident with the scene (the banks' tree is 11.6 MB), and what scales with the world is the area of bed under water. A channel pixel hashes its body-local position, reads its cell's header, and reads up to eight records, each rejected by its box before anything is evaluated; a pixel with no obstacle near pays the header's read and nothing more. Not a tree: the banks' walk costs 3.76 ms at 4K at the stream and is the cost S99 still owes. Not a list a section: the ribbon goes through the cluster builder, so a pixel's triangle no longer names its section. Not a texture of the baked forms: a pillow is a third of a boulder wide, and a texel fine enough for it over 30 km of channel is hundreds of megabytes.

### What each obstacle adds

All in the obstacle's own frame: x downstream from its centre, y across, r the distance, R its half width. η is the height added to the ribbon, **u** the velocity the detail and the strands are carried by, C the air. "A choice" marks a number this page sets; the flume holds every one.

**A row of stones is a step.** Keystones in a row (zone 4) are evaluated like any others, and the gaps between them are constrictions: the march's jump at a riser's foot, which runs straight across today, is multiplied across the width by the gaps' share, so the white water comes through the gaps and the keystones' own forms stand between. S101's lanes, which draw clasts "of their own from the channel's seed" because the description carried none, are replaced by the real ones. This is what the fourth pass's brief asks of the relief's places: "from something that has no grid: the bed's own clast field".

**A constriction** is read from the section the ribbon already has: where the width narrows downstream and the marched flow passes critical, the detail's amplitude falls with the acceleration (the tongue's glass; the factor U_before / U_after, a choice), and from each side an oblique line at arcsin(1/Fr) carries a jump's step and a toe's air to the crossing (w/2) / tan β below, with the train after it.

**The wet line on the rock.** A clast above the water is a ground pixel. It is wet to the water's height at its face, which is the pillow's on the upstream side and the wake's low surface behind, plus a splash band of a quarter of the stagnation head (a choice, as S99's spray bands are). S99's bank record gains the stretch's speed, and the rock's pixel reads its own obstacle's record for the rise; no new structure.

**Where a form meets its neighbours.** Heights and airs add; velocities compose as perturbations of the section's own (each obstacle's u less U, summed, plus U). An obstacle inside another's wake takes that wake's speed for its U at the cook, so a stone in an eddy makes no pillow.

### What moves in the mesh

A pillow, a hole and a standing train have height, and from a low camera height is silhouette: S101's seventh objection ("the relief is in the normal only, so no wave has a silhouette, and a jump has no step") and the asks audit's row 2.2.6. Because the forms stand, the height is **baked into the ribbon at the cook**: `build_channel_mesh` adds η to each vertex. To have vertices where the forms are, the grid is **warped, not subdivided**: a row keeps its count of columns and slides them across toward the obstacles in its reach (at least six across a form's width), and rows are inserted along the line through an obstacle's box, so the strip's topology, the phase in UV.x and the air in UV.y are as they are and no stitch is needed. Only forms taller than **3 cm** on an obstacle at least **0.4 m** wide move vertices (a silhouette smaller than that is under a pixel beyond a few metres); the rest shade. The pixel's normal is the analytic gradient of the same η, so shading and silhouette agree; the cluster builder's LOD takes the far ribbon back to flat, where the band limit has already folded the forms into roughness. The ribbon's budget of 65,536 vertices a channel stands; the estimate is a third to a half more triangles on a boulder reach.

The rock's mesh and the water's now cross where the pillow climbs the face, which is right: that crossing is the waterline on the rock, and the traced waterline of S101 softens it over its 5 cm.

### The bank

- **The slow margin** is already the lateral profile's (the speed follows the local depth); the margin's texture should follow it, which it does once the detail is carried by **u** and not the triangle's speed.
- **The eddy behind a point.** A keystone half in the bank, a cluster stone against it, a point bar's head, the inside of a sharp bend: each is an emergent obstacle with one flank, evaluated as half of the emergent form. The channels solver's description names the first two; the bar's head is the place the section's asymmetry changes sign.
- **Where the surface meets the bank.** On gravel the margin is a field of obstacles by the rule above, since the depth runs to nothing: each cobble at the line gets its small wake and, between 0.23 and 0.5 m/s and within a few metres of the eye, its fan of capillary ripples upstream (one sine at the capillary root's wavelength, fading over a few centimetres; the last stage, and a near-camera detail only). On turf the reels show a clean dark line under overhanging grass and no beach: that is the ground's and the sward's shape (the channels solver's open item on turf banks), and the water's part is S101's margin.

### What the build row needs from the fourth pass

The fourth pass of S101 holds every channel file while this is written. The build row starts from its result and needs these of it:

1. **Merged**, so `water.slang`'s channel sections, `water_channel_pixel` and `systems/water/src/channel.*` are free to edit.
2. **Its band limit**: the function that fades a term whose wavelength is under two footprints and returns the faded slope's variance to the roughness. Every obstacle form goes through it with its own length (R for a pillow, λ for a train).
3. **Its standing waves' rule** (the wavelength from the section's speed, the places "from something that has no grid"): the obstacle train replaces the places; what the build row needs is the rule as a function it can call with an origin and a speed, or a statement that the jittered cells stay for reaches with no obstacle.
4. **Its bed-ray rule** (trace only where the bed can be seen): white water from obstacles covers more bed, so more pixels skip the ray; the rule must read the pixel's final air.
5. **The quantity it carried along the ribbon at a riser's foot**, since stage 4 multiplies that same quantity across the width.
6. **The probe**, with room for more states.
7. **Its cost on the forest's four poses**, which is the baseline the +0.5 ms is counted from, and its flight driver with markers on both sides.
8. **The strands' direction settled** (S101b could not say whether they move downstream on the forest): stage 1 carries them by a new velocity and would otherwise inherit an open sign.

And of the channels solver (the other coordinator's): `obstacles`, `d50_m` and `d84_m` in the description; a tread's own depth below a step, which S101's page already asks for and which decides whether a hole behind a keystone is held or swept out.

## What to ask of the next reels

The shots settled the kind of thing and almost no quantity. What would settle the quantities, most useful first:

1. **A scale and a clock in every shot.** Something of known length at the water (a metre staff, a paddle, a person standing in it) and the footage at real time with its frame rate known, or a leaf or stick thrown in and followed. Without both, no speed, wavelength or lifetime can be pinned, and every number above is a ratio.
2. **A locked camera.** A tripod or a rock; four of these shots pan, and the pan is of the size of the water's motion.
3. **One emergent boulder from straight above** (a drone or a bridge), ten seconds: the pillow's width and standoff, the shoulder lines' angle, the eddy's length and whether it sways, where the foam sits and for how long. The same boulder at a second stage (after rain) would give the dependence on depth that the owner's sentence is about.
4. **The upstream face of a boulder from the bank at water level**: the pillow's height against the stone, the one form no shot shows.
5. **A just-submerged stone from the side at water level**: the dip, the hole's depth, how many crests follow and how fast they fall off. `rapids-low-camera` was meant for this and its train cannot be counted.
6. **A step from the side**: the nappe's path, where it lands, how far the white runs, with a scale. And the same step from above, for the gaps.
7. **A constriction from above**: the tongue's V and the angle of the lateral waves, with the surface speed from a float.
8. **A twig or a reed in a slow clear glide, close** (under a metre): the capillary fan upstream and the gravity Vs downstream, and a still pool beside it for the speed below which nothing stands.
9. **An eddy for twenty seconds**: how long a foam patch lives and how it circulates.
10. **A turf bank and a gravel bank at the waterline, close**, for the margin.
