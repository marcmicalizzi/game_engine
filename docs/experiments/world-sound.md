# The sound of wind and water outdoors (S110)

Roadmap S110 (2026-11-20). The owner: sound is the next major milestone beside performance and realism, "wind through the trees from the wind's strength and the trees nearby, bird calls from a game's wildlife, water (streams, falls, surf)". This page holds the published models a synthesis of those sounds rests on, the numbers that matter at the engine's scales, and for each what a real-time synthesis needs from the world and what it costs. [audio](../subsystems/audio.md#sound-for-the-world-the-design-s110) holds the design that follows and [ADR-0079](../adr/0079-world-sound-is-synthesised-from-world-state.md) its decisions. **Nothing was built or run for this page; it is a reading.**

## How each source was read

The row read the web through the fetch and search tools only, and several publishers refused the fetch (MDPI, Wiley, Nature, PubMed Central's pages, xeno-canto) or served a PDF the tool could not turn into text. So every source below carries one of four marks, and **a number is only as good as its mark**:

| Mark | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **[F]** | the text was opened and read through the fetch tool |
| **[A]** | only the abstract or the repository's record was read |
| **[S]** | only a search engine's excerpt of the text was read: the sentence is the source's, the context was not seen |
| **[N]** | not read; named because a later row must read it before a number from it is pinned |

Arithmetic done here on a source's numbers is marked "computed here". A row that pins a constant in code reads the source marked [S] or [N] first, as [the experiments index](README.md) asks of every grounded number.

## Wind in vegetation

**The models.** Fégeant's is the first semi-empirical prediction model of vegetation noise (Fégeant 1999, *Acta Acustica united with Acustica* 85, 228–240, with field measurements in part II, 241–249: **[N]**, known here through Bolin's licentiate thesis **[S]**). Bolin's "Prediction method for wind-induced vegetation noise" (2009, *Acta Acustica united with Acustica* 95(4), 607–619 **[A]**) couples a wind field model to a new source model for **coniferous, deciduous and leafless trees**, agrees better than the earlier model with measurements "in particular in the region below 1 kHz", holds at five measurement sites for wind speeds **up to 8.5 m/s**, and estimates the fluctuation of the noise level with the wind's turbulence. Heutschi, Pieren and others built a synthesiser on it for auralization (2014, *Acta Acustica united with Acustica* 100(1), 13–24 **[A]**): "an emission model that predicts a wind speed dependent volume-specific sound power spectrum for different tree species", "steered by a time varying wind speed function that is generated by a random process with a predefined power density spectrum". Their turbine paper in the same issue (Pieren and others, 25–33 **[A]**) is the method in small: tones plus broadband noise shaped in one-third-octave bands, each band's level a random process, and a listening test in which the synthesis "could easily be mistaken" for the recording.

**What the level follows.**

| Quantity | Law | Mark |
|---|---|---|
| Sound power per cubic metre of crown | a radiation constant of the species × the **leaf area density** × the wind speed at the crown raised to a species' coefficient; Bolin makes the leaf area term linear | [S] |
| Level against wind speed | about **30 log₁₀ U** (Fégeant): 9 dB a doubling of the wind (computed here) | [S] |
| Level inside a coniferous forest | A-weighted level linear in the wind speed, L_A = A·U + B, coefficients not seen | [S] |
| A leafless tree's spectrum | adjusted to an average of 45 dB at 5 m/s (the receiver's distance not seen) | [S] |
| Trees near a house | 51 to 55 dB(A) at 40 ft, wind speed not given; a trade article, low reliability | [S] |
| Where the energy is | strongest below about 1 kHz for leafless trees and below about 4 kHz for trees in leaf | [S] |
| Broadleaf against conifer | deciduous trees slightly quieter and more broadband; conifers put more energy at particular frequencies | [S] |
| Wind inside a forest | 0 to 4.3 m/s between the ground and 20 m where the open field beside it had 0 to 6 m/s (a Chalmers thesis) | [S] |

**Why a conifer hisses: the Aeolian tone.** A cylinder in a flow sheds vortices alternately and radiates a tone at **f = St·U / d** with the Strouhal number St about 0.2 (Wikipedia, "Aeolian sound", citing Raichel 2000, p. 363 **[F]**; Strouhal 1878 **[N]**; his own wires gave 0.185, and St rises to 0.45 only at Reynolds numbers of 2.5×10⁵ to 2×10⁶ **[S]**, far above a needle's few hundred, computed here as U d / ν for a millimetre at 5 m/s). The source is a dipole, a lift dipole across the flow and a drag dipole along it (Wikibooks, *Aeroacoustics*, "Acoustic Sources", citing Phillips 1956 **[F]**), and a dipole's power rises with **the sixth power of the speed and the square of the length** (the same page **[F]**; Curle 1955 **[N]**): 18 dB a doubling of the wind (computed here). Computed here from the relation, with diameters that are a species' own and that **no source was read for**:

| Cylinder | d | 3 m/s | 5 m/s | 10 m/s |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| a fine needle | 0.5 mm | 1.2 kHz | 2 kHz | 4 kHz |
| a needle | 1 mm | 600 Hz | 1 kHz | 2 kHz |
| a twig | 5 mm | 120 Hz | 200 Hz | 400 Hz |
| a branch | 30 mm | 20 Hz | 33 Hz | 67 Hz |

So a conifer's sound is a band of tones whose centre **rises in pitch with the gust** (an octave a doubling), where a broadleaf's is the broadband rustle of leaves struck together, and a bare crown is its twigs: low, under 1 kHz, as the table above says. Selfridge, Moffat, Avital and Reiss built a real-time Aeolian tone from these relations (*J. Audio Eng. Soc.* 66(7/8), 594–607, 2018 **[S]**: a compact source model, the fundamental from the lift dipole, the drag dipole and harmonics from a table of multiples); its text was not reached.

**Grass.** Boersma's measurements in open grassland (1997, *J. Acoust. Soc. Am.* 101 **[N]**) were not reached, and no number for a sward is on this page. The design gives blades the broadleaf's law at the wind of their height, which is low, and says it is unsourced.

**What a synthesis needs and costs.** From the world: the wind's speed at each crown at this instant (`gfx::wind_at` and `wind_gust`, the functions the sway already calls, so the gust that bends a crown is the gust that is heard), each nearby tree's **leaf area** and **class** (blade, needle, bare; the needle's width), and where it stands. It needs no simulation: the level is a closed form of the wind and the area, the spectrum a class's fixed shape whose needle band slides with U. It costs a shaped-noise voice a source and one evaluation of the gust a source a tick; the source's model is good to 8.5 m/s and extrapolated above.

## Wind at the listener

Most of what a person in a wind hears as wind is made at their own head: the flow over the head and the outer ear is turbulent and the pressure fluctuations at the ear canal are heard as a low roar. The numbers come from hearing-aid and helmet research, where it is a nuisance.

| Finding | Source |
|---|---|
| The pressure rises with the square of the wind speed: **12 dB a doubling**; measured increases can be steeper | Korhonen 2021, *Seminars in Hearing* 42(3), 248–259 **[F]**, citing Strasberg 1988 and Morgan and Raspet 1992 **[N]** |
| At a cyclist's ears in a wind tunnel: **84.9 dB at 10 mph (4.5 m/s), 120.3 dB at 60 mph (26.8 m/s)**; 13.7 dB a doubling (computed here); loudest at the downwind ear with the head 90° from the wind | Seidman and others 2017, *Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery* 157(5), 848–852 **[A]** |
| At a behind-the-ear microphone: up to 116 dB SPL at 12 m/s; 6 m/s exceeds the long-term speech spectrum at most frequencies | Zakis 2011, *J. Acoust. Soc. Am.* 129(6), 3897–3907 **[A]**, and through Korhonen **[F]** |
| The spectrum is low: flat below about 300 Hz and falling steeply above (the review says 26 dB an octave; a Brigham Young abstract on screened microphones says 26.7 dB a decade below a corner at V / 3D **[S]**: the two disagree in their unit, so the slope is a tunable and not a pin); a higher speed spreads the energy upward | Korhonen 2021 **[F]** |
| Louder facing the wind than side-on for a hearing aid; the head and the pinna shelter the canal | Korhonen 2021 **[F]** |

Two things follow. **The ear's own wind outgrows the trees'**: it climbs 12 to 14 dB a doubling where vegetation climbs 9, and at 4.5 m/s it is already 85 dB against a crown's 45 to 55. And **it turns with the head**: the two ears differ by where the wind comes from, which is the strongest cue a listener has for the wind's direction. A synthesis needs the wind relative to the listener (the gust at the listener's place less their own velocity), the head's yaw against it, and whatever shelters the listener; it costs two voices and nothing of the scene. Played at its physical level it would bury everything else, so its gain is a choice of the mix and one of [the owner's questions](../subsystems/audio.md#the-owners-questions).

## Running water

**The bubble.** Water alone is nearly silent; what is heard is air. A bubble of radius a rings at Minnaert's frequency, f = (1 / 2πa) √(3γp / ρ), which for air in water at one atmosphere is **f·a ≈ 3.26 m/s** (Wikipedia, "Minnaert resonance" **[F]**; Minnaert 1933, "On musical air-bubbles and the sound of running water", *Phil. Mag.* 16, 235–248 **[N]**). Computed here: 0.3 mm rings at 10.9 kHz, 1 mm at 3.26 kHz, 3 mm at 1.09 kHz, 10 mm at 326 Hz. Phillips, Agarwal and Jordan filmed a dripping tap (2018, *Scientific Reports* 8, 9515 **[F]**): the "plink" is the entrained bubble at 7.9 to 8.7 kHz, within 4 to 9 % of Minnaert's formula in three tests of five; **the sound in air was about 40 dB under the sound in the water** (0.28 Pa against 24 Pa), which their model explains as the bubble driving the bottom of the cavity above it like a piston (44 dB) where plain transmission through the surface would lose 70; and a drop entrains a bubble regularly only between about 1 and 5 mm across (their citation of Pumphrey and of Oğuz and Prosperetti).

**How many, and how big.** In a breaking wave bubbles larger than **about 1 mm** come from turbulent fragmentation and their number falls as **radius to the −10/3**; smaller ones come from jets and drops striking the face and fall as radius to the **−3/2**; the break between is the Hinze scale (Deane and Stokes 2002, *Nature* 418, 839–844 **[A]**). So white water is mostly bubbles near a millimetre, ringing near 3 kHz, with a tail of large ones that carries the low end.

**The synthesis built on it.** Van den Doel's "Physically based models for liquid sounds" (2005, *ACM Trans. Applied Perception* 2(4), 534–546 **[S]**) makes a bubble's sound a damped sinusoid at Minnaert's frequency with its damping, rising in pitch as the bubble nears the surface, and makes streams, pouring, rain and breaking waves from **a statistical population of such bubbles** in real time; Moss and others' "Sounding liquids" (2010, *ACM Trans. Graphics* 29(3) **[S]**) and Zheng and James' "Harmonic fluids" (2009 **[S]**) drive the same resonance from a fluid simulation. The engine has no particle fluid and needs none: the population is drawn from the flow's state.

**The level.**

| Finding | Source |
|---|---|
| A stream's "sound pressure level is proportional to the energy of the flowing water", by a rating curve whose response is linear, from 0.02 to 33 m³/s | Zreda 2024, EGU General Assembly, EGU24-14133 **[A]** |
| Sound says discharge best at **steps with a low width to depth ratio**; at plunge pools the acoustic power rises with flow **to a threshold and then holds or falls**; the drop's height, the pool's width and the depth downstream all move the power and the mean frequency; riffles are the least reliable | Gauvain, Anderson, Yager and McNamara 2026, *Earth and Space Science* 13(5) **[A]** |
| A fall's height raises the level 5 to 10 dB over the range tested, its flow and its width 2 to 3 dB, the same water over several streams 2 to 3 dB, each with less return as it grows; falling on water gives 5 to 10 dB more between 250 Hz and 2 kHz than falling on stone, which adds about 5 dB at the top | Galbrun and Ali's laboratory work, through two theses **[S]**; their paper (2013, *J. Acoust. Soc. Am.* 133(1), 227–237 **[A]**) adds that only high-flow waterfalls reach the low-frequency levels of road traffic |
| Field falls measured from 0.2 to 20,000 ft³/s and 1 to 270 ft | "Waterfall noise" **[S]**, title only |

**What a synthesis needs and costs.** From the world, per stretch of a stream: the discharge, the drop, the wet width and the regime (a fall's lip and foot and its height, a Froude number over 1), which the flow solve already holds ([channels](../subsystems/channels.md#reaches), [water](../subsystems/water.md#the-plunge)); the level then follows the power the water gives up, ρ g Q h a stretch, capped as Gauvain's pools cap. The dense part is shaped noise centred where millimetre bubbles ring; the sparse part, the separate "plinks" of a trickle, a handful of damped sinusoids a second drawn from the size law. It costs a voice a stretch near the listener. **What no source read here gives is the constant**: the airborne efficiency of a stream, power in the air over power dissipated. It is a stated choice until a source is read, anchored on the surf's numbers below.

## Surf

Bolin and Åbom measured the airborne sound of breaking waves on Baltic shores of several slopes and sediments (2010, *J. Acoust. Soc. Am.* 127(5), 2771–2779 **[A]**): **60 dB at a significant wave height of 0.4 m, 78 dB at 2.0 m**, with a semi-empirical model that scales the level by **the wave power dissipated in breaking and the surf similarity parameter**, and a spectrum that changes with the kind of surf. Computed here: 18 dB over a factor of five in height is the height to the power 2.6, which is how a shallow-water wave's energy flux grows (H² times a group speed that goes as √H). A later reading of their figure puts a broad peak **near 1 kHz, moving to 250–400 Hz for waves over 1.0 m**, and notes the figure's quantity is unclear (Tollefsen and Byrne 2011, *Canadian Acoustics* 39(3), 210–211 **[S]**; the paper's own abstract **[A]** names impact, single bubbles, collective bubble oscillation and bursting bubbles as proposed mechanisms). Deane's "Sound generated by breaking surf" (1997, *J. Acoust. Soc. Am.* 102 **[N]**) was not reached. The distance the 60 and 78 dB were taken at was not in the abstract.

**What a synthesis needs and costs.** Both of Bolin and Åbom's scaling variables are already computed for the picture: the energy flux field's breaking term along every ray of the train (`gfx::water_flux_field`, [water](../subsystems/water.md#the-surf)), and `gfx::surf_similarity` with its `breaker_type` (spilling, plunging, surging). The rhythm is the picture's too: a wave breaks at a place when the arrival field says its crest is there. So a stretch of surf is a voice whose level is the dissipation under it, whose spectrum leans low as the height passes a metre and by the breaker's type, and whose envelope is the train's own phase. A lake's lap is the same law at 0.1 m.

## Propagation outdoors

At the engine's scales (a source 10 m to 2 km away, a listener 1.7 m up) four terms matter, in this order.

**Spreading.** 6 dB a doubling of distance from a point, 3 dB from a line (Wikibooks, *Engineering Acoustics*, "Outdoor Sound Propagation", citing Crocker 1998 and Kinsler 2000 **[F]**). A stream and a surf line are lines near and points far.

**Air absorption** takes the top end first, and is why a distant fall is a rumble. In dB/km at 20 °C and one atmosphere (the same Wikibooks page's table **[F]**, which that page itself flags as dubious; ISO 9613-1:1993, the standard the numbers should be pinned from, **[N]**):

| Relative humidity | 62.5 Hz | 125 | 250 | 500 | 1 kHz | 2 kHz | 4 kHz | 8 kHz |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 % | 0.370 | 0.775 | 1.58 | 4.25 | 14.1 | 45.3 | 109 | 175 |
| 70 % | 0.090 | 0.339 | 1.13 | 2.80 | 4.98 | 9.02 | 22.9 | 76.6 |
| 90 % | 0.071 | 0.272 | 0.966 | 2.71 | 5.30 | 9.06 | 20.2 | 62.6 |

Computed here at 70 %: over 200 m the 8 kHz band loses 15 dB and 1 kHz one; over 1 km, 77 and 5. Dry desert air (10 %) takes three times as much at 1 and 2 kHz. A band's loss is less than a pure tone's at its centre, as the page says.

**Forest.** Hoover's formula as Bies and Hansen give it: **A = 0.01 · r · f^(1/3) dB** for r metres through forest at f Hz (Arup's Strutt help, "Dense foliage excess attenuation", citing *Engineering Noise Control* p. 227 **[F]**). Computed here: 0.05 dB/m at 125 Hz, 0.1 at 1 kHz, 0.2 at 8 kHz, so 100 m of forest is 10 dB at 1 kHz. ISO 9613-2's table A.1 is the other method; the page shows it as an image and it is **[N]**, as are Aylor 1972 and Price, Attenborough and Heap 1988.

**Terrain in the way.** A ridge between a fall and the listener is a barrier: the loss grows with the detour the sound makes over its top measured in wavelengths, so high bands are cut and low ones bend round. **Maekawa's curve (1968), the usual formula, was not read on any page reached: [N]**; the page that names it gives no formula. The design leaves the term's constants unpinned until it is.

**Left out, and said so.** The ground effect (a soft ground's dip in the low mid bands for a path that grazes it: Embleton, Piercy and Daigle 1983, Attenborough 1988, all **[N]**), and refraction by wind and temperature gradients (upward refraction "reduces sound levels near the ground", the Wikibooks page **[F]**; the shadow upwind of a source is the audible case). Both change levels by more than a just-noticeable step in real air, and both need a model of the air's profile the engine does not have.

## What a real-time synthesis needs, in one table

| Source | From the world, per source | Law | Per frame |
|---|---|---|---|
| A crown | gust at the crown, leaf area, class, needle width | level 30 log U + 10 log area; class spectrum; needle band at 0.2 U / d | one `wind_gust`, one voice or a share of a bed |
| The ear's wind | gust at the listener less their velocity, head yaw, shelter | 12 to 14 dB a doubling; low-pass corner rising with U; the two ears differ by yaw | two voices |
| A stream's stretch | discharge, drop, wet width, regime | level 10 log (ρ g Q h), capped; millimetre bubbles; sparse pings when the flow is small | one voice |
| A fall | discharge, height, what it lands on | the stretch's law at its height; low end by flow | one voice |
| A stretch of surf | dissipation, surf similarity, the train's phase | 10 log of the dissipated power; peak 1 kHz falling to 250–400 Hz over 1 m | one voice |
| Everything far | the same, summed | powers add band by band | a share of a bed |
| The path | distance, humidity, forest crossed, ground between | spreading, air, Hoover, a barrier term | a few multiplies a band |

**Cost, as an estimate and not a measurement.** A clip voice costs 1.1 µs a 10 ms block today ([audio](../subsystems/audio.md#performance-notes)). A shaped-noise voice is a generator and eight band filters a frame; with the eight bands in one vector register that is of the order of 3 µs a block, so the design's 27 such voices would be about 80 µs a block, under 1 % of one core. **That figure is arithmetic; the row that builds the voice measures it with a bench row before any budget is called met.** Memory is filter state, a few hundred bytes a voice.

## What was not found

- Any number for the sound of grass in wind.
- The airborne acoustic efficiency of a stream or a fall, and any absolute level for a stream at a stated distance.
- The distance of Bolin and Åbom's levels.
- A leaf's or a needle's own dimensions from an acoustic source: the engine's flora recipes hold their own, grounded on [their page](tree-leaf-sizing.md).
- Fégeant's and Bolin's constants (the radiation constant by species, the wind coefficient). The 30 log U and the 45 dB at 5 m/s are excerpts.
- The licences of xeno-canto's recordings beyond Wikipedia's sentence that all are published under one of the Creative Commons licences ("Xeno-canto" **[F]**); its own terms page refused the fetch.
