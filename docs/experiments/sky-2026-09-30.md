# The sky (2026-09-30)

**Question.** Does a physical sky — Hillaire's tables, a moon and stars on the world's clock, an exposure that follows the light — read as a sky over the erg from a walker's eyes at noon, a late afternoon, a sunset looking at and away from the sun, civil twilight, a full-moon and a half-moon night and a moonless one; does the GPU draw what the CPU mirror computes; and does it cost less than 0.3 ms a frame at the owner's 11520×2160 with the cascaded maps?

**Answer.** Mostly yes. The late afternoon and the sunset looking away from the sun read as a desert's sky; the sunset and twilight have their colours once the exposure stopped metering them like a photograph; the nights have a moon with its phase and aureole and a starfield with a Milky Way. **Noon is slate rather than deep blue**, for a reason in the sand rather than the sky (below). The resolve's uncovered pixels are the mirror's within one byte (mean 0.03), and the reference's are the resolve's. The sky costs **0.29 ms** at 11520×2160 with the maps by day (0.28 at night) and **0.09–0.10 ms** at 1080p, measured beside other agents' builds, so upper bounds.

## Setup

- **The sky** ([renderer](../subsystems/renderer.md#the-sky), [gfx](../subsystems/gfx.md#the-sky), [sky](../subsystems/sky.md), [ADR-0048](../adr/0048-the-sky-is-a-providers-model-drawn-by-the-renderer.md)): the erg's `sky` block — 31.1° N, epoch on day 100, turbidity 1.6, a regional ground albedo of 0.38, the moon 12.39 days old at the epoch and so full three years in, where the erg's `time` stands (midnight).
- **The camera**: one-pose paths at eye height (1.65 m over the ground) at (−1380, 0) on the erg's western floor, 60° vertical field; `--time-of-day` for the hour. The half-moon and moonless shots are the same scene with `moon_age_days` 5.03 and 27.16.
- **Machine**: the owner's desktop, RTX 5090, `msvc-release`, `engine-view` offscreen, `--shadows csm` for the pictures.

## The exposure, and how it was chosen

**The rule is a photograph's above a knee and an eye's below it** ([renderer](../subsystems/renderer.md#exposure)). The first version metered the light on a level surface at the eye with the knee at 300 lux and three fifths of a stop compensated for each stop below it. It washed the sunset and the twilight out — a white-peach sky over white sand — and captures at −2 stops (the sunset) and −1 (the twilight) looked right, so the rule was wrong there, twice:

- **The meter.** A level surface weighs each part of the sky by its elevation's sine, and a dusk's light is all at the horizon: the sunset's level reading was 1.68 klux where the sun's direct light plus π times the sky's mean radiance over its dome is 2.48. The meter now takes the sky's mean — the first harmonic of the ambient's projection, free — and the sun's and the moon's direct light on a level surface. That alone moved the sunset half a stop darker and the twilight a third.
- **The knee.** At 300 lux a sunset (2.5 klux) is still compensated like noon. With the knee at a clear sun about 20° up (40 klux) and three quarters of a stop per stop below it, noon and the late afternoon are unchanged, the sunset is a stop under a photograph's exposure, the twilight 2.6, the full moon 4.5 and a moonless night 5.8 — the shape of the eye's partial compensation, fitted by eye to the trial captures (the sunset landed 0.4 stop brighter than the −2 trial, the twilight 0.75 darker than the −1). A trial at 0.7 a stop (`sky/k40s70/`, beside 0.75's `sky/k40s75/`) darkened the sunset by a fifth of a stop and the full moon's sand by 0.9, into a brown that no longer read as moonlit; 0.75 was kept. Both numbers are tunables (`renderer.sky.exposure_knee_lux`, `renderer.sky.exposure_slope`).

**The shoulder.** Before it, the aureole and a sunset's horizon — several times the metered white — were clipped into flat white bands. From 0.6 of the display up each channel rolls off towards 1 (`renderer.sky.shoulder`), so the sunset band stays yellow-orange and the aureole a gradient.

## Why noon is slate

The rule shows the erg's sand, albedo (0.84, 0.69, 0.47) — about twice a real desert's — at nearly white, and the clear sky's zenith is then a tenth of it: its single-scattered luminance at turbidity 1.6 and the sun 66° up is about 1,150 cd/m² against the CIE clear sky's 1,260, so the sky's own brightness is right and a photograph exposed for that sand would show the same sky. The saturation is Hillaire's three-wavelength RGB's, which renders a paler blue than a spectral sky. Neither is changed here: the sand's albedo is the terrain's, and a spectral sky is a larger change than this one. A stop darker (`exposure_compensation_ev: -1` in the scene, or `-` twice in a window) would show the sky deeper and the sand no longer white, if the owner prefers it; that was not captured here.

## GPU against CPU

`domain/gfx`'s `sky_tests.cpp` (four skies: noon, the sun 2° up, 5° under, and a moonlit night): every texel of every table within 2.3 × 10⁻⁴ of the double-precision mirror at its worst, the frame's sums within 2 × 10⁻³, and the functions the resolve calls within 0.002 of a display byte. The renderer's `sky_scene_tests.cpp`, end to end at 192×108: the afternoon sun (its disc among the pixels), its horizon, a moonlit night's moon (its lit disc, 130 pixels brightened by a star) and its horizon — every uncovered pixel within **1** of 255 of the mirror, mean 0.03, with a pixel and a half either side of a disc's limb left out (46 pixels at the discs, 14–16 at the horizon views), and the reference path tracer's primary misses within 1 of the resolve's bytes.

## What it costs

Along the erg's walk path (`walk-path.json`, 1,201 frames), the scene with its sky and without it (the same file with the block removed), medians of the GPU's timestamps in milliseconds; "sky" is the four passes' zone, and the resolve and the frame are the whole passes. Two hours: 16:00 (`--time-of-day 16`), and the scene's own midnight under the full moon, whose moon keys the cascaded maps and whose sky shows its stars:

| | sky passes | resolve: 16:00 / midnight / no sky | frame: 16:00 / midnight / no sky | the sky's cost, 16:00 / midnight |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1920×1080, `--shadows off` | 0.079 | 0.144 / 0.145 / 0.123 | 0.295 / 0.298 / 0.195 | **0.10 / 0.10** |
| 1920×1080, `csm` | 0.085 | 0.166 / 0.164 / 0.148 | 0.432 / 0.438 / 0.340 | **0.09 / 0.10** |
| 1920×1080, `rt` | 0.079 | 0.160 / 0.159 / 0.170 | 0.547 / 0.547 / 0.474 | **0.07 / 0.07** |
| 11520×2160 surround3, `off` | 0.088 | 1.695 / 1.653 / 1.473 | 2.562 / 2.526 / 2.257 | **0.31 / 0.27** |
| 11520×2160 surround3, `csm` | 0.085 | 1.835 / 1.812 / 1.619 | 2.854 / 2.847 / 2.568 | **0.29 / 0.28** |
| 11520×2160 surround3, `rt` | 0.086 | 1.987 / 1.968 / 2.475 | 2.729 / 2.713 / 3.131 | **−0.40 / −0.42** |

A second run of the no-sky rows with the maps and of the midnight ones, half an hour apart, gave the same medians to 0.01 ms. **The passes are 0.08–0.09 ms at any resolution**, since the tables are the same size whatever the frame. **The resolve's share grows with the pixels**: 0.02 ms at 1080p, 0.19–0.22 at 11520×2160. **With traced shadows the sky is cheaper than the stand-in**, because a sky's scene has no orbiting point lights: the stand-in traces two shadow rays a pixel for them, which cost more than everything the sky adds. Against the 0.3 ms the sky was budgeted at surround with the maps, **0.29 ms by day and 0.28 at night are under, with little room**; with shadows off the day is at 0.31.

**Where the resolve's share goes** (11520×2160 surround3, the maps; the resolve compiled from the same source with a part left out, loaded through a shader manifest, so the rest of the frame is the build's):

| resolve | 16:00 | midnight |
|---|---|---|
| as built | 1.836 (twice) | 1.812 (a second run 1.795) |
| without the air to the surface | 1.721 | 1.686 |
| without the stars | 1.777 | 1.751 |
| the stars skipped at run time while the sun is up | 1.811 | — |
| without either | 1.667 | 1.638 |
| no sky at all (the stand-in) | 1.619 | 1.619 |

So of the 0.19–0.22 ms the sky adds to the resolve at 11520×2160, **the air is 0.12 ms, the stars 0.06, and everything else** — the sky-view lookup and the discs for an uncovered pixel, the harmonics, the sun's and the moon's transmittance — **0.02–0.05**. **Half the stars' cost is their code, not their work**: skipping them by a branch while the sun is up (it moves five pixels of two million by one level, at the sunset looking away from the sun) saves 0.025 ms of the 0.059 that compiling them out saves; the rest is what their loop costs the whole resolve by being in it (its registers, presumably, and so the pass's occupancy), whether it runs or not. The branch is not in the build: at 0.29 ms the frame is under its budget, and the structural cut below takes all of it.

**What to cut first, if a frame needs the room**: (1) the discs and the stars out of the resolve, into a pass of their own over the uncovered pixels, which takes their 0.06 ms at 11520×2160 off every covered pixel (the branch alone, 0.025); (2) the air to the surface, 0.12 ms: its two slices are eight 16-byte reads by device address and a bilinear weighting written out, where a sampled 3D texture would be one filtered fetch; (3) the passes' fixed 0.09 ms, not split by pass here: the aerial volume's 65,536 cells of two sub-steps and the sky-view table's 20,736 of 32 steps are the candidates, and either could be built every other frame, since the sun moves a fraction of a pixel's angle in one.

**A trap met on the way**: engine-view's `--benchmark` flew its path at the ground's own time and ignored `--time-of-day` (`FlightOptions` had a sun rate and no start), so the first "16:00" runs were the scene's midnight, and a run-time skip of the stars by day first measured as saving nothing. `FlightOptions::sun_time_s` is the fix, and the summary's `sky.hour` is what shows which hour a run drew.

Machine state: the GPU lock was ours; before each run the GPU was 2–6% busy, and its sample after each run read up to 99% — the run's own work, caught as it ended. Other agents were compiling throughout, and the Linux container build of this change ran beside the 16:00 runs: other processes used 7–100% of the CPU (`--wait-quiet 20` waited and ran anyway, and every summary says `quiet: false`). GPU timestamps do not include CPU time, but a busy CPU can move a GPU's clocks, so treat the absolute numbers as upper bounds; the with-and-without pairs were taken the same way.

## Caveats

One GPU, one scene, one walk. The half-moon and moonless scenes are variants in the scratch directory, not committed. The captures were judged by eye on an sRGB monitor by a model reading PNGs, not by the owner; the look is his to accept. Civil twilight's 29 lux at the sun 5.2° down is about three times the measured value at that depression (ozone's absorption in three wavelengths and a flat multiple-scattering term both err bright there); a full moon's 0.16 lux at 57° is in the measured range.
